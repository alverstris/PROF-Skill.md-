1. Quantities, mathematics, measurement and evidence

Units and calculations

A physical answer normally contains a value and a unit. Use SI consistently before substituting: kg, m, s, A, K and mol are the base units explicitly required here. Recognise derived units, including N = kg m s⁻², J = kg m² s⁻², W = J s⁻¹, Pa = N m⁻², C = A s, V = J C⁻¹, Ω = V A⁻¹ and F = C V⁻¹. An equation must have matching dimensions on both sides; dimensional agreement is a necessary check, not proof that its physics or numerical factor is correct.

Know the specified prefixes: pico 10⁻¹², nano 10⁻⁹, micro 10⁻⁶, milli 10⁻³, centi 10⁻², deci 10⁻¹, kilo 10³, mega 10⁶, giga 10⁹ and tera 10¹². A conversion applies to the whole powered unit: 1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m² and 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³. Distinguish mass from weight, energy from power, charge from current, and temperature from energy.

Use a scientific calculator for powers, roots, exponentials, natural and base-10 logarithms, and trigonometry. Choose degree or radian mode deliberately. Rearrange nonlinear formulae and solve quadratics, retaining a physically admissible root. Use ratios to predict how an outcome changes: if speed doubles, kinetic energy quadruples; doubling orbital radius multiplies period by 2³ᐟ². Estimate scales, check dimensions and consider whether a result is physically reasonable. Keep intermediate precision, then give justified significant figures. A 'show that' calculation should expose the substitutions and the unrounded value needed to justify the stated approximation.

Vectors, graphs and rates

Scalars have magnitude; vectors also have direction. Displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum and field strength are vectors. Distance, speed, mass, energy, pressure, charge and potential are scalars. Vector addition uses a triangle, parallelogram or perpendicular components. If θ is measured from the positive x axis, Aₓ = A cos θ and Aᵧ = A sin θ, with signs supplied by direction. Add components, then find magnitude √(Aₓ² + Aᵧ²) and a direction in the correct quadrant. Negative one-dimensional velocity means motion opposite the chosen positive axis; it does not mean negative speed.

Read and construct graphs with labelled quantities and units, sensible linear scales, small clear points and an appropriate best-fit line or curve. Do not force a line through the origin without physical and data justification. Find gradients using a large triangle on the fitted line. A tangent estimates an instantaneous rate, whereas a secant gives an average rate. Graph areas are quantities with units equal to the product of the axis units, and may have a signed physical meaning. Estimate curved areas with strips or trapezia when necessary.

Linearise models before fitting. For y = ax + b, the slope is a and intercept b. For y = axⁿ, log y = log a + n log x: the log-log slope gives n. For y = y₀e⁻ᵏᵗ, ln(y/y₀) = −kt, or a graph of ln y against t has slope −k when numerical values are used in specified units. A base-10 log graph has a different slope factor, −k/ln 10. A constant fractional decrease over equal intervals is evidence for an exponential, not a constant absolute decrease. Use small-angle approximations sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ and cos θ ≈ 1 only for a small angle expressed in radians.

Uncertainty and experimental judgement

Accuracy concerns agreement with a true or accepted value; precision concerns closeness of repeated results. Random effects produce scatter; systematic effects bias results, including an instrument zero error. Repeating and averaging can reduce random influence but does not eliminate a systematic bias. Resolution is the smallest readable increment; it is not automatically the entire uncertainty, especially for hand timing. Repeatability concerns the same method and conditions; reproducibility concerns changed operators, apparatus or methods.

State a result as x ± δx with a defensible absolute uncertainty; percentage uncertainty is 100δx/|x|. Follow the uncertainty stated in a question or marked on the apparatus. The current handbook's default is half a smallest division per analogue reading and one resolution per digital reading. A distance formed from two scale readings has two reading uncertainties; reaction time can dominate a stopwatch's display resolution. OCR also accepts correctly applied half-resolution or full-resolution instrument approaches in assessment because published resources differ. This tolerance should not be mistaken for a general metrology rule. Current practical handbook, measurement guidance.

For the elementary A-level propagation convention:

- In a sum or difference, add the absolute uncertainties: δ(a ± b) = δa + δb.
- In a product or quotient, add fractional or percentage uncertainties.
- For xⁿ, multiply fractional uncertainty by |n|; roots are fractional powers.
- A known exact constant adds no measurement uncertainty. For a wire area πd²/4, the percentage uncertainty is twice the diameter's.

Draw error bars where appropriate. A plausible steepest or shallowest line through the allowed regions can estimate a slope limit. With one worst line, use the absolute difference between its gradient and the best-fit gradient as the estimated gradient uncertainty; with both limits available, explain the chosen convention. Carry that uncertainty into the final physical result. Compare differences with uncertainty intervals before claiming disagreement. Percentage error relative to an accepted value differs from uncertainty; when comparing two experimental values, state the reference or denominator used for a percentage difference.

An anomaly warrants investigation or repetition and a reasoned decision; it is not discarded solely because it spoils a desired line. Explain specific limitations and improvements: longer timing intervals reduce fractional timing uncertainty; a longer wire gives a larger measurable extension; lower current limits unwanted heating. 'Human error' or 'use better apparatus' without a mechanism is inadequate. Rigorous statistics, standard errors, least-squares theory and uncertainty covariance are not required by this qualification. Current mathematics handbook, M1.5.

Practical competence assumed at this level

Plan an investigation from a question: identify an independent variable, a measured dependent variable and controlled variables; choose suitable ranges, resolution and repeats; anticipate hazards; record raw data before processing; justify the graph or comparison; evaluate the evidence. Read analogue scales, calipers and micrometers; use a balance, stopwatch, light gates and multimeter; construct and check circuits; use a signal generator and oscilloscope; use sensors, data logging and software. On an oscilloscope, voltage = vertical divisions × volts/division and period = horizontal divisions per cycle × time/division. Distinguish peak amplitude from peak-to-peak excursion.

Know the operation behind these measurements: correct a micrometer/caliper zero offset before averaging diameters; avoid parallax on a scale; measure the length of a light-gate interrupt card to convert blocking time to mean speed; use a fiducial mark and the same phase/direction when counting cycles; measure the original unloaded wire/spring length and then the change. A multimeter's current and voltage settings require different circuit connections and sensible ranges; an ohmmeter measures an isolated unpowered component. Identify actual risks and controls in the proposed procedure, such as securing falling masses, preventing wire recoil toward eyes, limiting electrical heating, keeping laser beams out of eyes, and minimising source-handling time with distance and appropriate shielding in supervised ionising-radiation work.

The student should be able to explain the following investigations sufficiently to choose apparatus, obtain a derived quantity and evaluate the method:

Investigation: Operational method and analysis
Motion, collisions and free fall: Obtain distances/times or light-gate speeds; interpret motion graphs; for release from rest fit distance against t² to obtain g/2, or velocity against t to obtain g. Account for release/timing arrangements.
Terminal speed in a fluid: Measure travel over a region where successive speeds are constant; vary a justified factor and distinguish the acceleration region from steady motion.
Springs and wires: Measure extension from an initial length under varied loads; plot force against extension; find stiffness in the linear region and recognise permanent deformation.
Young modulus: Use F, original length L, cross-sectional area A and extension x; obtain Y = FL/(Ax) or a fitted equivalent; use repeated diameter measurements and control loading.
Electrical component characteristics: Measure current through and p.d. across a component; vary supply, include polarity where appropriate, and interpret an I–V curve.
Resistivity: Obtain resistance and length of a uniform wire; measure diameter for area; fit R against L; distinguish material resistivity from component resistance and control temperature.
Internal resistance: Vary load and measure terminal voltage and current; V = ℰ − Ir gives intercept ℰ and slope −r.
Sensor potential divider: Construct the specified two-component circuit; measure output as temperature or illumination changes; account for which component has the output across it.
Waves and light: Use stationary-wave spacings, resonance tubes, oscilloscopes, double slits or a grating to infer speed, frequency or wavelength; state the geometric assumptions.
Quantum and optical effects: Explain LED threshold-voltage estimation of h, refraction/critical-angle measurements, polarisation and a photoelectric demonstration.
Gases: Investigate pV at fixed temperature or p/T at fixed volume; a pressure–Celsius temperature extrapolation estimates absolute zero.
Capacitors and decay: Record charging/discharging voltage or current versus time; fit an exponential or a log graph; background-correct radioactive counts before estimating half-life.
Oscillation and electromagnetism: Time multiple cycles using a fiducial marker; observe resonance/damping; infer B from a wire's force measured by balance; investigate changing flux and transformer ratios.
Thermal properties: Compare electrical input VIt with mcΔT or mℓ, identifying energy losses and apparatus heat capacity; use appropriate heating and mass-loss measurements.

Experience with apparatus and demonstrated practical skills are related but distinct. The endorsement requires at least 12 assessed activities, independent evidence of the CPAC skills and experience of the specified apparatus and techniques; the activities are not 12 universally fixed recipes. The practical result is reported separately from the examination grade. The 2026 guidance also requires assessed competence and written evidence to remain authentically the student's own when AI tools are used. Practical handbook, sections 4 and 6, current specification, section 4e.

2. Forces, motion, energy and materials

Motion and forces

Average speed is total distance/time; average velocity is displacement/time. Instantaneous velocity is the displacement–time gradient; acceleration is the velocity–time gradient. Signed area under a velocity–time graph gives displacement; area under a speed–time graph gives distance. A curved graph requires a tangent or area estimate, not a constant-acceleration equation applied blindly.

For constant acceleration along one axis, use v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as and s = ½(u + v)t, with signed u, v, a and s. In near-surface free fall without drag, acceleration is approximately constant g downward. For a projectile without drag, resolve launch velocity into horizontal and vertical components; horizontal velocity stays constant and vertical motion has acceleration −g if upward is positive. Both components share the same elapsed time. Solve for landing time using the actual launch and landing heights; a symmetric-flight shortcut applies only to equal heights.

Draw a free-body diagram for the chosen object and include only forces acting on it. Weight is mg downward; a normal contact force is perpendicular to the contact surface; tension acts along a taut string; friction and drag oppose the relevant relative motion or tendency. Apply ΣF = ma separately along chosen axes. Equilibrium requires zero resultant force; a body can then be at rest or move with constant velocity. Newton's first law describes that condition, the second relates resultant force to momentum change, and the third gives equal and opposite forces on different interacting bodies. A third-law pair is not two balancing forces on one body.

Drag depends on speed and such factors as shape, area and fluid properties. In a falling body it usually increases as speed increases, reducing the downward resultant and acceleration. Terminal velocity occurs when the resultant force is zero: weight balances drag plus any upthrust. Terminal motion therefore has nonzero speed but zero acceleration. A speed–time graph approaches a plateau. A-level reasoning includes changing acceleration; solving a drag differential equation is not presupposed.

Thinking distance = speed × reaction time. Stopping distance is thinking distance plus braking distance. Under an approximately constant braking force, work–energy reasoning makes braking distance proportional to speed squared; the result depends on maintaining that force assumption. Reaction time, surface/tyre conditions and gradients can change different parts of the stopping process.

Equilibrium, pressure and buoyancy

A moment is force × perpendicular distance from the pivot to its line of action, with clockwise/anticlockwise sign. A couple is equal and opposite parallel forces whose torque is Fd, with d their perpendicular separation. Full static equilibrium requires both ΣF = 0 and Σmoments = 0. Three coplanar forces in equilibrium form a closed vector triangle; include the rotational condition as well. Locate a suspended object's centre of gravity by intersecting vertical plumb lines from different suspension points. Centre of mass and centre of gravity coincide for the usual uniform gravitational field.

Density ρ = m/V. Pressure is normal force/area. In a fluid at rest with uniform density, the pressure increase over depth h is ρgh; absolute pressure also includes pressure at the surface. Upthrust equals the weight of displaced fluid, ρfluidgVdisplaced. A floating object balances its weight with upthrust, so displaced fluid mass equals object mass; using the whole object volume is correct only if it is fully submerged.

Energy, work, power and momentum

For a constant force acting through displacement s, work W = Fs cos θ, with θ between force and displacement. Work transfers energy; resistive forces can remove mechanical energy while total energy remains conserved. Kinetic energy is ½mv² for ordinary nonrelativistic motion. The gravitational potential-energy change mgh is valid in a uniform field; it is not the general formula for planetary distances. Obtain these results through constant-force work and motion relations. Solve transfers by explicitly accounting for input, useful stored energy and dissipated energy.

Average power is transferred energy/time; for force along the motion, instantaneous mechanical power is Fv. Efficiency is useful output/input, expressed as a fraction or multiplied by 100% for a percentage. Ratios of powers can replace energy ratios for steady operation.

Momentum p = mv is a vector. Mean resultant force over an interval is Δp/Δt; for constant mass this reduces to ma. Impulse equals momentum change and equals signed area under a force–time graph; FΔt applies to constant or average force. Calculate Δp as final minus initial momentum, including a change of direction. Increasing collision time for the same impulse reduces mean force.

Conserve total momentum for a system with negligible external impulse. This applies separately to x and y in two-dimensional interactions. In a perfectly elastic collision, total kinetic energy is also conserved; an inelastic collision converts some kinetic energy to other forms. Objects sticking together are a particular inelastic case. Momentum conservation does not imply kinetic-energy conservation. Recoil, explosions and oblique collisions can be analysed with these same rules. Angular momentum and rigid-body rotational dynamics are not supplied by this qualification.

Springs and material response

Extension x is the change from original length, not final length. In the linear region, Hooke's law gives force magnitude F = kx; k has units N m⁻¹. A restoring force has the opposite direction to displacement. The gradient of an F–x plot gives stiffness; the area under it gives work. Elastic energy ½kx² = ½Fx follows only for linear loading from zero extension. For nonlinearity use the actual graph area. Springs in series share force and add extensions; springs in parallel share extension and add forces, so an effective stiffness can be derived from those conditions.

Tensile stress σ = F/A, strain ε = x/L, and Young modulus Y = σ/ε = FL/(Ax) in the proportional region. Stress and modulus have units Pa; strain is dimensionless. If using a stress–strain graph, its initial slope is Y. Ultimate tensile strength is the maximum tensile stress supported. Elastic deformation recovers on unloading; plastic deformation leaves a permanent change. The elastic limit and limit of proportionality describe different properties even when close together. Distinguish a brittle material's small plastic region from a ductile material's substantial plastic extension, and recognise the different shape of polymer response. Use the relevant loading/unloading curve to discuss recoverable and dissipated energy.

3. Charge, electrical circuits, waves and quantum phenomena

Current and circuits

Current is charge flow rate: I = ΔQ/Δt, so constant current transfers Q = It. A proton carries +e and an electron −e; for a net ordinary object's charge, Q is an integer multiple of e. Metallic current involves electrons; electrolytes involve ions. Conventional current follows positive-charge motion, opposite electron drift. For a uniform metal conductor, I = nAe vdrift, with n free-electron number density in m⁻³, A cross-sectional area and vdrift mean drift speed. This follows by counting carriers in a length vdriftΔt. Carrier density helps distinguish conductors, insulators and semiconductors; temperature effects need not involve an assumed constant n.

Potential difference is energy transferred per charge between points, V = W/Q. Source e.m.f. ℰ is energy supplied per charge; neither is a mechanical force. A particle accelerated from rest through a suitable potential difference gains kinetic energy |q|ΔV = ½mv² if other energy transfers and relativistic corrections are negligible. One electronvolt is the energy gained by charge magnitude e through 1 V: 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

Resistance R = V/I is a component property at a specified operating state. Ohm's law asserts V ∝ I when conditions, especially temperature, are constant. On an I-against-V graph, an ohmic resistor has slope 1/R; for a nonlinear component V/I at a point is not generally the local tangent's inverse slope. A filament's resistance increases as it heats, an NTC thermistor's resistance falls with temperature, an LDR's falls as illumination increases, and a diode/LED conducts mainly under forward bias. Know circuit symbols and meter connections: an ammeter is in series and a voltmeter across the measured component. Common symbols include a cell's unequal parallel lines (long line positive), repeated cells for a battery, a rectangle for a resistor, a resistor with an adjustment arrow for variable resistance, A or V within a circle for meters, a circle with a cross for a lamp, and parallel equal lines for an unpolarised capacitor. An LED is a diode symbol with outward light arrows; an LDR uses inward light arrows. A junction dot distinguishes connected conductors from crossings without connection. Recognise the switch, thermistor and supply forms and preserve component polarity in an actual diagram.

For a uniform wire, R = ρelectricalL/A; resistivity has units Ω m. Increasing metal temperature normally increases resistivity through more lattice scattering; semiconductor/NTC behaviour can decrease resistance as mobile carrier availability increases. Keep electrical resistivity distinct from mass density despite the shared symbol ρ.

Kirchhoff's junction law follows charge conservation: total current in equals total current out. The loop law follows energy conservation: signed potential rises and falls sum to zero. Series components share current and their voltages add; parallel branches share voltage and their currents add. Hence resistors in series have Rtotal = ΣR and in parallel 1/Rtotal = Σ(1/R). Use these relations or simultaneous loop/junction equations for mixed circuits and circuits with more than one e.m.f. source.

For a source delivering current, terminal voltage V = ℰ − Ir; I = ℰ/(Rexternal + r). Internal power loss is I²r. The measured terminal voltage approaches ℰ as current tends to zero. For a two-resistor unloaded divider with output across R₂, Vout = VinR₂/(R₁ + R₂); a sensor changes this ratio. Swapping the output component reverses the response. If a finite-resistance load is connected, combine it in parallel with the output resistor before using the divider relation. A potentiometer is a continuously adjustable divider.

Electrical power P = VI = I²R = V²/R, where the latter forms use the same component's V = IR. Energy = VIt for constant values. A kilowatt-hour is 3.6 × 10⁶ J; multiply energy in the billing unit by cost per unit. Compare useful energy and input when discussing appliance efficiency.

Waves and optics

A progressive wave transfers energy while its medium undergoes local oscillation. Transverse displacement is perpendicular to travel; longitudinal displacement is parallel, producing compressions and rarefactions. Distinguish a snapshot against position from one point's displacement against time. Amplitude is maximum displacement, wavelength the repeat distance, period the repeat time, f = 1/T and wave speed v = fλ. A spatial separation Δx gives phase difference 2πΔx/λ and a time separation Δt gives 2πΔt/T, for the same periodic wave. Intensity = power/area and is proportional to amplitude squared for a fixed medium and wave type.

Reflection redirects a wave at a boundary. Refraction changes direction when speed changes at an oblique boundary; frequency remains set by the source, so wavelength changes. Diffraction spreads waves at apertures or edges and is conspicuous when wavelength is comparable with the aperture width. Polarisation restricts transverse oscillation directions and provides evidence that electromagnetic waves are transverse; a longitudinal sound wave cannot be plane-polarised in this sense.

Know the electromagnetic spectrum in order of increasing frequency: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma ray. All travel at c in vacuum; order-of-magnitude wavelength knowledge is expected. Useful approximate scales are radio wavelengths from metres upward; microwaves from millimetres to decimetres; infrared around micrometres and longer; visible roughly 400–700 nm; ultraviolet from tens to hundreds of nanometres; X-rays around 10⁻¹¹–10⁻⁸ m; and gamma commonly shorter. These are approximate categories with overlap, rather than exact universal boundaries; gamma and X-rays can be distinguished by production mechanism. Explain polarising filters for light and conducting grilles for microwaves.

For refractive index n = c/v, Snell's law is n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, with both angles from the normal. Total internal reflection requires travel toward a lower refractive index and incidence greater than the critical angle. In general sin C = n₂/n₁; the booklet's sin C = 1/n assumes the second medium is air/vacuum. Use rectangular or semicircular blocks to trace rays and establish these relations.

Superposition means simultaneous displacements add algebraically. Coherent sources maintain a fixed phase relationship and the same frequency. For initially in-phase sources, constructive interference occurs at path difference mλ and destructive interference at (m + ½)λ. Complete cancellation additionally requires equal amplitudes. Young's double slits have fringe spacing x ≈ λD/a, for slit separation a, screen distance D and a geometry with small observation angles and D much greater than a. Measure several spacings to reduce fractional uncertainty.

A grating with spacing d gives maxima d sin θ = mλ, with θ measured from the straight-through direction and m an integer order. Convert lines per unit length to spacing before substitution. A possible order must satisfy |mλ/d| ≤ 1. The grating equation is A-level content, even though it appears in the astrophysics section as well as practical wave work; proving it and knowing spectrometer construction are not required.

Standing waves form through superposition of waves of the same frequency travelling oppositely. Nodes have zero oscillation; antinodes have maximum amplitude. Adjacent nodes or adjacent antinodes are λ/2 apart; a node and its adjacent antinode are λ/4 apart. Points between the same adjacent nodes oscillate in phase; adjacent loops have opposite phase. The ideal standing pattern has no net transfer of energy along it.

For a string fixed at both ends, L = mλ/2 and fm = mv/(2L), m = 1, 2, 3, …. For ideal open-open air tubes, the displacement pattern has antinodes at both ends and the same harmonic sequence; closed-open tubes have a displacement node at the closed end and antinode at the open end, so L = (2m − 1)λ/4 and only odd harmonics of v/(4L). Real tube openings require an end correction; consecutive resonance lengths in a closed-open tube differ by λ/2, which can remove a constant end-correction offset.

Photons and matter waves

A photon carries E = hf = hc/λ. Increased monochromatic intensity means more photons per second, rather than more energy per photon. The photoelectric effect involves one photon transferring energy to one surface electron. With work function φ, hf = φ + Kmax; threshold frequency f₀ = φ/h. Below threshold, the simple one-photon mechanism emits no photoelectrons however large the intensity. Above threshold, increasing frequency increases maximum kinetic energy, while increasing intensity at fixed frequency increases emission rate. Prompt emission and these dependencies support the photon model. If stopping potential is supplied, Kmax = eVs connects it to the emitted electrons' maximum energy.

For the LED experiment, treat eVthreshold ≈ hf as an experimental estimate, not an exact universal semiconductor law: a graph of threshold voltage against frequency estimates h/e. Detailed band theory is not required. For particles, de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p; combine with p = mv and accelerating-potential energy for nonrelativistic electrons. Graphite electron diffraction supports matter-wave behaviour. A greater accelerating voltage increases momentum and decreases wavelength. Wave-particle duality is usable qualitative and quantitative A-level knowledge; wavefunctions, operators, probability amplitudes and the Schrödinger equation are not assumed.

4. Thermal physics, circular motion, oscillations and gravitation

Temperature, internal energy and gases

Thermal equilibrium means no net thermal energy transfer between systems in thermal contact; their temperatures agree. Absolute temperature uses kelvin, approximately T/K = θ/°C + 273. A temperature interval of 1 K equals 1 °C, but gas equations require absolute T. Solids, liquids and gases differ in particle ordering, spacing and motion; Brownian motion is irregular visible-particle movement caused by molecular impacts.

Internal energy is the total microscopic random kinetic and interaction potential energy. Heating without a phase change generally increases temperature and microscopic kinetic energy. During a phase change at constant pressure, supplied energy can change intermolecular potential energy while temperature remains constant. Absolute zero is the lower temperature limit and minimum internal energy, not a licence to assert that every microscopic energy is literally zero.

Heat transferred for a temperature change is Q = mcΔT, with c in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, using a constant or appropriate average specific heat capacity for the stated material, state and process. A gas's heat capacity can differ between constant-volume and constant-pressure heating. Phase-change heat is Q = mℓ, with ℓ in J kg⁻¹ for the stated transition and conditions; distinguish fusion from vaporisation. These heat-input expressions do not by themselves assert Q = ΔU: a gas can also do expansion work on its surroundings. For a specified closed system with no change in bulk kinetic or gravitational potential energy, whose only transfers are heat input Q and work output W, energy conservation gives ΔU = Q − W. This is a conservation deduction when the transfers and work are supplied or established, rather than a grant of further thermodynamic identities or unprovided process-specific heat capacities. A process crossing a phase boundary can require several heat terms that must be added. Electrical input VIt equals the relevant sample energy gain only after considering work, losses and heating of containers or heaters.

Amount n = N/NA, where N is particle count and NA Avogadro's constant. The ideal-gas model uses many rapidly and randomly moving particles with negligible volume, negligible interparticle forces except during collisions, elastic collisions and short collision durations. Pressure results from particle momentum changes at container walls. pV = nRT = NkT, with k = R/NA. For a fixed particle number and composition, pV is constant when temperature is fixed, and p/T is constant when volume is fixed. Identify all held-fixed quantities before applying a gas-law shortcut; fixed volume alone does not make p/T constant if particles are added, removed or changed in number by a reaction.

Kinetic theory gives pV = ⅓Nm⟨v²⟩ for a population of particles with a common mass m. If it is applied to one species in an ideal mixture, N, m and ⟨v²⟩ refer to that species and p is its contribution to pressure; the species' pressure contributions add. Root-mean-square speed is √⟨v²⟩, not the mean speed. Combining the two pressure expressions gives mean translational kinetic energy ½m⟨v²⟩ = 3kT/2 and vrms = √(3kT/m). At a common temperature, ideal species have the same mean translational kinetic energy but different rms speeds when their masses differ; do not use one species' mass as a common particle mass for an arbitrary mixture. The A-level translational particle model gives total kinetic contribution 3NkT/2; applying this as the complete internal energy needs the corresponding model, rather than an unspoken claim about all real polyatomic gases. Increasing T broadens a Maxwell–Boltzmann speed distribution for the stated species and shifts its characteristic speeds upward, while its area remains tied to that particle number. The qualitative shape is required; deriving the distribution or the kinetic pressure equation is not.

Circular motion and simple harmonic motion

A full turn is 2π radians; arc length is rθ. For uniform circular motion, ω = 2π/T = 2πf and speed v = ωr. Velocity changes direction even when speed is constant. Acceleration magnitude v²/r = ω²r points toward the centre, requiring net inward force mv²/r. Identify which actual forces supply this resultant: gravity, tension, friction or field force. 'Centripetal force' describes their resultant role and should not be added as an extra force. Uniform circular motion requires a force of constant magnitude whose direction changes around the circle.

Simple harmonic motion satisfies a = −ω²x, where x is measured from stable equilibrium. Its restoring acceleration is proportional to displacement and opposite in direction. Use x = A cos ωt or A sin ωt with an appropriate time origin/phase. Speed is |v| = ω√(A² − x²); the sign depends on the direction of motion. At equilibrium speed is maximum ωA and acceleration zero; at either extreme speed is zero and acceleration magnitude ω²A. Displacement and acceleration are opposite in phase; velocity is a quarter-cycle out of phase with displacement. Ideal SHM period is amplitude-independent within the model's validity.

For an ideal spring oscillator, combine F = −kx and F = ma to obtain ω² = k/m and T = 2π√(m/k). Potential and kinetic energies trade while total energy ½kA² stays constant; their displacement graphs are ½kx² and ½k(A² − x²). Timing real spring or pendulum motion is required practical experience. A small-angle pendulum can be explained using the supplied/derived restoring-force model; the specification does not separately list memorisation of its period formula as a learning outcome.

Free oscillations occur at a system's natural frequency; a driving force produces forced oscillations. Damping dissipates mechanical energy, reducing free-oscillation amplitude. At resonance a driving frequency near the natural frequency produces a large response; damping reduces and broadens the resonance peak. Relate the driving-frequency/amplitude graph to practical systems. The algebraic SHM results and energy plots are baseline knowledge; solving its differential equation, or general damped/forced oscillator equations, is not a Physics A prerequisite.

Gravity and orbital motion

Gravitational field strength is force per unit test mass, g = F/m, in N kg⁻¹, numerically equivalent to free-fall acceleration. Fields point toward attracting masses. Outside a spherically symmetric mass distribution, use its total mass at its centre: field magnitude GM/r² and force magnitude GMm/r². r is centre-to-centre separation, not height above the surface. With outward radial direction positive, the radial force and field components are negative. Close to Earth's surface, modest height changes permit the uniform-g approximation.

For a circular orbit dominated by central mass M, gravity supplies the inward resultant, so v = √(GM/r) and T² = 4π²r³/(GM). Derive this by equating force expressions, rather than merely recognising it. Kepler's laws describe elliptical orbits with the central body at a focus, equal areas swept in equal times, and period squared proportional to the cube of semimajor axis. The circular formula uses radius; the ellipse generalisation uses semimajor axis. A geostationary satellite must have an equatorial circular orbit, follow Earth's rotation in the same direction and match its rotation period; matching the period alone is insufficient.

Set gravitational potential zero at infinity. For a point/exterior spherical mass, Vg = −GM/r is potential energy per unit mass and U = mVg = −GMm/r. Calculate energy changes from final minus initial values; moving outward raises potential energy toward zero. Graphical area under a force–distance curve gives work with a carefully stated force direction and work convention. Escape to infinity with zero final speed requires ½mvesc² = GMm/r, giving vesc = √(2GM/r) if drag, rotation and other masses are neglected. The formulas for gravitational potential and escape speed are explicitly A-level content.

5. Stars and cosmology

Distinguish planets, their satellites, comets, solar systems, galaxies and the universe. Stellar formation begins with gravity gathering gas and dust; heating leads to fusion, and a main-sequence star balances inward gravitational effects with pressure associated with hot matter and radiation. Hydrogen fusion supplies energy. Low-mass evolution can proceed through a red giant and loss of outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving a white dwarf. A white dwarf is supported by electron degeneracy pressure: quantum restrictions on electron states oppose further compression, even without fusion sustaining ordinary thermal pressure. The Chandrasekhar limit is approximately 1.4 solar masses; above it that form of support cannot maintain a white dwarf. Massive stars can become red supergiants, undergo supernova and leave neutron stars or black holes. Know the characteristics of these remnants qualitatively; a neutron star is an extremely compact, dense, neutron-rich remnant, and a black hole has an event horizon from which light cannot escape.

An HR diagram plots luminosity against surface temperature; temperature conventionally decreases to the right. Locate main-sequence stars, red giants/supergiants and white dwarfs and explain how stars of similar temperature can have very different luminosities through their radii.

An isolated atom has discrete electron energy levels. Taking the free electron as zero makes bound energies negative. An emission transition to a lower level produces photon energy |ΔE| = hf = hc/λ; absorption requires a matching energy gap. Use the gap, not one level's value relative to zero, unless ionisation is the transition. Continuous, emission-line and absorption-line spectra have different origins; line patterns identify stellar elements.

For a black-body approximation, Wien's relation is λmaxT = constant, so hotter surfaces peak at shorter wavelength. The specification/booklet supplies the proportionality λmax ∝ 1/T; use a given Wien constant or comparison data when required. Stellar luminosity L = 4πRstar²σT⁴ is total emitted power; received flux at distance d is L/(4πd²) for isotropic emission without intervening loss. Combine spectral peak, luminosity or observed flux and distance to estimate surface temperature and radius. Do not confuse star radius with distance to the observer.

Use AU, light-year and parsec as distance units. Stellar parallax angle is the half-angle of the apparent annual shift; d in parsecs = 1/p in arcseconds. One arcsecond is 1/3600 degree. Apply the small-angle geometry carefully to a one-AU baseline.

For electromagnetic Doppler shifts at speeds small compared with c, recession gives Δλ/λ ≈ v/c, while the signed frequency change has the opposite sign; the booklet writes the fractional shifts in magnitude form. Hubble's relation v ≈ H₀d connects measured recession and distance in its applicable regime. Convert km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ to s⁻¹ before using 1/H₀ as a time estimate. A Hubble age is an estimate, not an exact age for every expansion history.

The cosmological principle treats the universe as homogeneous and isotropic on large scales, with universal physical laws. Galactic redshifts support expansion; the approximately 2.7 K microwave background supports a hot early universe through its nearly uniform thermal spectrum, interpreted as early radiation cooled/redshifted by expansion. Describe an early hot dense phase, cooling/expansion, formation of nuclei and atoms, and later structure and stars. The Big Bang describes expansion of space-time; it is not an ordinary blast from a privileged centre into pre-existing empty space. Know that ordinary matter is a small part of the current model. Dark matter is inferred through gravitational effects without the ordinary detectable light emission that would explain its mass; dark energy is the model's contribution associated with accelerated cosmic expansion. Detailed cosmological dynamics, tensor relativity and quantitative degeneracy theory are beyond this baseline.

6. Capacitors, electric and magnetic fields

Capacitance and exponential processes

For a linear capacitor, C = Q/V, where ±Q are equal-magnitude opposite plate charges and V the potential difference. The capacitor is net neutral overall in the usual charging circuit; electrons move through the external circuit between plates rather than crossing the insulating gap. Parallel capacitances add because voltages agree and charges add. Series capacitors carry equal charge in the ordinary initially uncharged arrangement, their voltages add, and 1/Ctotal = Σ(1/C). Analyse capacitor/resistor circuits using those charge, current and voltage constraints.

Energy is area under the V-against-Q graph: U = ½QV = Q²/(2C) = ½CV². Changing C has different consequences depending on whether a source fixes V or an isolated capacitor fixes Q. State which condition applies before predicting changes.

For constant R and C, a discharge has Q = Q₀e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ, V = V₀e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ and current magnitude I = I₀e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ. Current direction depends on the adopted convention; a signed charge-removal rate is ΔQ/Δt ≈ −Q/(RC). The time constant τ = RC is the time to reach 1/e of an initial decaying value. Charging from zero through R from constant supply ℰ gives Vcapacitor = ℰ(1 − e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ), Q = Cℰ(1 − e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ), while current decays as (ℰ/R)e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ. The charging expression does not apply to the current. At one time constant the capacitor has reached about 63% of its final charge. Use ln V versus time for discharge to obtain slope −1/(RC); solve for times by logarithms. Model successive small changes in a spreadsheet without presupposing differential-equation solving.

Electric fields and potential

Electric field strength is force per unit positive test charge, E = F/q; its units are N C⁻¹ or V m⁻¹. Field arrows show the force direction on a positive charge, so a negative charge accelerates opposite to the arrows. A point charge Q gives radial field Q/(4πε₀r²), force on q equal to qE, and potential V = Q/(4πε₀r), with zero at infinity. A positive source has outward field and positive potential; a negative source has inward field and negative potential. Potentials add as scalars and fields as vectors; charges can attract or repel, while the gravitational point-mass force is attractive.

For broad parallel plates away from edges, uniform field magnitude is ΔV/d. A charged particle experiences constant acceleration qE/m, so link the field to one-dimensional acceleration or projectile-like deflection. Energy U = qV and work by the electric field is −ΔU. These signs matter for electron motion. Outside a uniformly charged sphere, model charge at its centre; that exterior formula should not be applied indiscriminately inside it.

For a parallel-plate capacitor, C = εA/d, with ε = εrε₀, reducing to ε₀A/d in vacuum/approximately air. A dielectric with greater permittivity increases C at fixed geometry; detailed microscopic dielectric theory is not required. For an isolated conducting sphere of radius R, surface potential Q/(4πε₀R) and C = Q/V give C = 4πε₀R. Force–distance graph areas and potential differences describe the same work when the sign convention is maintained.

Magnetic forces and induction

Moving charges and permanent magnets create magnetic fields. Recognise the field patterns around a straight current, a coil and a solenoid, including their direction relative to current. Magnetic flux density B is measured in tesla. A straight wire length L carrying current I in uniform B has force magnitude BIL sin θ, where θ is the angle between current and field. Fleming's left-hand rule gives the force direction for conventional current.

For a charge moving perpendicular to B, force magnitude is |q|vB, perpendicular to both velocity and field; reverse its direction for negative charge. A magnetic force changes direction rather than speed and does no work. Equating it to mv²/r gives r = mv/(|q|B) for the circular orbit. The explicitly specified charged-particle case is perpendicular motion; general vector products are not presupposed. In crossed fields arranged to oppose, a straight-through velocity selector has |q|E = |q|vB and selected speed v = E/B.

Magnetic flux through a flat area is Φ = BA cos θ for uniform B, with θ measured between field and the area's normal. Flux linkage is NΦ for N identical linked turns; Φ is in Wb. An induced e.m.f. arises when linkage changes: mean ℰ = −Δ(NΦ)/Δt, or use the tangent rate at a specified instant. The minus sign expresses Lenz's opposition to the change in flux, consistent with energy conservation; it does not mean opposition to an existing field in every case. Change B, area, orientation or relative position to induce an e.m.f. A search coil's induced-voltage/time area measures a linkage change.

A simple alternating-current generator uses rotating coil linkage to generate alternating e.m.f.; faster rotation or larger linked flux gives greater magnitude. A transformer uses changing primary current and flux in a core to induce secondary e.m.f. For an ideal transformer, Vs/Vp = Ns/Np = Ip/Is and input power equals output power. A step-up voltage reduces current for the same transferred power; reduced line current reduces I²R transmission loss. Laminations reduce eddy-current heating; real transformers have losses. Constant DC does not sustain transformer action after the initial change.

7. Nuclear and particle physics, and medical imaging

Nuclear structure and particle rules

Alpha scattering supports a small dense positively charged nucleus: most particles pass through, while a small fraction deflect strongly. An atom has a scale around 10⁻¹⁰ m and a nucleus around 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m. In nuclide notation ᴬZX, Z is proton number, A is total nucleon number and neutron number is A − Z. Isotopes share Z but differ in neutron number. Nuclear radius R = r₀A¹ᐟ³ implies nuclear volume proportional to A and approximately constant nuclear density. Nuclear strong-force behaviour is short range, attractive over roughly 0.5–3 fm and repulsive at still shorter separations.

An antiparticle has the same mass as its partner and opposite relevant quantum properties; electron/positron and proton/antiproton have opposite charge. Hadrons, including protons and neutrons, participate in the strong and weak interactions; leptons, including electrons and neutrinos, do not participate in the strong interaction but can participate in the weak. The simple quark model uses u, d and s and their antiquarks: u has +2e/3; d and s have −e/3; antiquark charges reverse. Proton = uud and neutron = udd. Add constituent charges and check charge conservation in proposed transformations.

Beta-minus changes d → u + e⁻ + electron antineutrino, corresponding to n → p + e⁻ + antineutrino. Beta-plus changes u → d + e⁺ + electron neutrino, corresponding to p → n + e⁺ + neutrino in an energetically allowed decay. In beta-minus, nuclide A remains fixed and Z rises by one; beta-plus lowers Z by one. Alpha emission decreases A by four and Z by two. Gamma emission changes nuclear energy without changing A or Z. Balance nucleon and charge numbers, include the relevant beta neutrino, and recognise that beta particles are not pre-existing orbital electrons expelled from the atom.

Radioactivity and nuclear energy

Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random: a single nucleus's decay time cannot be predicted, but a large population has a predictable average behaviour. Ionisation removes electrons from atoms or molecules. Alpha particles are helium nuclei, highly ionising, travelling only a few centimetres in air and stopped by paper or the outer layer of skin; beta particles are electrons/positrons with intermediate penetration, typically travelling farther in air and attenuated by millimetres of suitable material such as aluminium; gamma photons are more penetrating and less densely ionising, requiring substantial lead/concrete to reduce intensity rather than having a sharp universal stopping range. Actual ranges depend on energy and material. Identify appropriate absorbers and distinguish external from internal exposure in the stated context. Correct a detector's count rate for background and recognise that count rate is not automatically the source's total activity because efficiency and geometry intervene.

Activity A = λN is the expected decay rate in Bq, where N is the number of undecayed nuclei at that time and λ is the isotope's constant conditional decay rate per surviving nucleus, in s⁻¹. N = N₀e⁻λt, A = A₀e⁻λt and t½ = ln 2/λ. Given that a nucleus has survived to the start of an interval Δt, the exponential survival law gives probability 1 − e⁻λΔt of decay during that interval; this is approximately λΔt only when λΔt is small compared with one. Thus the usual phrase 'decay probability per unit time' describes a rate, rather than an exact finite-interval probability. Use either repeated halvings or logarithms; the same fraction disappears in equal time intervals. ln(activity) against time has slope −λ only after background and other relevant contributions are dealt with. A small-time spreadsheet model uses ΔN ≈ −λNΔt under the same small-λΔt condition. Dating assumes an initial isotopic ratio/activity and a closed-system history adequate for the model; the measured fraction gives t = ln(N₀/N)/λ. Simple probability reasoning and dice models are part of this level; the interval-probability expression follows directly from the stated survival law and does not assume advanced probability theory.

Mass defect is the separate constituents' mass minus the bound nucleus's mass; binding energy is Δmc². Use compatible masses, especially whether quoted masses are nuclear or atomic and whether electron masses cancel. Convert kg, unified atomic mass units and eV consistently. Binding energy per nucleon measures how strongly bound a nucleus is on average, not the energy of one chosen nucleon. A reaction moving products toward higher total binding energy can release energy: fission of heavy nuclei and fusion of light ones. Calculate reaction energy from the total initial mass minus final mass times c², or from consistent total binding energies; distinguish energy per nucleon from total energy.

Induced fission can emit neutrons that continue a chain reaction. Fuel supplies fissile nuclei, a moderator slows neutrons where the reactor design requires it, and control rods absorb neutrons to control the chain. Explain heat transfer and waste implications in context. Fusion combines light nuclei and requires sufficiently energetic encounters to overcome electrostatic repulsion; high temperature and confinement matter. Balance the nuclear equations.

Particle–antiparticle annihilation converts rest energy and any kinetic energy into other products while conserving energy and momentum. Pair creation requires sufficient energy for both rest masses and a way to conserve momentum. For an electron–positron pair at rest, two opposed annihilation photons each have approximately 511 keV. This is an application of E = mc², not an assumed relativistic-mechanics course.

Imaging models and calculations

In an X-ray tube, a heated cathode supplies electrons; a high voltage accelerates them toward a metal anode target. Electron deceleration produces a continuous spectrum and atomic transitions can give characteristic lines. Maximum photon energy cannot exceed eVaccelerating, so λminimum = hc/(eV) under that model. Most input energy heats the target.

X-ray attenuation includes scattering, photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering and, at sufficiently high photon energy, electron–positron pair creation. Know the physical distinctions: photoelectric absorption removes the incident photon; Compton scattering transfers some energy to an electron and leaves a lower-energy scattered photon; simple scattering changes direction; pair creation makes particles. The quantitative Compton wavelength-shift formula is not required.

For constant attenuation coefficient μ and the specified beam/material model, I = I₀e⁻μx; μ has inverse-length units. A plot of ln(I/I₀) against thickness has slope −μ, and half-value thickness is ln 2/μ. Keep thickness units consistent. Different attenuation across tissues produces an image; barium or iodine contrast increases the relevant visibility. A plain radiograph combines structures along a line of sight; a CAT/CT scanner acquires many angular X-ray measurements with a rotating tube, thin fan beam and detectors, then reconstructs slices, reducing superposition ambiguity.

A tracer's emissions reveal its distribution/function in the body. Know technetium-99m and fluorine-18 as the specified examples; a useful choice considers detectable radiation, biological targeting and a half-life long enough for the examination but short enough to limit dose. A gamma camera uses a collimator to select directions, a scintillator to convert gamma detection to light, photomultiplier tubes to produce/amplify electrical signals, and a computer to form an image. In PET, positron-emitting tracer decays are followed by annihilation; nearly opposing photon detections in coincidence identify a line on which the event occurred, and many events form a reconstructed activity image. Imaging geometry does not locate one event at an exact point without additional information.

Ultrasound is a longitudinal wave above 20 kHz. A piezoelectric transducer converts electrical signals into ultrasound and returning sound into electrical signals. Echo timing gives depth = cΔt/2 because travel is outward and back. An A-scan displays echo amplitude against delay/depth; a B-scan combines lines to map brightness in a section.

Acoustic impedance Z = ρc. For the stated normal-incidence boundary model, the reflected intensity fraction is (Z₂ − Z₁)²/(Z₂ + Z₁)². Large mismatch produces a large reflection; coupling gel removes an intervening air gap and improves transmission. In the assessed low-speed Doppler ultrasound model, |Δf|/f = 2v|cos θ|/c, where f is transmitted frequency, v is blood-flow speed, c is sound speed, v is small compared with c, and θ is the angle between the directed flow and beam. Approaching the transducer increases reflected frequency; moving away decreases it. Solve for v, account for the round-trip factor two, and recognise that perpendicular flow gives no Doppler shift in this model. If θ is explicitly the acute angle between the flow line and beam, cos θ is already nonnegative. Ultrasound avoids ionising radiation, while X-ray, gamma camera and PET techniques involve it; compare choices using the anatomical/function information and context given, not a universal ranking of scanners. MRI is not part of the H556 imaging content listed here.

8. What may be assumed when first-year teaching begins

The student can provisionally be expected to select equations, justify their assumptions, connect multiple topic areas, use a diagram and graph, carry units and signs through a calculation, and explain physical mechanisms in a coherent argument. A question can combine acceleration through a p.d., a magnetic orbit and a photon transition; it need not be presented as a familiar single-topic exercise. Practical methods and evidence evaluation apply throughout. The 2025 Unified physics examiner report supports testing multi-step reasoning, unfamiliar contexts, data selection, unit prefixes and contextual explanations.

Physics A itself does not establish fluency in formal differentiation/integration, vector dot/cross notation, differential-equation solving, linear algebra, Maxwell's equations, thermodynamic identities or entropy, relativistic spacetime, quantum operators or advanced statistical data analysis. It does establish rate/area reasoning graphically, exponential and logarithmic calculations, component vectors, algebraic SHM, inverse-square fields and potentials, and the substantive nuclear/medical/astrophysical models above. A student taking A-level Mathematics or Further Mathematics may already possess additional formal methods; those belong to that separate subject baseline.

No fixed threshold score or claimed personal mastery follows from curriculum completion. First-year diagnostic testing should distinguish lack of retained knowledge, incorrect assumptions/signs, weak algebra and unfamiliar formalism rather than treating every university-labelled topic as new.
