A1. On x>0, fprime(x)=1/(2sqrt(x)). At a=4, f(a)=2 and fprime(a)=1/4. The displacement of the requested input is h=4.08-4=0.08. Thus the exact tangent polynomial is L(x)=2+(x-4)/4=1+x/4, and its exact value at 4.08 is 2.02. The approximation is sqrt(4.08) approximately L(4.08)=2.02, not equality of the function and polynomial. A2. The function is smooth on x<1/2, in particular in a neighbourhood of zero. exp(3x)=1+3x+(9/2)x²+o(x²), and (1-2x)^(-1/2)=1+x+(3/2)x²+o(x²). Product terms at degree two are (9/2)x² from the first factor times 1, (3x)(x)=3x², and (3/2)x² from the second factor times 1. Their sum is 9x². Thus L(x)=1+4x and Q(x)=1+4x+9x². The basepoint quantities are g(0)=1 and gprime(0)=4. The remainder is o(x²), so [g(x)-1-4x]/x²=9+o(1), giving the limit 9. First-order information g=1+4x+o(x) alone supplies no value for the divided remainder: for example adding kx² for any fixed k preserves the same first-order expansion and changes that limit to k. The substitutions approach zero as x tends to zero; practical small-input language is |3x| and |2x| small, with 1-2x>0. It does not provide a finite numerical error bound by itself. Independent derivative check: (log g)prime=3+1/(1-2x); its derivative is 2/(1-2x)². Therefore gprime(0)=4 and gdoubleprime(0)=4²+2=18, matching Q coefficient 18/2=9.

A4. Let h=x-a and let f have two continuous derivatives near a. The linear polynomial is L_a(x)=f(a)+fprime(a)h; differentiability provides f(a+h)=L_a(a+h)+o(h). The quadratic polynomial is Q_a(x)=f(a)+fprime(a)h+fdoubleprime(a)h²/2, and the C2 assumption supplies f(a+h)=Q_a(a+h)+o(h²). For a trial polynomial c0+c1h+c2h², the value, first derivative and second derivative at h=0 are c0,c1,2c2, hence the factor 1/2 matches fdoubleprime(a). At zero, cos has value 1, first derivative 0 and second derivative -1, so cos(x)=1-x²/2+o(x²); the requested limit is -1/2. The constant-plus-linear approximation 1 leaves only an o(x) remainder, which cannot determine the quotient by x². For sine at zero, value 0, first derivative 1 and second derivative 0 give L(x)=Q(x)=x. A quadratic approximation may have zero coefficient of x²; the method does not require the resulting polynomial to have degree exactly two. All trigonometric derivative and remainder statements use radians.
