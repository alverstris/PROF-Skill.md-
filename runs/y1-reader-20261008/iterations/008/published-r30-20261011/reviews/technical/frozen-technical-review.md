Mathematical and explanatory reconstruction

1. Linearization, lesson A. The ordinary tangent formula becomes a function of the displacement h=x−x0, with f(x0) and f'(x0) fixed values. Substituting h=0 checks the height. Defining the difference R1 and dividing by h connects the approximation to the already-known derivative limit, so R1(h)/h tends to zero. This licenses the later first-order coefficient and limit deductions. A horizontal tangent is explicitly admissible. The logarithm's basepoint, domain and shift are worked rather than left to visual inference. The warning is local and does not assert that error must increase monotonically with distance.

2. Standard forms and composition, lesson B. All five linear coefficients are correct. Angles are in radians; r is fixed; positive base 1+u is a sufficient real domain for arbitrary real r; ln(1+u) needs u>−1. The input to each template is identified. For exp(−2x)/sqrt(1+x), the factor linearizations give 1−5x/2. Their product's x² term is incomplete as second-order information because each factor's omitted quadratic term also contributes. The lesson explains that discarding an order does not make it zero. Its direct derivative check gives −5/2, independently agreeing with the original-only calculation. A nonzero denominator is needed for the reciprocal/quotient shortcut, and the lesson supplies that condition rather than treating closeness alone as sufficient.

3. Limit, lesson C. For F(x)=(1+2x)^10, F(0)=1 and F'(0)=20, so the displayed quotient is the derivative quotient at zero. The alternative remainder route divides an equality with R1(x)/x tending to zero; it does not divide an unqualified approximate equality by a vanishing denominator. The exact binomial calculation in the independent source preparation gives the same limit 20. The lesson's warning discriminates this valid argument from a plausible but unsupported approximate substitution.

4. Quadratic construction and local error, lesson D. Differentiating A+Bh+Ch² gives A, B and 2C at the basepoint, so coefficient matching correctly produces the half factor. It exactly reproduces a quadratic and may degenerate to degree zero or one. The term “best fit” is confined to local derivative matching and does not claim least-squares optimality. The stated sufficient hypothesis—f'' exists and is continuous in an interval around the basepoint—is valid. The lesson identifies f'' as the constrained object and explains the relevant continuity test in terms of nearby second-derivative values. Its twice-integrated difference has zero initial value and slope; a bound M on its second derivative gives |R2(h)|≤Mh²/2 for either sign of h. Continuity permits M to tend to zero. This proves the stated local limit without silently assuming a general remainder theorem from the baseline. It supplies no false fixed-tolerance guarantee.

The cosine polynomial 1−x²/2, its value 0.98 at x=0.2, and both stated error sizes agree with an independent 60-digit numerical check. All five quadratic formulas are correct. Sine's second derivative at zero vanishes, giving Q(x)=x rather than a forced nonzero squared term. The logarithm and power derivatives correctly establish their signs and coefficients.

5. Complete second-order product, lesson E. exp(−2x)=1−2x+2x² through degree two and (1+x)^(−1/2)=1−x/2+3x²/8. The three quadratic contributions are 2, 1 and 3/8, summing to 27/8; thus Q=1−5x/2+27x²/8. Independent differentiation gives f''(0)=27/4. The lesson correctly discards the resulting cubic/quartic products for a quadratic model, without claiming that these are complete higher-order coefficients of the original function.

6. Clock model, lesson F. T is the carried clock's elapsed proper time; T' is coordinate time between the same two events, assigned by synchronized reference clocks. The prime is explained as part of the name. The constant-velocity, inertial-frame, gravity-neglected premise is stated before use, with the inertial-frame meaning supplied. The equation is introduced as a physical law rather than derived by calculus or confused with received-signal delays. For 0≤v<c and positive T, the sign T'>T at positive speed, the zero-speed limit, units, and dimensionless ratio are correct.

With epsilon=v²/c², u=−epsilon and r=−1/2, the ratio is 1+epsilon/2+3epsilon²/8 through degree two. Multiplication by T is retained when converting the ratio to time. For the illustrative source speeds, epsilon=1/5625000000, the leading fractional increase is 8.8888888889×10^−11, and over a carried interval of 86400 s it contributes 7.68×10^−6 s. The second fractional correction is 1.1851851852×10^−20, or 1.024×10^−15 s for that interval. The leading omitted ratio term is 5epsilon³/16. Independent exact rational arithmetic agrees. “Linear” and “quadratic” are explicitly relative to epsilon; they are second and fourth degree in v/c. The lesson does not infer attainable absolute precision from a small fraction and does not present this model as the complete GPS correction.
