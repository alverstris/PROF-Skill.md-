   correct. Evidence: p3-proof-equalities.png.

2. PDF p3 / printed p2, Proof of 5, final display: rendered
      f(x)=f(0)+f'(0)x=1+rx.
   The first equality is generally false. Use f(x) approximately 1+rx, or define
   L(x)=f(0)+f'(0)x=1+rx. Exact equality happens in special cases r=0 or r=1.
   Evidence: p3-proof-equalities.png.

3. PDF p5 / printed p4, Quirk expansion below the T/T' definitions: rendered
      T'=T(1-v^2/c^2)^(-1/2) approximately 1+(1/2)(v^2/c^2).
   A multiplicative T is omitted on the right. The corrected dimensional formula
   is T' approximately T[1+(1/2)(v^2/c^2)], or the dimensionless ratio
   T'/T approximately 1+(1/2)(v^2/c^2). Evidence:
   p5-time-factor-exponent.png. This is a source-rendered defect, not extraction.

INDEPENDENT DERIVATIONS: GENERAL FORM AND BASIC LISTS

Write h=x-x0. At a differentiable point,
  L(x)=f(x0)+f'(x0)h, and f(x)=L(x)+o(h).
At a twice differentiable point with suitable local regularity,
  Q(x)=f(x0)+f'(x0)h+(1/2)f''(x0)h^2,
  f(x)=Q(x)+o(h^2).
For the smooth functions here, the respective remainder orders near the base
point are O(h^2) and O(h^3), except that vanishing next coefficients can improve
the order. A numerical tolerance requires a remainder bound or comparison,
not just a small-argument slogan. A zero remainder at a point does not make
the identity global. Approximation error need not increase monotonically with
distance for an arbitrary function, despite the source's informal description.

For p(x)=a+bx+cx^2 at x0=0:
  p(0)=a, p'(x)=b+2cx, p'(0)=b, p''(x)=2c.
Thus a=p(0), b=p'(0), c=p''(0)/2, verifying the half factor exactly. At arbitrary
x0, substituting h gives the same derivative matching; Q=p for every x when p
has degree at most 2. This does not establish exactness for other functions.

Basepoint-zero derivative table (PDF pp3,6 / printed pp2,5):
function          f(0)  f'(0)  f''(0)     linear L              quadratic Q
sin x             0     1       0         x                     x
cos x             1     0      -1         1                     1-x^2/2
exp x             1     1       1         1+x                   1+x+x^2/2
ln(1+x)           0     1      -1         x                     x-x^2/2
(1+x)^r           1     r       r(r-1)    1+rx                  1+rx+r(r-1)x^2/2

Independent derivatives:
sin'=cos, sin''=-sin; cos'=-sin, cos''=-cos; exp'=exp''=exp;
[ln(1+x)]'=1/(1+x), [ln(1+x)]''=-1/(1+x)^2;
[(1+x)^r]'=r(1+x)^(r-1), second derivative=r(r-1)(1+x)^(r-2).
These agree with every entry in the source lists. The sin quadratic has degree
at most two with zero x^2 coefficient; it need not have a nonzero x^2 term.
Leading omitted terms near zero:
sin x = x-x^3/6+O(x^5);
cos x = 1-x^2/2+x^4/24+O(x^6);
exp x = 1+x+x^2/2+x^3/6+O(x^4);
ln(1+x)=x-x^2/2+x^3/3+O(x^4);
(1+x)^r=1+rx+r(r-1)x^2/2+r(r-1)(r-2)x^3/6+O(x^4), for fixed real r.

Domain/units checks:
sin/cos arguments use radians. sin, cos, exp are real and smooth on all R.
ln(1+x) requires x>-1. For arbitrary real r, using x>-1 gives a positive base
and smooth real (1+x)^r. Integer or special rational exponents can allow larger
domains, but no such extension is needed for these local calculations.
The binomial approximation is for fixed r as x tends to zero; a large |r| can
make coefficients large, so merely checking |x|<1 is not an accuracy guarantee.

ln x at x0=1 (PDF p2 / printed p1): f(1)=0, f'(x)=1/x, f'(1)=1, so
  ln x approximately x-1.
Setting x=1+u gives ln(1+u) approximately u, u0=0, u>-1. Strictly this is a
change of variable that moves the expansion center to zero in the new variable.
Because ln is concave on its real domain, ln(1+u)<=u, equality only u=0.

INDEPENDENT DERIVATIONS: e^(-2x)/sqrt(1+x)

Source locators: Example 2 starts PDF p3 / printed p2 and finishes PDF p4 /
printed p3; Example 5 is PDF p7 / printed p6.

Real domain: x>-1; the original is undefined at x=-1 and not real for x<-1.
For this domain f(x)=e^(-2x)(1+x)^(-1/2)>0, so f has no real zeros.

Factor expansions:
  e^(-2x)=1-2x+2x^2+O(x^3),
  (1+x)^(-1/2)=1-x/2+3x^2/8+O(x^3).
Linear product: (1-2x)(1-x/2)=1-(5/2)x+x^2.
The x^2 here cannot be retained as a quadratic approximation because the
discarded factor terms 2x^2 and 3x^2/8 contribute at the same order.
Complete quadratic coefficient:
  2 + (-2)(-1/2) + 3/8 = 2+1+3/8=27/8.
Therefore
  L(x)=1-(5/2)x,
  Q(x)=1-(5/2)x+(27/8)x^2.
The exact product of the two quadratic truncations is
  1-(5/2)x+(27/8)x^2-(7/4)x^3+(3/4)x^4.
Its cubic coefficient is not the true cubic coefficient, since both factor
cubic terms were omitted. The true series starts
  f(x)=1-(5/2)x+(27/8)x^2-(163/48)x^3+O(x^4).

Independent derivative route, with z=1+x>0:
  f'/f=-2-1/(2z),
  f''/f=4+2/z+3/(4z^2),
  f'''/f=-8-6/z-9/(2z^2)-15/(8z^3).
At x=0, f=1, f'=-5/2, f''=27/4, f'''=-163/8; Q coefficient f''/2=27/8.
Thus f is strictly decreasing and strictly convex over x>-1. f'''<0 there.
Its tangent L is a strict underestimate away from zero. Taylor's remainder
shows f(x)<Q(x) for x>0 and f(x)>Q(x) for -1<x<0.

Sign/zero checks on the approximants:
L has root x=2/5=0.4 and becomes negative for x>0.4, whereas the true function
is always positive; this demonstrates the local limitation. Q has discriminant
25/4-27/2=-29/4<0 and positive leading coefficient; its minimum occurs at
x=10/27 with value 29/54>0. These roots/signs agree with the numerical checks.

INDEPENDENT DERIVATIONS: LIMIT FOR (1+2x)^10

Locator: PDF p4 / printed p3, Example 3. Define g(x)=(1+2x)^10.
g(0)=1 and g'(x)=20(1+2x)^9, hence g'(0)=20. By the derivative definition,
  lim[x->0] ((1+2x)^10-1)/x =20.
The quotient is defined for every real x other than zero; the removable
discontinuity at zero is filled by value 20. The polynomial g is defined on R.
Exact binomial coefficients from degree 0 to 10 are
  1,20,180,960,3360,8064,13440,15360,11520,5120,1024.
Thus g=1+20x+180x^2+960x^3+... and the quotient=20+180x+960x^2+...,
which independently proves the limit and supplies the needed remainder order.
Writing only g approximately 1+20x without specifying an error small relative
to x does not by itself rigorously justify division by x followed by a limit.

Decimal quotient checks:
x=-0.01: 18.292719311245310976
x=-0.001: 19.820956648050575348485118976
x=+0.001: 20.180963368077455371525121024
x=+0.01: 21.899441999475713024
These approach 20 from the expected sides close to zero.
True g has its real zero at x=-1/2; its linear truncation has a spurious root
at -1/20, while quadratic 1+20x+180x^2 has discriminant -320 and no real root.
No global zero locations should be inferred from those local truncations.

PLANET QUIRK: MODEL MEANING, CALCULATION AND ROUNDING

Source locators: formula PDF p4/printed p3; definitions and linear expansion
PDF p5/printed p4; quadratic expansion PDF p6/printed p5; order comparison PDF
p7/printed p6. Figure 3 is an illustration and supplies no numerical scale.

Verified primary official-publisher reference:
OpenStax, University Physics Volume 3, section 5.3 Time Dilation, equations
5.1 and 5.2 and the Proper Time section:
https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/5-3-time-dilation
It derives Delta t=Delta tau/sqrt(1-(v/c)^2)=gamma Delta tau between inertial
frames. Delta tau is the interval between two events at the single moving
clock in its own rest frame; Delta t assigns times to those same events in
the ground frame, where they occur at distinct locations. Constant relative
velocity is the inertial-frame idealization. Gravity is excluded by this SR
model. Therefore map T=onboard proper interval Delta tau, T'=ground-frame
coordinate elapsed interval Delta t. The source's wristwatch description
needs a synchronized ground-frame time-assignment interpretation when the
satellite events occur elsewhere; it is not a comparison of light arrival
times and does not include signal travel delay or Doppler reception effects.

For q=(v/c)^2, gamma(q)=(1-q)^(-1/2), Taylor derivatives give
gamma(0)=1, gamma'(0)=1/2, gamma''(0)=3/4. Thus
  T'/T=gamma=1+q/2+3q^2/8+O(q^3).
Direct binomial coefficient: (-1/2)(-3/2)/2=3/8, with (-q)^2=q^2.
This is linear/quadratic in q, not in v. The first nonconstant term is order
v^2; the quadratic-in-q term is order v^4. At v=0, T'=T. For 0<|v|<c,
gamma>1, T'>T for positive elapsed T, consistent with a moving clock running
slowly relative to ground-frame time. The factor is even in v. Domain is
|v|<c or 0<=q<1; gamma diverges as q->1 from below.

Using the SOURCE'S stated numerical inputs in the SAME units,
v=4 km/s, c=3*10^5 km/s=300000 km/s, yields
  beta=v/c=1.3333333333333333333333333333...*10^-5,
  q=16/(9*10^10)=1.7777777777777777777777777777...*10^-10,
  q^2=3.1604938271604938271604938271...*10^-20,
  q/2=8.8888888888888888888888888888...*10^-11,
  3q^2/8=1.1851851851851851851851851851...*10^-20,
  5q^3/16=1.7558299039780521262002743484...*10^-30.
Full high-precision recomputation:
  gamma=1.000000000088888888900740740742496570644991921963159...
  gamma-1=8.88888889007407407424965706449919219631590794...*10^-11
  gamma-(1+q/2)=1.18518518536076817561030330742701905197...*10^-20
  gamma-(1+q/2+3q^2/8)=1.75582990425118122241833866789...*10^-30.
Rounding examples: q=1.78*10^-10 (3 significant figures), or q approximately
2*10^-10 (1 significant figure); q has order 10^-10. The source's approximate
10^-10 is acceptable as an order estimate. Substituting it as an actual
rounded q in q/2 produces 5*10^-11, which underestimates the source-input
increment 8.888...*10^-11 by 43.75%. Keep q unrounded through the calculation.
The source's order-10^-20 statement about q^2 is also reasonable as an order
estimate; the actual quadratic contribution includes coefficient 3/8.

If T=86400 s is an ONBOARD proper interval, T'-T is
  7.6800000010240000001517037... microseconds.
If instead T'=86400 s is the GROUND-frame elapsed interval, the onboard lag
T'-T=T'[1-sqrt(1-q)] is
  7.6800000003413333333636741... microseconds.
The linear approximation is 7.68 microseconds in either case, but precise
claims must identify which interval is fixed. Neither daily value appears in
the lecture; these are extra independent scale checks, not source quotations.
The source does not give T, so the directly available correction is fractional.

Remainder/tolerance statement: gamma'''(q)=15/8*(1-q)^(-7/2)>0. For fixed
0<=q<1, the linear factor underestimates gamma by approximately 3q^2/8, and the
quadratic factor underestimates by approximately 5q^3/16. A usable bound is
  0<=gamma-(1+q/2)<= [3/8]*(1-q)^(-5/2)*q^2,
and
  0<=gamma-(1+q/2+3q^2/8)<= [5/16]*(1-q)^(-7/2)*q^3.
Multiplying by the specified positive T turns factor error into time error.
This supports a tolerance-based decision without any current-clock assertion.

Cancellation check: ordinary binary floating arithmetic gives gamma-1 as
8.888889624358853e-11 for these inputs, losing accuracy in the small difference.
The algebraically stable expression is
  gamma-1=q/[sqrt(1-q)*(1+sqrt(1-q))].
It agrees with direct 80-digit Decimal subtraction to the configured precision.

GPS caveat independently confirmed by NIST's official account:
https://www.nist.gov/atomic-clocks/a-powerful-tool-for-science/putting-einstein-test
The cited NIST page gives about -7 microseconds/day from satellite motion,
about +45 microseconds/day from weaker gravity, and net +38 microseconds/day.
Thus this Quirk calculation isolates a kinematic correction; GPS clock
calibration involves both kinematic and gravitational effects. Its result
must not be presented as the full GPS clock correction or the net GPS sign.
