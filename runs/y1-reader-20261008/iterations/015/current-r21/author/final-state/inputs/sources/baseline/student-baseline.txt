Mathematics A: operational entry baseline

Scope: Cambridge OCR A Level Mathematics A, H240, checked on 7 October 2026. Mathematics B (MEI) is excluded. This is an explicit mathematical knowledge inventory for a learner who has completed the course. It describes curriculum exposure and usable prior knowledge, not a claim that every learner can perform every operation flawlessly. It may be used with supplied university notes to diagnose whether an explanation contains everything needed for a first-year bachelor's assessment. University material in the notes can extend it. The baseline itself supplies no unlisted university knowledge.

Current authority: H240 specification, version 3.1, August 2026 (https://www.ocr.org.uk/Images/308723-specification-accredited-a-level-gce-mathematics-a-h240.pdf). The operational mathematics below is newly written explanatory material, using conventional mathematical facts and original examples. It is not a reproduction of OCR's content tables. A separate research record identifies the documents actually inspected.

Prior arithmetic, geometry and mathematical language

Ordinary GCSE mathematics is available: signed arithmetic, fractions, decimals, percentages, ratio, proportion, powers, roots, standard form, rounding, significant figures, elementary estimation, rearranging formulae, substitution, expanding and factorising simple expressions, solving linear equations, coordinates, angle properties, similarity and scale factors, Pythagoras, right-triangle trigonometry, elementary circle geometry, areas and volumes of standard shapes, basic probability, and interpreting elementary tables and graphs. Operations must preserve units and dimensional consistency. A percentage change from a starting value \(A\) to an ending value \(B\) is \(100(B-A)/A\), for \(A\ne0\). A percentage multiplier \(1+r\) applied repeatedly produces \(A(1+r)^n\).

An expression has a value; an equation asserts equality for particular values; an identity holds for all admissible values. Variables range over a specified domain. A constant remains fixed within the problem. An integer is a whole number, possibly negative or zero. A rational number is \(p/q\), where \(p,q\) are integers and \(q\ne0\); an irrational real number cannot be written that way. Set notation includes membership, union, intersection, empty set and complements. \((a,b)\) excludes both endpoints, \([a,b]\) includes them, and mixed brackets include just the indicated endpoint. Write both branches of a solution such as \(x<1\) or \(x>4\).

Logical implication \(P\Rightarrow Q\) means that \(Q\) follows when \(P\) is true; it does not establish the converse. \(P\Leftrightarrow Q\) requires both directions. \(\equiv\) denotes an identity in algebraic work. Division by an expression assumes it is nonzero. Squaring an equation can introduce solutions; taking a root can lose a sign. Check proposed solutions against the original equation, its domain and the physical context.

Useful elementary mensuration is explicit: circle circumference \(2\pi r\) and area \(\pi r^2\); triangle area \(bh/2\) with perpendicular height \(h\); rectangle area \(ab\); trapezium area \((a+b)h/2\) for parallel sides \(a,b\); prism volume \(A\ell\) for constant cross-sectional area \(A\) and length \(\ell\); cylinder volume \(\pi r^2h\); cone volume \(\pi r^2h/3\); sphere volume \(4\pi r^3/3\) and surface area \(4\pi r^2\). Similarity with length factor \(k\) gives area factor \(k^2\) and volume factor \(k^3\). Angles in a triangle sum to \(180^\circ\); angles around a point sum to \(360^\circ\).

Proof and mathematical work

Deduction starts from definitions or given assumptions and maintains valid steps to the conclusion. Exhaustion proves a claim by considering every member of a finite list of cases. A counterexample disproves a universal statement: one admissible case violating it suffices. For example, the claim that every prime is odd fails at 2. A contradiction proof assumes the negation of the desired result and obtains an impossibility.

Two familiar contradiction structures are available. If \(\sqrt2=p/q\) in lowest integer terms, \(p^2=2q^2\) makes \(p\) even, and then makes \(q\) even, contradicting lowest terms. If the only primes were \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\), the integer \(p_1\cdots p_n+1\) would have a prime divisor different from each listed prime, contradicting completeness. An algebraic divisibility proof should use general integers and an explicit factor or remainder. Numerical examples can suggest a pattern but do not prove an unrestricted claim.

Choose a method rather than relying only on a named template. Connect graphs, equations and words; interpret final values in context; explain assumptions and whether they remain plausible. A model can omit influences or idealise behaviour. Test its outputs against the situation, state where the model is useful, and refine assumptions when the predictions fail.

Algebra, functions and coordinate geometry

For a positive base, \(x^a x^b=x^{a+b}\), \(x^a/x^b=x^{a-b}\), \((x^a)^b=x^{ab}\), \(x^0=1\), \(x^{-a}=1/x^a\), and \(x^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{x^m}\). Real negative bases require attention to whether the relevant root exists. \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\), not always \(x\). Simplify surds by extracting square factors. Rationalise \(1/\sqrt a\) by multiplying numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt a\), and \(1/(a+\sqrt b)\) by the conjugate \(a-\sqrt b\), when denominators permit it.

For \(ax^2+bx+c=0\), \(a\ne0\),
\[
x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}.
\]
The discriminant \(b^2-4ac\) gives two distinct real roots when positive, a repeated real root when zero, and no real roots when negative. Completing the square gives
\[
ax^2+bx+c=a\left(x+\frac b{2a}\right)^2+c-\frac{b^2}{4a},
\]
which identifies the vertex and axis of symmetry. Substitute \(u=x^2\), \(e^x\), a trigonometric expression or another appropriate expression into equations quadratic in \(u\), then recover admissible \(x\). Solve simultaneous equations by substitution or elimination, including a line with a quadratic curve. Graphically, solutions are intersections.

For an inequality, reverse the inequality when multiplying or dividing by a negative number. For a factored polynomial inequality, place roots on a number line, determine the sign between them, and retain the required intervals and endpoints. A zero of even multiplicity usually touches the axis without a sign change; odd multiplicity crosses it. For rational expressions, factor and cancel common factors while keeping excluded denominator zeros. Polynomial division produces quotient and remainder. The factor theorem gives \(f(a)=0\) exactly when \(x-a\) is a factor. Repeated use can factor and solve cubic or quartic equations when suitable factors are available.

The modulus is \(|x|=x\) for \(x\ge0\), \(-x\) for \(x<0\). For \(c\ge0\), \(|u|=c\) gives \(u=\pm c\); \(|u|<c\) gives \(-c<u<c\); \(|u|>c\) gives \(u<-c\) or \(u>c\). A negative right side needs separate thought. \(y=|ax+b|\) is the corresponding straight line with its negative part reflected above the axis. Solve modulus comparisons by cases or graph intersections.

A function assigns exactly one output to each input in its domain. Its range is the set of resulting outputs. A function can be many-to-one; a one-to-many relation is not a function. A one-to-one function has an inverse on its range. Restricting a domain can make an inverse possible, for example \(x^2\) on \(x\ge0\). To find \(f^{-1}\), set \(y=f(x)\), solve for \(x\), and exchange the variables; its graph reflects the original in \(y=x\). Inverse functions do not mean reciprocals. Composition \((f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))\) is defined only when \(g(x)\) lies in the domain of \(f\). \(f^2(x)\) in composition notation means \(f(f(x))\), so distinguish it from \([f(x)]^2\).

Relative to \(y=f(x)\): \(f(x)+a\) moves the graph vertically by \(a\); \(f(x-a)\) moves it right by \(a\); \(af(x)\) scales vertical coordinates by \(a\); \(f(ax)\) scales horizontal coordinates by \(1/a\), for \(a\ne0\). Negative factors include reflection. Compound transformations must be applied in a declared order or obtained directly from the transformed equation. Sketches show intercepts, stationary points, asymptotes, endpoint restrictions and large-\(|x|\) behaviour where relevant. A polynomial's leading term governs its large-\(|x|\) behaviour. Direct proportion \(y=kx\) gives a straight line through the origin, inverse proportion \(y=k/x\) a reciprocal graph, and other power relationships \(y=kx^n\) their corresponding power graphs. Graphs of \(a/x\) and \(a/x^2\) have vertical asymptote \(x=0\) and horizontal asymptote \(y=0\), for nonzero \(a\). A horizontal asymptote describes limiting behaviour; it need not be a forbidden crossing for a general function.

Partial fractions use undetermined coefficients or substitution after clearing the denominator. Distinct linear factors give terms such as \(A/(x-a)+B/(x-b)\); a squared factor requires both \(A/(x-a)\) and \(B/(x-a)^2\). Three distinct linear factors may require three fractions. If the original fraction is improper, polynomial division separates its polynomial part before decomposition. These decompositions support integrations and power expansions.

A line has forms \(y=mx+c\), \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\), or \(ax+by+c=0\). Between two points, \(m=(y_2-y_1)/(x_2-x_1)\) when the denominator is nonzero. A vertical line has equation \(x=k\). Parallel nonvertical lines have equal gradients; perpendicular ones have \(m_1m_2=-1\); horizontal and vertical lines form the exceptional pair. Midpoint coordinates are averages. Distance is \(\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\).

A circle with centre \((a,b)\) and radius \(r\) has \((x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2\). Complete squares to identify the centre and radius from an expanded equation. The radius is perpendicular to a tangent at contact. A perpendicular from the centre bisects a chord; an angle subtended by a diameter at the circumference is a right angle. To test intersections of a line and circle, substitute the line into the circle and inspect the resulting roots; a repeated root represents tangency. For two circles, subtraction of their equations can yield the line containing their intersections.

Parametric coordinates \(x=f(t),y=g(t)\) define a point by a parameter. Eliminate \(t\) when possible, but preserve the parameter restriction and hence the actual part of the Cartesian curve. For example, \(x=\cos t,y=\sin t\) gives \(x^2+y^2=1\), while the interval of \(t\) determines which arc is traced and its direction. Parametric descriptions can model position and related rates.

Sequences, series and binomial expansion

A sequence is an ordered list; a series is a sum of terms. A rule \(u_n=f(n)\) gives a term directly; \(u_{n+1}=g(u_n)\), with a starting value, generates terms recursively. A strictly increasing sequence has \(u_{n+1}>u_n\), a strictly decreasing one has \(u_{n+1}<u_n\), and a periodic sequence repeats after a fixed number of terms. Convergence means approaching a finite limiting value; a divergent sequence lacks such a limit. Investigate behaviour from terms, algebra and graphs. A fixed limiting value \(L\), when it exists and the recurrence permits passage to a limit, satisfies \(L=g(L)\); a solution of that equation alone does not prove convergence.

\(\sum_{r=1}^n u_r\) means adding the indexed terms from 1 to \(n\). Index limits matter. An arithmetic progression with first term \(a\) and common difference \(d\) has
\[
u_n=a+(n-1)d,\qquad S_n=\frac n2\{2a+(n-1)d\}=\frac n2(a+u_n).
\]
A geometric progression with first term \(a\) and ratio \(r\) has
\[
u_n=ar^{n-1},\qquad S_n=\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}\quad(r\ne1).
\]
If \(r=1\), \(S_n=na\). For \(|r|<1\), \(S_\infty=a/(1-r)\). Positive or negative \(r\) and the sign of \(a\) affect monotonicity. Use these models for repeated growth, decay, repayments or accumulated contributions, making the timing of payments explicit. Solving a time inequality often needs logarithms and then a whole-number ceiling or floor, with the original inequality checked.

For nonnegative integer \(n\),
\[
(a+bx)^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr a^{n-r}(bx)^r,\qquad
\binom nr=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!},\quad0!=1.
\]
Pascal's triangle obtains an interior coefficient by adding the two above it. Match powers carefully when asking for a coefficient. For rational \(n\), the expansion starts
\[
(1+u)^n=1+nu+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}u^2+\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{6}u^3+\cdots,
\]
with \(|u|<1\) for the infinite expansion. First factor \((a+bx)^n=a^n(1+bx/a)^n\), where appropriate; its usable interval is \(|bx/a|<1\). A finite truncation is an approximation, especially useful when \(|u|\) is small. Partial fractions may produce several expansions whose validity intervals must all hold. Binomial coefficients also count placements of successes in binomial probability calculations.

Trigonometry

On the unit circle, cosine is the horizontal coordinate and sine the vertical coordinate; \(\tan\theta=\sin\theta/\cos\theta\), wherever \(\cos\theta\ne0\). Sine and cosine have period \(2\pi\), tangent period \(\pi\). \(\sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta\), \(\cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta\). Their signs come from the quadrant. Exact sine/cosine values at \(0,\pi/6,\pi/4,\pi/3,\pi/2\) are respectively \((0,1),(1/2,\sqrt3/2),(\sqrt2/2,\sqrt2/2),(\sqrt3/2,1/2),(1,0)\); extend by symmetry and periodicity. Tangent is their ratio and is undefined where cosine vanishes.

Radians satisfy \(\pi\text{ radians}=180^\circ\). For a circle, arc length \(s=r\theta\) and sector area \(A=r^2\theta/2\) require \(\theta\) in radians. For a triangle, sides \(a,b,c\) opposite angles \(A,B,C\) satisfy \(a/\sin A=b/\sin B=c/\sin C\), \(a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A\), and area \(bc\sin A/2\). With the sine rule, a sine value can correspond to an acute angle or its supplementary obtuse angle; check both against the triangle information. Bearings measure clockwise from north and need the geometry interpreted before choosing an angle.

For a small angle in radians, \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\), \(\tan\theta\approx\theta\), and \(\cos\theta\approx1-\theta^2/2\). Keep the requested order consistently when substituting and simplifying; these are approximations near zero, not identities for arbitrary angles.

Reciprocal functions are \(\sec x=1/\cos x\), \(\cosec x=1/\sin x\), \(\cot x=\cos x/\sin x\). Their denominator zeros give excluded inputs and vertical asymptotes. Secant and cosecant have range \(( -\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty)\). Cotangent takes all real values on each suitable open branch. Principal inverse functions have \(\arcsin:[-1,1]\to[-\pi/2,\pi/2]\), \(\arccos:[-1,1]\to[0,\pi]\), and \(\arctan:\mathbb R\to(-\pi/2,\pi/2)\). Their graphs reflect the corresponding restricted original graph in \(y=x\). The notation \(\sin^{-1}x\) means arcsine, not cosecant.

The identities
\[
\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1,\quad1+\tan^2x=\sec^2x,\quad1+\cot^2x=\cosec^2x
\]
hold on their relevant domains. Angle formulae are
\[
\sin(A\pm B)=\sin A\cos B\pm\cos A\sin B,
\]
\[
\cos(A\pm B)=\cos A\cos B\mp\sin A\sin B,
\qquad
\tan(A\pm B)=\frac{\tan A\pm\tan B}{1\mp\tan A\tan B},
\]
where the tangent expression is defined. Thus \(\sin2x=2\sin x\cos x\), \(\cos2x=\cos^2x-\sin^2x=1-2\sin^2x=2\cos^2x-1\), and \(\tan2x=2\tan x/(1-\tan^2x)\) when defined. Addition formulae can be justified geometrically by decomposing components after a rotation, with signs tracked by the chosen angles.

Convert \(a\cos x+b\sin x\) to \(R\cos(x-\alpha)\), with \(R=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), \(R\cos\alpha=a\), \(R\sin\alpha=b\), and a quadrant-consistent \(\alpha\); an equivalent sine form is possible. This identifies amplitude, phase, extrema and solutions. A model \(D+A\sin(\omega t+\phi)\) has midline \(D\), amplitude \(|A|\), period \(2\pi/|\omega|\) when \(\omega\ne0\), and phase controlled by \(\phi\).

To solve a trigonometric equation, use identities or a substitution to obtain a basic trigonometric value, then find every solution in the requested interval. For example, \(\sin x=s\) gives \(x=\alpha+2k\pi\) or \(x=\pi-\alpha+2k\pi\), where \(\alpha=\arcsin s\); \(\cos x=c\) gives \(x=\pm\arccos c+2k\pi\); \(\tan x=t\) gives \(x=\arctan t+k\pi\). For a multiple angle, determine its wider transformed interval before filtering back. Dividing by a trigonometric factor can discard roots at its zeros. A proof of an identity may transform one side into the other using known identities, or show both equal a third expression, while respecting denominators.

Exponentials and logarithms

For \(a>0\), \(a^x\) is positive; for \(a>1\) it increases, and for \(0<a<1\) it decreases. \(e^x\) is the exponential with derivative equal to itself. For \(a>0,a\ne1,x>0\), \(\log_a x=y\) means \(a^y=x\); \(\ln x=\log_e x\). Logarithms and the corresponding exponentials are inverse functions. \(\ln1=0\), \(\ln e=1\), \(e^{\ln x}=x\) for \(x>0\), and \(\ln(e^x)=x\) for real \(x\).

For positive \(u,v\), \(\log_a(uv)=\log_a u+\log_a v\), \(\log_a(u/v)=\log_a u-\log_a v\), and \(\log_a(u^k)=k\log_a u\) when the expressions are defined. There is no corresponding rule replacing \(\log(u+v)\) by \(\log u+\log v\). To solve \(a^x=b\), take a logarithm of both sides: \(x\ln a=\ln b\), requiring \(b>0\) and \(a\ne1\). Log inequalities need monotonicity considered, especially for bases below 1.

For \(y=Ax^n\) with positive \(x,y,A\), \(\ln y=\ln A+n\ln x\); a straight plot of \(\ln y\) against \(\ln x\) has gradient \(n\) and intercept \(\ln A\). For \(y=Kb^x\), \(\ln y=\ln K+x\ln b\). For \(y=Ae^{kt}\), the rate is \(ky\); \(k>0\) gives growth, \(k<0\) decay. A doubling time is \(\ln2/k\) for \(k>0\), and a half-life is \(\ln2/(-k)\) for \(k<0\). A constant proportional growth-rate model can be unreasonable indefinitely, for example under finite resources.

Differentiation and its applications

The derivative is a local rate of change and tangent gradient:
\[
f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h
\]
where the limit exists. The gradient of a chord is an average rate; the limit gives the instantaneous rate. Use \(f'(x)\), \(dy/dx\), \(f''(x)\) and \(d^2y/dx^2\) consistently. First-principles arguments for \(x,x^2,x^3,x^4\) expand the difference, cancel, divide by \(h\), and pass to the limit. For sine and cosine, use the angle-addition formulae together with \(\sin h/h\to1\) and \((\cos h-1)/h\to0\), with radians, to obtain derivatives \(\cos x\) and \(-\sin x\).

Core derivatives, on intervals where the functions are differentiable, are
\[
\frac d{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1},\quad
\frac d{dx}e^{kx}=ke^{kx},\quad
\frac d{dx}a^{kx}=k\ln(a)a^{kx},\quad
\frac d{dx}\ln x=\frac1x\ (x>0),
\]
\[
\frac d{dx}\sin(kx)=k\cos(kx),\quad
\frac d{dx}\cos(kx)=-k\sin(kx),\quad
\frac d{dx}\tan(kx)=k\sec^2(kx).
\]
Trig derivative formulae require radians. Constants differentiate to zero; differentiation distributes over sums and constant multiples. Reciprocal trig derivatives, also usable from their displayed forms, are \((\sec x)'=\sec x\tan x\), \((\cot x)'=-\cosec^2x\), \((\cosec x)'=-\cosec x\cot x\).

For differentiable \(u,v\),
\[
(uv)'=u'v+uv',\qquad
\left(\frac uv\right)'=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}\ (v\ne0),\qquad
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}.
\]
The chain rule differentiates an outer function and multiplies by the derivative of its inner input. For connected rates, \(dy/dt=(dy/dx)(dx/dt)\). The inverse-rate relation \(dx/dy=1/(dy/dx)\) applies where the local inverse and nonzero derivative permit it.

At \((x_0,y_0)\), a nonvertical tangent has \(y-y_0=f'(x_0)(x-x_0)\). Its normal has gradient \(-1/f'(x_0)\) when that derivative is nonzero. A horizontal tangent has a vertical normal. Stationary points satisfy \(f'=0\). A positive-to-negative change of \(f'\) gives a local maximum; negative-to-positive gives a local minimum. At a stationary point, \(f''>0\) gives a minimum and \(f''<0\) a maximum; \(f''=0\) is inconclusive. Increasing and decreasing intervals follow from the sign of \(f'\). To sketch a derivative graph from a curve, place zeros at horizontal tangents, positive values over increasing sections and negative values over decreasing sections; compare slope steepness to estimate derivative size and track whether the gradient increases or decreases.

Positive \(f''\) gives a convex section with increasing gradient; negative \(f''\) gives a concave section. An inflection requires a change in concavity, not merely a zero second derivative. In ordinary smooth examples, test the sign of \(f''\) either side of a candidate root. An inflection is stationary only if \(f'=0\) there as well. For optimisation, express the target in a suitable variable, identify the valid domain, find candidate stationary values and check endpoints or other domain restrictions as necessary.

For an implicit relation, differentiate each side with respect to \(x\), treating \(y\) as a function of \(x\); for example \(x^2+y^2=r^2\) yields \(2x+2y\,dy/dx=0\). For \(x=x(t),y=y(t)\),
\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt},
\]
provided \(dx/dt\ne0\). These first derivatives support tangent and normal questions and rates in a model. A differential equation can be constructed from a rate statement, for example proportional increase means \(dy/dt=ky\), with \(k\) having the required sign and units.

Integration and elementary differential equations

An indefinite integral gives a family of antiderivatives: if \(F'=f\), then \(\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C\). A condition such as \(F(a)=b\) determines \(C\). For a suitably continuous integrand, the fundamental theorem gives \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)\). Integration can also be understood as the limit of rectangle sums, \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\Delta x\), for a partition whose widths shrink to zero in the usual well-behaved cases.

Standard antiderivatives include
\[
\int x^n dx=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\ (n\ne-1),\quad
\int\frac1x dx=\ln|x|+C\ (x\ne0),\quad
\int e^{kx}dx=\frac{e^{kx}}k+C\ (k\ne0),
\]
\[
\int\sin(kx)dx=-\frac{\cos(kx)}k+C,\qquad
\int\cos(kx)dx=\frac{\sin(kx)}k+C\quad(k\ne0).
\]
Constants, sums and differences integrate term by term. Use trig identities first when useful, such as \(\cos^2x=(1+\cos2x)/2\). Reverse the chain rule:
\[
\int f'(x)[f(x)]^n dx=\frac{[f(x)]^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\ (n\ne-1),\qquad
\int\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}dx=\ln|f(x)|+C
\]
on intervals where defined. For example, \(\int\tan x\,dx=-\ln|\cos x|+C\).

With substitution \(u=g(x)\), replace the differential consistently, \(du=g'(x)dx\), and rewrite the whole integral in \(u\). For a definite integral, transform both limits or return fully to \(x\) before substituting the old limits. A suitable one-step substitution may remove a root, a composite input or a reciprocal derivative pattern. Integration by parts reverses the product rule:
\[
\int u\frac{dv}{dx}\,dx=uv-\int v\frac{du}{dx}\,dx.
\]
Choose \(u\) so differentiating it simplifies the remaining integrand, and choose an integrable \(dv/dx\). It can be applied more than once; \(\int\ln x\,dx=x\ln x-x+C\) for \(x>0\). Partial fractions reduce suitable rational integrands to logarithms and powers of linear factors.

A definite integral is signed. Geometric area requires nonnegative contributions: split at crossings of the axis and negate a below-axis integral as necessary, or integrate the upper curve minus the lower curve between their intersections. For a parametric curve, \(\int y\,dx=\int y(t)x'(t)\,dt\); limits, direction of traversal and sign must agree with the requested area. A time integral of velocity is displacement; distance travelled uses the speed and may need splitting where velocity changes sign.

A first-order equation \(dy/dx=p(x)q(y)\) is separable where \(q(y)\ne0\):
\[
\int\frac{1}{q(y)}dy=\int p(x)dx+C.
\]
An initial condition determines the constant. Dividing by \(q(y)\) may remove constant solutions at its zeros, so check those separately. For \(dy/dt=ky\), \(y=Ae^{kt}\), including \(y=0\). Solve directly by integration when the derivative depends only on the independent variable. Interpret the solution's positivity, units, long-term limit, growth or decay and the range in which the initial model remains plausible.

Numerical methods

If a continuous \(f\) has opposite signs at \(a,b\), it has at least one root between them. Repeated narrowing or bisection locates it more accurately. A sign change across a discontinuity or vertical asymptote need not identify a root; a tangent root can exist without a sign change. To justify a decimal approximation, bracket the root with values that lie inside the corresponding rounding interval rather than merely displaying two nearly equal iterates.

Rearrange an equation as \(x=g(x)\), choose a starting value and compute \(x_{n+1}=g(x_n)\). Cobweb or staircase diagrams alternate between \(y=g(x)\) and \(y=x\). Near a fixed point \(a\), \(|g'(a)|<1\) supports local convergence for a sufficiently close starting value; a negative derivative can cause alternation, and a positive derivative can cause approach from one side. Other rearrangements or starts can diverge, cycle, leave the domain or converge to a different solution. A limiting iterate must still solve the original equation.

Newton-Raphson uses a tangent intercept:
\[
x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
\]
It requires a defined nonzero denominator. A stationary starting point makes this step unavailable, and unfavourable starts can diverge or switch roots. Record iterates and retain internal precision; verify the final accuracy appropriately.

For equal subintervals of width \(h=(b-a)/n\), with \(y_i=f(a+ih)\), the trapezium approximation is
\[
\int_a^b f(x)dx\approx\frac h2\left(y_0+y_n+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}y_i\right).
\]
A convex curve lies below its chords and hence gives an overestimate of the signed integral; a concave curve gives an underestimate. If concavity changes, a single global conclusion needs justification. For a nonnegative monotone curve, left/right endpoint rectangle sums produce lower/upper area bounds in the order determined by whether it is increasing or decreasing. Unequal subintervals can be handled by summing individual trapezia. These methods can solve contextual equations or estimate areas when symbolic methods are unavailable.

Vectors

A scalar has magnitude only; a vector also has direction. Vectors can be written in components, as column vectors, or with basis symbols: \(a_x\mathbf i+a_y\mathbf j\), extended to \(a_z\mathbf k\) in three dimensions. Add and subtract corresponding components; multiply every component by a scalar. \(\overrightarrow{AB}=\mathbf b-\mathbf a\), where \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b\) are position vectors. Parallel nonzero vectors are scalar multiples. Equal vectors have the same components even at different positions.

The magnitude is \(\sqrt{a_x^2+a_y^2}\) in two dimensions or \(\sqrt{a_x^2+a_y^2+a_z^2}\) in three; a unit vector in the nonzero direction \(\mathbf a\) is \(\mathbf a/|\mathbf a|\). The distance between points is \(|\mathbf b-\mathbf a|\). In two dimensions, magnitude \(r\) and direction \(\theta\) from the positive horizontal axis give \((r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\). Recover direction using a quadrant-correct inverse tangent, with special attention to zero components. The zero vector has no unique direction.

Geometric vector reasoning includes displacement paths, collinearity, equal or parallel sides, ratios along line segments and identifying a resultant. Apply the same component operations to forces, position, velocity and acceleration. Three-dimensional component and distance calculations are available; three-dimensional angles, scalar products, cross products, line/plane vector equations and matrix methods require a separate baseline or explicit new teaching.

Statistics: data, probability and distributions

A population is the whole group under investigation; a sample is the observed subset. In simple random sampling every sample of the specified size has the same selection chance. Opportunity sampling uses conveniently available units and can overrepresent accessible cases. Understand systematic selection (regular interval after a start), stratified selection (sample within subgroups), cluster selection (choose groups), and quota selection (fill prescribed subgroup counts), and critique their suitability in context. A large sample can reduce random variation but cannot automatically remove systematic bias. A sampling frame must match the target population; nonresponse and selection methods can distort conclusions. Different random samples need not give the same result.

Interpret vertical line charts, dot plots, bar charts, stem-and-leaf plots, box plots, cumulative frequency graphs and histograms, including unfamiliar representations. In a histogram, frequency is proportional to area, so frequency density is frequency divided by class width. Use consistent class boundaries. On a cumulative graph, read a quantile from its cumulative position. A box plot identifies median, quartiles, spread and potentially marked outliers; it does not show every observation.

For observations \(x_i\), \(\bar x=\sum x_i/n\); for frequencies \(f_i\), \(\bar x=\sum f_ix_i/\sum f_i\). A median separates the ordered observations, mode is a most common value, and \(\mathrm{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1\). The descriptive variance and standard deviation are
\[
\sigma^2=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}{n}=\frac{\sum x_i^2}{n}-\bar x^2,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}.
\]
For frequency data, replace each sum by its frequency-weighted sum and \(n\) by \(\sum f_i\). With grouped data, use midpoints to obtain estimates; the actual observations within the classes are unknown. Understand calculator symbols distinguishing denominator \(n\) from denominator \(n-1\). Compare centres and spreads in the units and context of the data. Translating every value by \(b\) shifts the mean by \(b\) but leaves spread unchanged; scaling by \(a\) scales the mean by \(a\), variance by \(a^2\), and standard deviation by \(|a|\).

Potential outliers can be flagged below \(Q_1-1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}\), above \(Q_3+1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}\), or farther than two standard deviations from the mean under a stated rule. A flag does not prove an error. Examine missing entries, units, miscoding, duplicates and genuine exceptional values before deciding how to clean data. State the effect of exclusion or correction when drawing conclusions.

A scatter plot can show a positive or negative association, nonlinear structure, weak association, clusters or distinct subpopulations. A regression line summarises a predictive relationship; use the provided equation with the explanatory input variable. Interpolation within the observed range is usually more defensible than extrapolation. Correlation does not prove a causal relationship: confounding variables, common influences and selection can explain it.

For an event \(A\), \(0\le P(A)\le1\), \(P(A^c)=1-P(A)\). Union means at least one event, intersection means both. Mutually exclusive events have \(P(A\cap B)=0\). Independent events satisfy \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\); neither property implies the other, except trivial zero-probability cases. General rules are
\[
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B),\qquad
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\quad(P(B)>0),
\]
\[
P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B)=P(A)P(B\mid A).
\]
Use a tree, Venn diagram, sample space or two-way table to represent cases. Along a tree path multiply conditional probabilities; add mutually exclusive paths. Sampling without replacement changes subsequent fractions, unless an explicit large-population approximation applies. Conditional probability restricts the reference population to the given event. An elementary reverse conditional can be obtained by constructing and counting a joint table or branches; a separately memorised Bayes theorem is not part of this H240 grant.

A finite discrete random variable has a table or formula for probabilities of its possible values. Every probability is nonnegative and their sum is 1. Determine an unknown normalising constant with that sum, combine requested disjoint values, or use complements. Independent repeated draws multiply their probabilities; enumerate admissible outcomes carefully for sums or order restrictions. For elementary counting, multiply the number of choices at successive stages when each branch has those choices, or add mutually exclusive branches. Arranging \(n\) distinct items gives \(n!\) possibilities; selecting and ordering \(r\) of them gives \(n!/(n-r)!\); dividing by the \(r!\) orders of a fixed selection gives \(\binom nr\) unordered selections. Adjust when repeats or restrictions change the branch counts.

\(X\sim\mathrm{B}(n,p)\) counts successes in \(n\) independent trials, each with the same success probability \(p\) and only success/failure classifications. Its support is \(0,1,\ldots,n\), and
\[
P(X=r)=\binom nr p^r(1-p)^{n-r},\qquad
\mu=np,\quad\sigma^2=np(1-p).
\]
Distinguish a fixed trial count, defined outcomes and independence from a modelling assumption that the chance is constant. In a real context, explain why weather, serial dependence, learning or heterogeneous individuals may break the model. Calculate exact and cumulative binomial probabilities with a calculator or summation. For integer \(r\), \(P(X\ge r)=1-P(X\le r-1)\) and \(P(X>r)=1-P(X\le r)\). The mean and variance identify a possible matched normal model for large \(n\); H240 does not demand numerical normal-approximation probability calculations.

\(X\sim\mathrm N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) is a continuous, symmetric bell-shaped model with mean \(\mu\) and positive standard deviation \(\sigma\). A probability is an area under its curve; \(P(X=x)=0\), so strict and non-strict endpoints give the same probability. Standardise using \(Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma\sim\mathrm N(0,1)\). If \(\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)\), then
\[
P(a<X<b)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right).
\]
Use calculator cumulative and inverse-normal functions, or supplied percentile values. To find an unknown boundary or parameter, equate its standardised value to the appropriate quantile. The supplied H240 normal percentiles can be carried explicitly: probabilities \(p=0.75,0.90,0.95,0.975,0.99,0.995,0.9975,0.999,0.9995\) have \(\Phi^{-1}(p)\) approximately \(0.674,1.282,1.645,1.960,2.326,2.576,2.807,3.090,3.291\), respectively. Symmetry gives \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\). Roughly 68%, 95% and 99.7% lie within one, two and three standard deviations; curve inflections occur at \(\mu\pm\sigma\). A symmetric-looking histogram can motivate a normal model, while clear skew, multiple populations or restrictive physical bounds can undermine it. Other normal probabilities or PMCC critical values require the relevant calculator evaluation, table, p-value or numerical result to be provided by the assessment environment or notes; exposure to their use does not imply memorising a full numerical table.

Statistical hypothesis tests

State a null hypothesis \(H_0\) about a population parameter, and an alternative \(H_1\) describing the proposed difference or direction. Define the parameter in context. The alternative determines one or two tails before looking at the result. A significance level \(\alpha\) controls the chance of rejecting a true null under the test model; a discrete test's actual rejection probability can be smaller than its stated maximum. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. A p-value is the probability, calculated under the null model, of a result at least as extreme in the relevant direction(s). Reject when the result belongs to the chosen critical region, or when an appropriate p-value is no larger than the level. Failure to reject does not establish equality or prove the null.

For a binomial proportion test, \(H_0:p=p_0\) supplies \(X\sim\mathrm B(n,p_0)\). For \(H_1:p>p_0\) use the upper probability \(P(X\ge x_{\rm obs})\); for \(H_1:p<p_0\) use \(P(X\le x_{\rm obs})\). Obtain a boundary by finding the first upper, or last lower, count whose relevant tail probability is at most \(\alpha\). For a conventional two-tail test, allocate \(\alpha/2\) to each tail and choose integer critical values meeting those tail limits; do not assume the two actual discrete tail probabilities equal \(\alpha/2\). Report the sampling/model assumptions and evidence for the population claim in words tied to the stated level.

For independent observations from a normal population with known, supplied or assumed variance \(\sigma^2\),
\[
\bar X\sim\mathrm N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right),\qquad
Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}
\]
has the standard normal distribution under \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\). Use the standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt n\), not the individual-observation standard deviation. Compare \(Z\) with a one-tail quantile \(z_{1-\alpha}\), a lower-tail quantile, or \(\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\) for a two-tail test. At 5%, the common upper one-tail cutoff is about 1.645 and the two-tail magnitudes about 1.960. Equivalent critical bounds can be expressed in units of \(\bar X\). Normality of the population is part of this H240 test model; an unrestricted central-limit-theorem argument for non-normal populations is an additional topic.

Pearson's product-moment coefficient \(r\in[-1,1]\) measures linear association, with its sign giving direction and \(|r|\) giving closeness to a straight line. Zero indicates no linear correlation, while a nonlinear relation can still exist. A hypothesis test uses the population correlation parameter \(\rho\), commonly \(H_0:\rho=0\), and an appropriate directional or two-sided alternative. Use the supplied \(r\), p-value or critical-value table for the sample size and level. The usual H240 inference assumes observations from a bivariate normal population. A significant result is evidence of population linear association, without proving causation. Calculation of \(r\) itself need not be recalled in this baseline.

The current H240 large-data-set context is 2001/2011 census age structure and methods of travel to work for 348 aligned local authorities in England and Wales. Regions and geography codes identify areas. Travel counts concern employed people and their usual main travel method; 2011 retains a separate non-employed category. Age bands have unequal widths, and the final age band is open-ended. Use the right denominator for a proportion, compare corresponding categories, and account for different population sizes. Aggregate counts do not track individual residents: similar counts ten years apart need not imply no migration. The official dataset remains historical; it is not a source of current demographics. Exact cell values need not be memorised. Currently linked OCR H230/H240 data workbook (https://www.ocr.org.uk/Images/308727-units-h230-and-h240-large-data-set-lds-sample-assessment-material.xlsx).

Mechanics

Length, time and mass use metres, seconds and kilograms. Velocity uses \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), acceleration \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), force and weight newtons, and moment \(\mathrm{N\,m}\). Position, displacement, velocity, acceleration and force are vectors; distance travelled and speed are scalars. In one dimension, signed velocity can be negative while speed is \(|v|\). Displacement is final minus initial position, and total distance can exceed its magnitude. State a coordinate direction before assigning signs.

For constant acceleration \(a\) in a straight line, \(u\) is initial velocity, \(v\) final velocity, \(s\) displacement over elapsed time \(t\):
\[
v=u+at,\quad s=ut+\tfrac12at^2,\quad s=\tfrac12(u+v)t,
\quad v^2=u^2+2as,\quad s=vt-\tfrac12at^2.
\]
These formulae need constant acceleration over the interval. They follow by integrating constant acceleration, by displacement as area under a straight velocity-time graph, and by eliminating \(t\). In two dimensions use \(\mathbf v=\mathbf u+\mathbf a t\), \(\mathbf s=\mathbf u t+\tfrac12\mathbf a t^2\), \(\mathbf s=(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)t/2\), and \(\mathbf s=\mathbf v t-\tfrac12\mathbf a t^2\), applied consistently to components. Position is \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r_0+\mathbf s\).

For variable acceleration,
\[
\mathbf v=\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt},\qquad
\mathbf a=\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}=\frac{d^2\mathbf r}{dt^2},\qquad
\mathbf r=\int\mathbf v\,dt,\quad\mathbf v=\int\mathbf a\,dt,
\]
where vector differentiation/integration is componentwise and initial conditions determine constant vectors. A displacement-time gradient is velocity; a velocity-time gradient is acceleration; signed area under velocity-time is displacement. Turning points in one-dimensional travel require checking where velocity changes sign. In two dimensions speed is \(\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}\), and the velocity direction describes motion, while acceleration describes change in velocity.

For a projectile with only uniform gravity, choose horizontal \(x\), upward \(y\), and \(\mathbf a=(0,-g)\). From \((x_0,y_0)\) with speed \(u\) at launch angle \(\theta\),
\[
x=x_0+u\cos\theta\,t,\quad
y=y_0+u\sin\theta\,t-\tfrac12gt^2,
\quad v_x=u\cos\theta,\quad v_y=u\sin\theta-gt.
\]
If \(u\cos\theta\ne0\), eliminating time gives
\[
y=y_0+(x-x_0)\tan\theta-
\frac{g(x-x_0)^2}{2u^2\cos^2\theta}.
\]
The maximum occurs when \(v_y=0\), giving height rise \(u^2\sin^2\theta/(2g)\) for an upward launch. For landing at launch height, positive flight time is \(2u\sin\theta/g\) and horizontal range \(u^2\sin2\theta/g\), assuming a physically appropriate launch. A different landing height needs the vertical equation solved, selecting the positive relevant time. Determine velocity and its direction at any given time or position. The model treats the object as a particle, ignores air resistance and assumes constant gravity; this explains its limitations.

Draw a separate force diagram for each body. Newton's first law says that zero resultant external force means constant velocity, including rest. For constant mass, Newton's second law is \(\sum\mathbf F=m\mathbf a\); \(\mathbf F\) denotes the resultant, not automatically one chosen force. Apply it to components. Newton's third-law pairs are equal and opposite interactions acting on different bodies, so they do not cancel on one body's diagram. Weight is \(mg\) downward; mass is not weight. Near Earth's surface use the problem's \(g\), commonly \(9.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\); gravity is not the same everywhere.

A contact reaction is perpendicular to the surface. On a horizontal surface \(R=mg\) only when other vertical forces and vertical acceleration allow that balance. A smooth surface has no tangential friction. A rough contact has normal reaction \(R\) plus tangential friction \(F\), whose resultant magnitude is \(\sqrt{R^2+F^2}\). A supplied resistance force can be included in a straight-line Newton problem, for example upward air resistance for a falling particle; this is distinct from assuming an unprovided drag law. A light string has negligible mass, an inextensible string fixes its length, and a taut string transmits tension. A smooth light pulley supports the same tension on the two sides in the ideal model. Connected bodies constrained by a taut inextensible string have compatible accelerations. Write one equation for each body, with signs chosen consistently, and solve for tension and acceleration. Alternatively a combined-system equation cancels internal forces but cannot alone recover the tension. Loss of contact requires \(R=0\); a string cannot push and a negative tension may reveal failure of the assumed model.

For an incline at angle \(\alpha\), weight resolves to \(mg\sin\alpha\) down the slope and \(mg\cos\alpha\) into it. Additional forces can alter \(R\), so \(R=mg\cos\alpha\) applies only when the remaining normal-force balance permits it. Equilibrium of a particle requires zero resultant in two independent directions. Resolve parallel/perpendicular to a plane or choose axes simplifying the forces; a closed polygon of forces gives another representation of balance. A time-dependent force in a plane can be used as \(\mathbf a(t)=\sum\mathbf F(t)/m\) and integrated with initial velocity and position.

For the elementary friction model, static friction adjusts as needed within \(0\le F\le\mu R\) and opposes impending relative sliding. At limiting equilibrium \(F=\mu R\); in the course's sliding model the friction magnitude also uses \(\mu R\), opposing actual sliding. \(\mu\) is dimensionless. Do not set \(F=\mu R\) automatically for an ordinary static equilibrium. Determine the necessary friction from balance and test whether it fits the inequality. For motion along a rough incline, combine \(mg\sin\alpha\), friction, other tangential forces and \(ma\), then use the appropriate kinematics; a stop or reversal may change the friction direction and require a new phase.

The moment of a force about a point has magnitude force times the perpendicular distance from that point to the force's line of action, with clockwise and anticlockwise signs chosen consistently. It is measured in \(\mathrm{N\,m}\). A force through the pivot has zero moment. A rigid body in planar static equilibrium needs both zero resultant force and zero resultant moment. Choose a moment point that removes unknown forces, then use horizontal/vertical balances for the remaining reactions. A uniform rod's weight acts at its midpoint; a rectangular lamina's weight acts at its symmetry centre. A nonuniform rod uses a specified centre of mass or one found from moment balance. Apply this to beams, rods and a ladder against a wall, accounting for each smooth/rough contact and any limiting-friction condition.

What needs new teaching beyond this Mathematics A inventory

The positive inventory above is broad: elementary proof, substantial single-variable calculus, numerical iteration, two-/three-dimensional component vectors, applied probability tests and planar mechanics are already available. Merely presenting an unfamiliar symbol for one of these ideas does not make the underlying operation new. Nevertheless the following are not supplied by H240 alone: complex numbers and matrices; induction; general scalar and vector products; general 3D line/plane geometry; Taylor/Maclaurin theory beyond the displayed binomial and small-angle approximations; formal epsilon-delta analysis; multivariable/partial differentiation; multiple integrals; second-order or general nonseparable ODE methods; eigenvalues; general linear algebra; probability density integration and the normal density formula; expectation/variance formulae for arbitrary random variables; Poisson and other distributions; confidence intervals; t, chi-square or other advanced tests; estimator theory; unrestricted CLT inference; mechanics work/energy/power, momentum/impulse, circular motion, rotational dynamics or deformable-body laws. Some of these are available from the separate Further Mathematics or Physics baselines, which must be combined explicitly if the learner has those subjects.

H240's own narrower limits also matter: no required complex-root quadratic solving, modulus graphs beyond a linear function under a single modulus, memorised general logarithm change-of-base, integration reduction formulae, Simpson's rule, calculation of regression-line equations or PMCC, formal unbiased population-variance estimation, Bayes-formula reversal, numerical normal approximation to binomial probabilities, nonnormal-population mean tests, projectile landing on an inclined plane, resistive-projectile problems, 3D direction calculations, moment cross products or the angle of friction. These limits do not prohibit a learner deriving a result from available ideas or using a properly explained result in supplied notes. They identify what the baseline must not silently assume already learned.

Further Mathematics A starting knowledge

Qualification H245. Compulsory Pure Core Y540 and Y541, with the user's selected Statistics Y542 and Mechanics Y543. Current official specification checked on 7 October 2026: Version 2, October 2025. Both stages of the A-level content apply. Mathematics A knowledge is supplied separately in the Mathematics packet.

This is an operational starting-knowledge packet for a student assumed to have completed these components. Each entry supplies usable mathematical premises, not just a topic name. It is not a claim that a particular real graduate recalls every result perfectly. Knowledge of Additional Pure Y545 or Discrete Y544 is not assumed. A result outside this packet can nevertheless be obtained by a valid deduction from supplied premises; an assessment exclusion is not a prohibition on reasoning.

Pure Core

FM01 Mathematical induction. To prove P(n) for integers n at or above n0, establish P(n0), assume P(k) for an arbitrary eligible k, and prove P(k+1) from that assumption and established rules. The implication and base case are both necessary. It is legitimate to form a conjecture by computing cases, but cases alone do not prove an unrestricted statement. Apply this to divisibility, inequalities, finite sums, powers of matrices, derivatives, exponentials and factorials.

FM02 Complex quantities. i^2=-1. A complex number z=x+iy has real part x and imaginary part y, with x and y real. Equality requires equality of both parts; z=0 requires x=y=0. The conjugate is z*=x-iy and the modulus |z|=sqrt(x^2+y^2). Therefore zz*=|z|^2. i is not an unspecified variable or a real square root.

FM03 Complex arithmetic. Add and subtract real and imaginary components; multiply by distribution and i^2=-1. Divide by nonzero w using z/w=zw*/|w|^2. For example (1+2i)/(3-i)=(1+7i)/10. Products conjugate componentwise: (zw)*=z*w*. A conjugate is not a reciprocal in general.

FM04 Polar and exponential form. For nonzero z, z=r(cos(theta)+i sin(theta))=r exp(i theta), r=|z|>0. Select theta using both component signs; a single arctangent without quadrant checking is insufficient. Angles differ by multiples of 2pi. State any required principal-argument interval; otherwise either (-pi,pi] or [0,2pi) is available in OCR A. The zero number has no unique argument. Euler's relation is exp(i theta)=cos(theta)+i sin(theta).

FM05 Polar arithmetic and geometry. Multiplication multiplies moduli and adds arguments; division divides moduli and subtracts arguments. On an Argand diagram, real and imaginary components are Cartesian coordinates. Conjugation reflects in the real axis; multiplication by exp(i theta) rotates anticlockwise through theta, while multiplication by a positive real scale changes modulus. Addition is vector addition. These statements describe complex arithmetic, not a quantum-state interpretation.

FM06 Polynomial roots. A polynomial with real coefficients has non-real roots in conjugate pairs. Use the quadratic formula with complex square roots when the real discriminant is negative. To find square roots of a+ib, write u+iv, impose u^2-v^2=a and 2uv=b, and retain both opposite solutions. Factor theorem and supplied roots or factors can reduce cubics and quartics; an unrestricted memorised cubic or quartic solution formula is not assumed.

FM07 Complex loci. |z-a|=r is the circle of radius r centred at a, for r>0. |z-a|<r selects its interior; > selects exterior. |z-a|=|z-b| is the perpendicular bisector for distinct a,b. arg(z-a)=theta is a ray from a with its vertex excluded because the argument there is undefined. Re(z)=c and Im(z)=c are vertical and horizontal lines. Translate combinations into intersections of sets and distinguish included from excluded boundaries; clearly identify the chosen region instead of assuming a shading convention.

FM08 De Moivre and roots. (cos(theta)+i sin(theta))^n=cos(n theta)+i sin(n theta) for integer n; negative powers require a nonzero base. For r>0 the n roots of r exp(i theta), n a positive integer, are r^(1/n) exp(i(theta+2kpi)/n), k=0,...,n-1. These are equally spaced on an Argand circle. Use roots of unity, finite geometric sums and Euler's relation to obtain trigonometric multiple-angle identities or sums. No complex analysis theorem is supplied by these rules.

FM09 Matrix objects and operations. An m by n matrix has m rows and n columns. Equal matrices have equal shape and entries. Add or subtract equal shapes entrywise; multiply every entry by a scalar. If A has shape m by n and B has shape n by p, AB has shape m by p with (AB)_(ij)=sum_(k=1)^n A_(ik) B_(kj). Matrix entries may be real or complex. A transpose A^T swaps row and column indices and shape; it does not conjugate complex entries. A conjugate transpose has not been granted as a separate taught operation here.

FM10 Order and identities. Matrix multiplication is associative and distributive but generally AB is different from BA. For column vectors, ABv applies B first, then A. The n by n identity I leaves conformable vectors/matrices unchanged. Adding a zero matrix leaves entries unchanged; multiplying by a conformable zero matrix produces zero. A^k is repeated multiplication for positive integer k. For example A=[[1,2],[0,1]], B=[[1,0],[3,1]] gives AB=[[7,2],[3,1]] and BA=[[1,2],[3,7]].

FM11 Transformation matrices. A linear transformation represented on coordinate column vectors has columns equal to the images of coordinate unit vectors. In two dimensions, anticlockwise rotation through theta is [[cos(theta),-sin(theta)],[sin(theta),cos(theta)]]. Reflection in the x axis is diag(1,-1), in the y axis diag(-1,1), in y=x is [[0,1],[1,0]], and in y=-x is [[0,-1],[-1,0]]. Uniform enlargement is kI; axis stretches and shears follow by mapping the coordinate unit vectors. For successive transformations, preserve their application order. A translation by a nonzero vector is not represented by a two-by-two linear matrix alone.

FM12 Three-dimensional transformations. Reflections in x=0, y=0 or z=0 reverse the corresponding coordinate. Coordinate-axis rotations are obtained by holding that coordinate fixed and rotating the other two with the specified orientation. Positive rotation is anticlockwise when viewed towards the origin from the positive side of the rotation axis. State the viewing direction before interpreting signs. Only these named three-dimensional transformations are baseline assessment knowledge.

FM13 Invariance. An invariant point satisfies Av=v. An invariant line is mapped onto itself as a set and need not consist entirely of fixed points. Substitute a general point of a candidate two-dimensional line into the transformation to test the distinction. This does not grant a general eigenvalue/eigenvector theory; the selected Pure Core uses invariance directly.

FM14 Determinants. det([[a,b],[c,d]])=ad-bc. For a three-by-three matrix, expand along a row or column using alternating signs and the corresponding two-by-two minors. det(AB)=det(A)det(B). A nonzero determinant means non-singular; zero means singular. In two dimensions |det(A)| is the area scale factor and its sign records preservation or reversal of orientation. In three dimensions |det(A)| is the volume scale factor and its sign distinguishes orientation. These are geometric properties of the selected finite-dimensional transformations.

FM15 Inverses. An inverse of a square A satisfies A^(-1)A=AA^(-1)=I. For a two-by-two matrix with ad-bc nonzero, A^(-1)=[[d,-b],[-c,a]]/(ad-bc). For a non-singular three-by-three matrix use cofactor matrix, transpose to form the adjugate, then divide by the determinant; verify by multiplication. A singular matrix has no two-sided inverse. (AB)^(-1)=B^(-1)A^(-1) when the relevant inverses exist; an inverse undoes its transformation.

FM16 Simultaneous equations. Encode two or three linear equations as Ax=b. If det(A) is nonzero, x=A^(-1)b is the unique solution. If singular, use the equations to distinguish inconsistency from dependence; zero determinant alone does not distinguish no solution from infinitely many. Interpret three equations as planes that can meet at a point, along a line, coincide appropriately or have no common point. Finding a full parameterisation of every infinite solution set or a line of intersection of planes is not automatically prior assessment knowledge.

FM17 Lines and planes. A line is r=a+t u, where a is a point and u is a nonzero direction. In three dimensions its Cartesian symmetric form follows componentwise, with zero direction components treated separately rather than dividing by zero. A plane is r=a+s u+t v with independent directions u,v, or n dot (r-a)=0 with nonzero normal n. Its Cartesian form is ax+by+cz=d, with normal (a,b,c). Convert between forms using their geometric roles.

FM18 Scalar products and angles. a dot b=sum a_i b_i=|a||b|cos(theta) for real Cartesian vectors. Nonzero perpendicular vectors have scalar product zero. Use this to find angles between lines or between plane normals. A line-plane angle is the complement of the acute angle between its direction and the plane normal; choose the requested acute or oriented angle deliberately. This real-vector rule is not a rule for complex quantum inner products.

FM19 Vector products. For a=(a1,a2,a3), b=(b1,b2,b3), a cross b=(a2b3-a3b2, a3b1-a1b3, a1b2-a2b1). The result is perpendicular to both; swapping inputs negates it. Use it to construct a plane normal or a mutual perpendicular. The component formula is expressly supplied when needed in OCR A; it is supplied here so outside formula lookup is unnecessary. The separate magnitude-angle identity |a cross b|=|a||b|sin(theta), surface-area interpretation and angular-momentum/torque interpretation are not automatically recalled from this content. A magnitude can still be calculated from resulting components using the ordinary vector norm.

FM20 Intersections and distances. Equate line components and solve parameters to test a common point; parallel directions do not prove coincident lines, and nonparallel three-dimensional lines may be skew. Substitute a line into a plane equation for its intersection. The distance from point b to plane n dot r=d is |n dot b-d|/|n|. For skew lines through a and b with mutual perpendicular n, shortest distance is |(b-a) dot n|/|n|. In two dimensions point (x0,y0) to ax+by=c has distance |ax0+by0-c|/sqrt(a^2+b^2). These distance formulae are supplied resources, not a claim of unaided exam recall. Projection or perpendicular conditions determine a point-line distance.

FM21 Roots and coefficient relations. For a monic polynomial x^n+c1 x^(n-1)+...+cn with roots r1,...,rn, sum roots=-c1, sum pairwise products=c2, and successive elementary symmetric sums alternate signs, with product roots=(-1)^n cn. The selected assessment covers through quartics. Use these relations, expansions and substitution to construct a new polynomial whose roots are scaled, shifted or reciprocated, retaining exclusions where roots are zero.

FM22 Extended partial fractions. For a rational function, first divide polynomials if the numerator degree is at least the denominator degree. Use constants over linear factors, successive powers for repeated factors, and a linear numerator over a quadratic factor when required. Determine coefficients by multiplication and comparison or suitable substitutions. Integrate the resulting terms using established logarithmic, power and inverse-function rules. Do not cancel a denominator without retaining its original domain restrictions.

FM23 Sums and telescoping. sum_(r=1)^n r=n(n+1)/2; sum r^2=n(n+1)(2n+1)/6; sum r^3=[n(n+1)/2]^2. Rearrange a term into a difference whose neighbouring sums cancel and retain endpoint terms. For an infinite telescoping sum, compute the limit of the finite partial sum and establish that the remaining boundary term converges. A finite cancellation pattern alone does not establish convergence of an arbitrary infinite series.

FM24 Hyperbolic definitions. sinh(x)=(e^x-e^(-x))/2, cosh(x)=(e^x+e^(-x))/2, tanh(x)=sinh(x)/cosh(x). sinh is odd with range all real numbers; cosh is even with range [1,infinity); tanh is odd with range (-1,1). cosh^2(x)-sinh^2(x)=1. Other hyperbolic identities can be derived from these definitions, rather than imported as memorised facts.

FM25 Hyperbolic calculus. d(sinh x)/dx=cosh x, d(cosh x)/dx=sinh x, d(tanh x)/dx=1/cosh^2 x. Antiderivatives of sinh and cosh exchange them; integral tanh x dx=ln(cosh x)+C. Use the supplied ordinary product, chain and substitution rules to extend these expressions.

FM26 Inverse hyperbolic functions. arsinh(x)=ln(x+sqrt(x^2+1)) for real x; arcosh(x)=ln(x+sqrt(x^2-1)) for x>=1, selecting the nonnegative inverse of cosh; artanh(x)=0.5 ln((1+x)/(1-x)) for |x|<1. Derivatives are 1/sqrt(1+x^2), 1/sqrt(x^2-1) for x>1, and 1/(1-x^2), respectively. Their inverses and logarithmic forms can be derived by solving the exponential definitions; an inverse notation is different from a reciprocal.

FM27 Maclaurin expansions. Build the expansion around zero as f(0)+f'(0)x+f''(0)x^2/2!+...+f^(n)(0)x^n/n!+... when the expansion is valid. Supplied standard expansions are e^x=sum x^n/n!; sin x=x-x^3/3!+x^5/5!-...; cos x=1-x^2/2!+x^4/4!-...; ln(1+x)=x-x^2/2+x^3/3-...; and (1+x)^a=1+ax+a(a-1)x^2/2!+.... Exponential, sine and cosine expansions apply for real x. The supplied logarithmic series applies for -1<x<=1, including ln(2)=1-1/2+1/3-.... Use |x|<1 for a nonterminating general binomial expansion unless its boundary is separately established. Nonnegative integer binomial expansions terminate. Substitution changes the validity interval. Expansions in a non-real argument, convergence proofs and a general remainder theorem are not automatically granted.

FM28 Improper integrals. Replace an infinite endpoint by a finite bound and take its limit. If an integrand is singular inside an interval, split into two integrals and require each relevant one-sided limit to exist separately. For example integral_1^infinity x^(-p) dx converges for p>1, as follows by evaluating its finite primitive and limit. Cancellation across a divergent singularity is not a legitimate improper-integral value.

FM29 Inverse trigonometric derivatives and substitution. Principal real inverse domains give d(arcsin x)/dx=1/sqrt(1-x^2) and d(arccos x)/dx=-1/sqrt(1-x^2) for |x|<1; d(arctan x)/dx=1/(1+x^2). Differentiate implicit inverse relationships to derive them. Integrals involving a^2-x^2, a^2+x^2 or x^2-a^2 can be treated by suitable trigonometric or hyperbolic substitutions, with signs and real domains checked. For a>0: integral dx/sqrt(a^2-x^2)=arcsin(x/a)+C for |x|<a; integral dx/(a^2+x^2)=arctan(x/a)/a+C; integral dx/sqrt(a^2+x^2)=arsinh(x/a)+C; and integral dx/sqrt(x^2-a^2)=arcosh(x/a)+C for x>a. The latter follows using x=a cosh(u) on the positive branch; ln|x+sqrt(x^2-a^2)|+C works on either interval x>a or x<-a, with a separate constant on each disconnected interval. Verify variants by differentiation.

FM30 Volumes and means. Rotating a region around a coordinate axis produces disks or washers. About the x axis V=pi integral [R(x)^2-r(x)^2] dx, with analogous y-axis form. Derive it from cross-sectional areas. For parametric curves use dx=x'(t)dt or dy=y'(t)dt with correctly ordered bounds. The average value of f on a finite interval [a,b], a<b, is integral_a^b f(x) dx/(b-a); this is distinct from a probability-weighted mean unless the distribution is uniform.

FM31 Polar curves. With r>=0, x=r cos(theta), y=r sin(theta). Converting back requires modulus and quadrant checks. Analyse symmetry, intercepts, extrema of r and angular ranges to sketch r=f(theta). Enclosed area on a correctly selected angular interval is 0.5 integral r(theta)^2 dtheta; for a region between radial boundaries use the difference of squares. A curve traced twice must not be counted twice accidentally.

FM32 First-order differential equations. A general solution contains arbitrary constant(s); initial or boundary conditions choose a particular solution. For y'+P(x)y=Q(x), use integrating factor mu(x)=exp(integral P(x)dx), giving (mu y)'=mu Q and y=[integral mu Q dx+C]/mu. Rearrange to this linear form before applying the rule. Separability from Mathematics remains available. Deriving an equation from a model and solving it are separate abilities.

FM33 Constant-coefficient homogeneous second-order equations. For a y''+b y'+c y=0 with a nonzero constant, solve a r^2+b r+c=0. Distinct real roots give C1 e^(r1 x)+C2 e^(r2 x); a repeated root r gives (C1+C2 x)e^(r x); roots alpha +/- i beta give e^(alpha x)(C1 cos(beta x)+C2 sin(beta x)). Use two independent conditions where available and verify by substitution. The corresponding OCR normalised form has leading coefficient one.

FM34 Forced second-order equations. For a y''+b y'+c y=f(x), add a particular solution to the homogeneous solution. For polynomial forcing try a polynomial of sufficient degree; for exponential forcing try an exponential multiple; for sine/cosine forcing try both sine and cosine terms. If the trial duplicates a homogeneous mode, multiply by sufficient powers of x to remove that duplication. Substitute to determine coefficients; a guessed trial is not an answer until checked. Supplied trial forms may extend an individual problem. Laplace transforms and arbitrary variable-coefficient solution methods are not granted.

FM35 SHM and damping. x''=-omega^2 x, with omega>0, has x=A cos(omega t)+B sin(omega t)=R sin(omega t+phi), period 2pi/omega, with displacement measured from equilibrium. A positive damping term in m x''+c x'+k x=0 changes the roots: c^2<4mk is oscillatory decay, equality is critical damping, and c^2>4mk is overdamping. These classifications assume m,k>0 and c>0. Interpret initial conditions and physical decay; do not confuse the undamped natural frequency with every observed damped frequency.

FM36 Coupled linear equations. For x'=a x+b y+f(t), y'=c x+d y+g(t), with constant coefficients, differentiate one equation and use the equations to eliminate the other variable, producing a second-order equation where the coupling permits. Recover the eliminated variable from an original equation and check both. Apply initial data to the full system. This permits elimination-based solution, not an assumed general eigenmode or matrix-exponential method.

Statistics Y542

FM37 Counting. n!=n(n-1)...1, 0!=1; ordered choices of r distinct objects from n have count n!/(n-r)!, unordered choices n!/[r!(n-r)!]. Identical repetitions divide arrangements by the factorials of repeated counts. Handle restrictions by grouping, complementary counting or sequential choices, with attention to whether choices are equally likely. Use these counts within the conditional-probability rules in Mathematics; counting alone does not establish equiprobability.

FM38 Discrete distributions and expectation. A probability mass function p(x) is nonnegative and sums to one on its support. E(X)=sum x p(x), E(g(X))=sum g(x)p(x), and Var(X)=E(X^2)-E(X)^2=sum (x-mu)^2 p(x). Standard deviation is sqrt(Var(X)). These rules permit weighted means directly. A distribution table or normalising factor must specify the weights; these rules do not select canonical thermal weights or quantum probabilities. A nonstandard distribution in the assessed baseline is finite.

FM39 Linear coding and binomial moments. E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b and Var(aX+b)=a^2 Var(X). For X binomial(n,p), P(X=k)=C(n,k)p^k(1-p)^(n-k), mean np and variance np(1-p), under fixed-number, independent trials with common success probability. Interpret the random quantity before selecting a distribution.

FM40 Discrete uniform and geometric models. Uniform on {1,...,n}, for positive integer n, gives P(X=k)=1/n, mean (n+1)/2 and variance (n^2-1)/12. A geometric(p) variable, with 0<p<=1, counts trials up to and including first success: P(X=k)=p(1-p)^(k-1), k>=1, P(X>k)=(1-p)^k, mean 1/p, variance (1-p)/p^2. This assumes independent trials with unchanged p. For p=1 the first trial succeeds with certainty; p=0 gives no finite first-success distribution. A failures-before-success convention has different support and mean and must be explicitly translated.

FM41 Poisson model. For independent events at a constant average rate without clustering in disjoint small intervals, counts over an interval are modelled by Poisson(lambda), lambda>=0: P(X=k)=e^(-lambda)lambda^k/k!, k=0,1,...; E(X)=Var(X)=lambda. For lambda=0 the count is zero with certainty. Rescale lambda for interval size. Independent Poisson counts sum to a Poisson count with parameter sum lambda_i. A Poisson approximation to a binomial distribution is not separately granted by the selected OCR content.

FM42 Continuous density and distribution. A density f(x)>=0 integrates to one on its support; P(a<X<b)=integral_a^b f(x) dx and an individual point has probability zero for an absolutely continuous distribution. F(x)=P(X<=x)=integral_(-infinity)^x f(t)dt, and f=F' at differentiable points. For piecewise densities enforce the support, normalisation and boundary continuity of F. Medians and quartiles solve F(x)=0.5,0.25,0.75 respectively. A density value is not itself an interval probability.

FM43 Continuous moments. E(X)=integral x f(x) dx; E(g(X))=integral g(x)f(x)dx; Var(X)=integral x^2 f(x)dx-mu^2, over the support where the required integrals converge. For Y=g(X), construct F_Y(y)=P(g(X)<=y) using the inverse inequality and all relevant branches, then differentiate if a density exists. An arbitrary remembered change-of-variables Jacobian formula is not supplied.

FM44 Standard continuous distributions. Uniform on [a,b], b>a, has density 1/(b-a), mean (a+b)/2, variance (b-a)^2/12. Exponential with rate lambda>0 has density lambda e^(-lambda x) for x>=0, F(x)=1-e^(-lambda x), mean 1/lambda, variance 1/lambda^2; waiting time to the next event in a Poisson model connects these quantities. Normal(mu,sigma^2), sigma>0, is symmetric with density exp[-(x-mu)^2/(2sigma^2)]/(sigma sqrt(2pi)); standardise with Z=(X-mu)/sigma. Probability values may be evaluated from numerical information supplied in the notes; no external calculator access is implied by this packet.

FM45 Sums of variables. E(aX+bY+c)=aE(X)+bE(Y)+c without an independence requirement. If X,Y are independent, Var(aX+bY+c)=a^2 Var(X)+b^2 Var(Y). Independent normal variables have normally distributed linear combinations; their means and variances follow these formulae. Without independence, the simple variance-sum formula is not justified. The general covariance correction is not automatically supplied here.

FM46 Sampling distributions. For independent identically distributed samples with finite population mean mu and variance sigma^2, the sample mean has mean mu and variance sigma^2/n. A normal population gives a normal sample mean exactly; a sufficiently large random independent sample often supports a central-limit normal approximation. OCR uses roughly n>25 as a practical indication, not a universal guarantee for every population shape. Unbiased sample variance is sum (x_i-xbar)^2/(n-1), n>1; the sample mean estimates population mean.

FM47 Tests for a population mean. State H0 and H1 in terms of the population mean. With known variance, use z=(xbar-mu0)/(sigma/sqrt(n)); use a normal population or a justified large-sample approximation. For large samples with unknown variance, replace sigma by the supplied unbiased sample estimate s under the approximation. Compare the relevant one- or two-tailed probability or critical value to the significance level. Rejecting H0 is evidence against it, not certainty; failure to reject does not prove H0. Small-sample Student t tests are not selected baseline knowledge.

FM48 Confidence intervals. Under the conditions for the preceding normal calculation, a two-sided confidence interval for mu is xbar +/- z_(1-alpha/2) sigma/sqrt(n), using s for the appropriate large-sample unknown-variance approximation. z_(0.975) approximately 1.960 gives 95%; z_(0.995) approximately 2.576 gives 99%. The confidence level describes the repeated-sampling coverage of the procedure, not a new probability distribution over the fixed parameter after observing this sample. Additional quantiles must be supplied or derived from supplied numerical data.

FM49 Chi-squared independence. Under independent observations classified in an r by c contingency table, expected count E_ij=(row total_i)(column total_j)/(grand total) under independence. Compute sum (O-E)^2/E and compare with a supplied chi-squared critical value at degrees of freedom (r-1)(c-1). Combine appropriate rows/columns when expected frequencies are below five; this changes degrees of freedom. OCR calls for Yates' continuity correction in a two-by-two table: reduce each absolute O-E discrepancy by 0.5, without making a discrepancy negative, before squaring and dividing by E. Interpret evidence for association, not a proved causal effect.

FM50 Chi-squared goodness of fit. For mutually exclusive classes, a hypothesised distribution supplies expected counts N p_i. Combine adjacent classes as needed to keep expected counts at least five. Sum (O-E)^2/E and use degrees of freedom equal to number of resulting classes minus one minus the number of parameters estimated from these data. Compare with the supplied critical value and state the conclusion in context. Numerical critical-value tables are resources, not all memorised values.

FM51 Sign tests. For an independent random sample with no observation equal to the hypothesised median, signs relative to that median have probability one-half under H0. Count one sign using a binomial(n,0.5) model and use the appropriate tail(s). For paired observations apply signs to within-pair differences; do not treat paired and unpaired samples as interchangeable. This provides a non-parametric alternative when a mean/normal model is inappropriate.

FM52 Wilcoxon signed ranks. For a single random sample or independent sampled pairs, form differences around the hypothesised centre, rank absolute nonzero differences, attach original signs and sum positive/negative ranks W+ and W-. The null model requires a continuous symmetric distribution of centred differences and independent observations or pairs. Ties and zero differences are outside the selected assessment cases. OCR's table statistic is T=min(W+,W-): reject for T at or below the supplied critical value, using the table's one- or two-tailed level and the correct sign of difference for a directional alternative. For sufficiently large n, either raw sum W+ or W- has null mean n(n+1)/4 and variance n(n+1)(2n+1)/24. Apply a normal approximation to that raw sum, not to folded T. For an integer-valued raw statistic R with matched normal variable N: P(R<=k) approximately P(N<=k+0.5), P(R>=k) approximately P(N>=k-0.5), and P(a<=R<=b) approximately P(a-0.5<=N<=b+0.5).

FM53 Wilcoxon rank sum. For two independent random unpaired samples, choose the smaller size m<=n, rank all m+n observations and sum the smaller sample's ranks as R_m. Under the null hypothesis that the populations have the same continuous distribution and with no ties, E(R_m)=m(m+n+1)/2 and Var(R_m)=mn(m+n+1)/12. OCR's table uses the reflected statistic W=min(R_m, m(m+n+1)-R_m), with rejection for W at or below the supplied critical value. Use its stated one- or two-tailed level and check that the direction of the observed difference supports a directional alternative. A large-sample normal approximation applies to raw R_m with the displayed mean and variance and FM52's explicit continuity corrections, not to folded W. For a median-shift interpretation, the two population distributions must have the same shape and spread. This is different from the paired signed-rank calculation.

FM54 Correlation and coding. For paired data define Sxx=sum(x-xbar)^2, Syy=sum(y-ybar)^2 and Sxy=sum(x-xbar)(y-ybar). Pearson r=Sxy/sqrt(Sxx Syy) where both sums of squares are nonzero. It measures linear association and lies between -1 and 1. Adding constants leaves r unchanged; multiplying x,y by nonzero a,b changes its sign by sign(ab) and preserves magnitude. Positive linear coding preserves its value. A source shorthand that linear coding leaves r unchanged must not license ignoring a negative scale factor.

FM55 Rank correlation and tests. With n paired observations and no ties, Spearman r_s=1-6 sum d_i^2/[n(n^2-1)], n>1, where d_i is the difference of the two ranks. It assesses monotonic rank association. Select Pearson for an appropriate linear relationship and Spearman for suitable ranked/monotonic relationships. For Pearson hypothesis testing, use the supplied critical values or p-values under the specified bivariate-normal assumption; rank tests use their supplied critical values and relevant sampling assumptions. Correlation does not establish causation.

FM56 Regression. A least-squares line of y on x minimises vertical squared residuals. Its slope b=Sxy/Sxx and intercept a=ybar-b xbar, provided Sxx>0. Use it to predict response y from controlled/explanatory x within a reasonable data range. Do not reverse it to treat y as the controlled predictor or claim reliability of extrapolation without justification. Linear coding changes slope and intercept through substitution. A regression estimate has uncertainty even when arithmetic is exact.

Mechanics Y543

FM57 Dimensions. Write dimensions in mass M, length L and time T; position L, velocity LT^(-1), acceleration LT^(-2), force MLT^(-2), energy ML^2T^(-2), momentum MLT^(-1), power ML^2T^(-3). Terms added must share dimensions. Solve for exponents in a proposed relationship and check dimensional consistency. Matching dimensions cannot determine a dimensionless coefficient or establish that a physical law is true.

FM58 Work and energy. Constant force work is F dot displacement. A one-dimensional variable force does work integral F(x)dx. Kinetic energy is m(v dot v)/2, local gravitational potential energy is mgh. Net work equals change of kinetic energy for the stated fixed-mass particle model. Conservative changes can be tracked through potential energy; include dissipative loss explicitly when mechanical energy is not conserved. The multidimensional line-integral form for a variable field is university teaching, not a prior rule supplied by this mechanics option.

FM59 Elastic strings and springs. For natural length l, extension x and modulus lambda, tension T=lambda x/l in the Hookean range, and stored elastic energy=lambda x^2/(2l). A spring can resist compression within its model; an elastic string carries tension when extended and becomes slack when not taut. Spring constant k=lambda/l gives E=kx^2/2. Establish which length is extension and whether the string is taut before using energy or force equations.

FM60 Power. Average power is work/time and instantaneous power is F dot v. For positive force and speed magnitudes this is +Fv in the same direction and -Fv in opposite directions; signed one-dimensional components already account for the direction. Resolve force components on slopes and balance tractive force with resistance/weight components. Constant power does not imply constant driving force or speed. If a model predicts a terminal or maximum speed, check its assumptions and force balance.

FM61 Momentum and impulse. Momentum is m v for a fixed-mass particle. Total vector momentum is conserved when resultant external impulse is zero over the event. Impulse equals momentum change; for constant force J=F Delta t, and in one dimension a variable force has J=integral F(t)dt. Track signs and resolve components for two-dimensional events. Internal interaction impulses cancel for the system; momentum of either object alone need not be conserved.

FM62 Restitution. For a direct impact, relative separation speed=e times relative approach speed along the impact line, with 0<=e<=1 in the assessed models. With consistent one-dimensional coordinates this is v2-v1=e(u1-u2). For a smooth fixed plane, the normal velocity component reverses and is scaled by e; the tangential component is unchanged in the smooth model. For two smooth spheres use their line of centres at contact as the normal, apply restitution and conservation of normal momentum, and leave each sphere's tangential component unchanged because the smooth impact impulse is normal. e=1 has no impact kinetic-energy loss; e=0 is the maximum-loss normal impact case. General scientific terminology calls 0<e<1 inelastic too; OCR's stated coalescing 'inelastic' limiting case is e=0. Coalescence of oblique bodies is not established merely by equal normal components.

FM63 Centres of mass. For point masses, r_cm=sum m_i r_i/sum m_i, with positive total mass. Uniformity and symmetry can locate a body's centre. Combine uniform bodies by signed masses when material is removed, checking the resulting physical body and mass. For a uniform lamina between y=f(x) and y=g(x), on bounds with f>=g and positive area A=integral(f-g)dx, xbar=integral x(f-g)dx/A and ybar=integral(f^2-g^2)dx/(2A). For a uniform solid with cross-sectional area A(x)>0 on its occupied interval, xbar=integral x A(x)dx/integral A(x)dx. A solid made by revolving the region from the x axis to y=f(x) has A(x)=pi f(x)^2; a hollow washer with outer radius R(x) and inner radius r(x) has A(x)=pi[R(x)^2-r(x)^2], R>=r>=0. These use one-variable integration and do not grant arbitrary multivariable density integrals.

FM64 Standard uniform-body centres. A rod's centre is its midpoint; a triangle's centroid lies one-third of its altitude from the base. A semicircular lamina's centre is 4r/(3pi) from its diameter; a hemispherical solid's centre is 3r/8 from its flat face; a thin hemispherical shell's centre is r/2 from the sphere centre towards its pole. A solid cone's centre has perpendicular height h/4 above its base; a hollow thin conical surface's centre has perpendicular height h/3. A solid pyramid's centre is r_base+(r_vertex-r_base)/4, where r_base is the base centroid; its perpendicular height is h/4, while its distance along an oblique centroid-to-vertex line is one-quarter of that line's length. A circular-sector lamina of half-angle alpha has centre 2r sin(alpha)/(3alpha) from its circle centre on its symmetry axis; a uniform circular arc with angle 2alpha has centre r sin(alpha)/alpha on that axis. Angles are in radians, alpha>0, and these formulae assume the stated uniform solid, surface, lamina or wire model. The OCR booklet supplies the shape formulas apart from the rod midpoint obtained by symmetry and the semicircle obtained by setting alpha=pi/2 in its sector formula. Notes must supply any further shape constant actually required.

FM65 Equilibrium and stability. In a uniform gravitational field a body's weight can be represented at its centre of mass. For coplanar equilibrium use zero resultant force and zero resultant moment about a chosen point. Resolve support/friction forces and check limiting friction if relevant. A body may topple when the vertical line through its centre of mass reaches the edge of its support; sliding depends on friction and normal reaction. The current H245 6.04e text both mentions hinged bodies and excludes them in the same stage-two cell. This ambiguity is retained in the research record; a test requiring a specific hinge-assessment convention must supply the hinge model explicitly in its notes.

FM66 Circular kinematics. For a point on a circle of fixed radius r>0, signed tangential velocity is r theta', speed v=r|theta'|, inward radial acceleration v^2/r=r(theta')^2, and signed tangential acceleration r theta''. For a horizontal circle at constant speed, resolve forces and equate the inward resultant to mv^2/r. 'Centripetal force' names this resultant rather than an extra force added to tension, gravity or contact force. These circular components do not grant a general spherical-coordinate acceleration formula.

FM67 Vertical circles and loss of constraint. For variable-speed circular motion, use energy to obtain speed and radial Newton's law to obtain tension or normal reaction, with radial gravity sign dependent on position. A string can sustain nonnegative tension but cannot push; a contact reaction cannot pull. A calculated zero force is the boundary: check whether subsequent motion can retain the constraint or whether maintaining it would require a negative force. Zero at an isolated point does not necessarily imply departure; a limiting complete vertical circle can have zero tension at its top. When the constraint is lost, use the specified free-fall/projectile model and check actual separation. A result requiring negative tension indicates breakdown of the assumed taut-string path.

FM68 Variable-force motion. In one dimension use m dv/dt=F and, where a velocity-as-position description is valid, a=v dv/dx from the chain rule. Solve the resulting separable or linear first-order equation with appropriate data; a force depending on velocity need not produce constant acceleration. Recover position and interpret stopping, limiting speed and the physical domain. Variable mass and arbitrary nonlinear/PDE solution methods are not granted. The Pure Core exclusion of a=v dv/dx does not remove it from this selected Mechanics option.

FM69 What is not a prior university formalism. This selected course does not by itself grant partial differentiation, a gradient/divergence/curl calculus, multiple/line/surface integrals of fields, general abstract vector spaces, eigenvalue diagonalisation, Hilbert-space inner products, quantum measurement postulates, Lagrangians or stationary action, a canonical partition function, Laplace/Fourier transforms, stochastic processes or general PDE solving. Earlier supplied operations can support an explicit deduction, but their names cannot silently import these additional theories.

FM70 Resources and calculation conditions. All formulas actually used from this packet are supplied in its text. An assessment may require a numerical normal/chi-squared/rank critical value normally obtained from a calculator or statistical table; the corresponding notes must supply needed values if outside tools are unavailable. A missing lookup datum is different from an untaught probability concept. Use consistent symbols and define quantities afresh when a university course uses a different convention.

FM71 Combination with the other subjects. The complete baseline includes the separate Mathematics, Physics and Chemistry packets. Physical interpretations in those packets may support deductions using the mathematics here. Knowledge of a mathematical exponential weight does not select a physical distribution; knowledge of complex matrices does not define quantum states; knowledge of reaction Gibbs energy does not supply statistical free-energy identities. Evaluate each consequential bridge against the actual combined premises.

- Vacuum permittivity ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹, equivalently C² N⁻¹ m⁻²; Stefan constant σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴.
- Electron rest mass me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg; proton rest mass mp = 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; neutron rest mass mn = 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; alpha-particle rest mass mα = 6.646 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
- Unified atomic mass unit 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; 1 day = 8.64 × 10⁴ s; 1 year ≈ 3.16 × 10⁷ s; 1 light-year ≈ 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m; 1 parsec ≈ 3.1 × 10¹⁶ m. For an AU calculation, use a supplied value or the ordinary approximation 1 AU ≈ 1.50 × 10¹¹ m.

Useful geometry and mathematical identities: circumference 2πr; circle area πr²; cylinder curved surface area 2πrh and volume πr²h; sphere area 4πr² and volume 4πr³/3; trapezium area (a + b)h/2. In a right triangle a² = b² + c²; generally a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A and a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C. ln(ab) = ln a + ln b, ln(a/b) = ln a − ln b, ln(aⁿ) = n ln a, and ln(eˣ) = x. Use logarithms of ratios or numerical values in explicitly stated units. One full turn is 360° = 2π radians.

1. Quantities, mathematics, measurement and evidence

Units and calculations

A physical answer normally contains a value and a unit. Use SI consistently before substituting: kg, m, s, A, K and mol are the base units explicitly required here. Recognise derived units, including N = kg m s⁻², J = kg m² s⁻², W = J s⁻¹, Pa = N m⁻², C = A s, V = J C⁻¹, Ω = V A⁻¹ and F = C V⁻¹. An equation must have matching dimensions on both sides; dimensional agreement is a necessary check, not proof that its physics or numerical factor is correct.

Know the specified prefixes: pico 10⁻¹², nano 10⁻⁹, micro 10⁻⁶, milli 10⁻³, centi 10⁻², deci 10⁻¹, kilo 10³, mega 10⁶, giga 10⁹ and tera 10¹². A conversion applies to the whole powered unit: 1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m² and 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³. Distinguish mass from weight, energy from power, charge from current, and temperature from energy.

Use a scientific calculator for powers, roots, exponentials, natural and base-10 logarithms, and trigonometry. Choose degree or radian mode deliberately. Rearrange nonlinear formulae and solve quadratics, retaining a physically admissible root. Use ratios to predict how an outcome changes: if speed doubles, kinetic energy quadruples; doubling orbital radius multiplies period by 2³ᐟ². Estimate scales, check dimensions and consider whether a result is physically reasonable. Keep intermediate precision, then give justified significant figures. A 'show that' calculation should expose the substitutions and the unrounded value needed to justify the stated approximation.

Vectors, graphs and rates

Scalars have magnitude; vectors also have direction. Displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum and field strength are vectors. Distance, speed, mass, energy, pressure, charge and potential are scalars. Vector addition uses a triangle, parallelogram or perpendicular components. If θ is measured from the positive x axis, Aₓ = A cos θ and Aᵧ = A sin θ, with signs supplied by direction. Add components, then find magnitude √(Aₓ² + Aᵧ²) and a direction in the correct quadrant. Negative one-dimensional velocity means motion opposite the chosen positive axis; it does not mean negative speed.

Read and construct graphs with labelled quantities and units, sensible linear scales, small clear points and an appropriate best-fit line or curve. Do not force a line through the origin without physical and data justification. Find gradients using a large triangle on the fitted line. A tangent estimates an instantaneous rate, whereas a secant gives an average rate. Graph areas are quantities with units equal to the product of the axis units, and may have a signed physical meaning. Estimate curved areas with strips or trapezia when necessary.

Linearise models before fitting. For y = ax + b, the slope is a and intercept b. For y = axⁿ, log y = log a + n log x: the log-log slope gives n. For y = y₀e⁻ᵏᵗ, ln(y/y₀) = −kt, or a graph of ln y against t has slope −k when numerical values are used in specified units. A base-10 log graph has a different slope factor, −k/ln 10. A constant fractional decrease over equal intervals is evidence for an exponential, not a constant absolute decrease. Use small-angle approximations sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ and cos θ ≈ 1 only for a small angle expressed in radians.

Uncertainty and experimental judgement

Accuracy concerns agreement with a true or accepted value; precision concerns closeness of repeated results. Random effects produce scatter; systematic effects bias results, including an instrument zero error. Repeating and averaging can reduce random influence but does not eliminate a systematic bias. Resolution is the smallest readable increment; it is not automatically the entire uncertainty, especially for hand timing. Repeatability concerns the same method and conditions; reproducibility concerns changed operators, apparatus or methods.

State a result as x ± δx with a defensible absolute uncertainty; percentage uncertainty is 100δx/|x|. Follow the uncertainty stated in a question or marked on the apparatus. The current handbook's default is half a smallest division per analogue reading and one resolution per digital reading. A distance formed from two scale readings has two reading uncertainties; reaction time can dominate a stopwatch's display resolution. OCR also accepts correctly applied half-resolution or full-resolution instrument approaches in assessment because published resources differ. This tolerance should not be mistaken for a general metrology rule. Current practical handbook, measurement guidance.

For the elementary A-level propagation convention:

- In a sum or difference, add the absolute uncertainties: δ(a ± b) = δa + δb.
- In a product or quotient, add fractional or percentage uncertainties.
- For xⁿ, multiply fractional uncertainty by |n|; roots are fractional powers.
- A known exact constant adds no measurement uncertainty. For a wire area πd²/4, the percentage uncertainty is twice the diameter's.

Draw error bars where appropriate. A plausible steepest or shallowest line through the allowed regions can estimate a slope limit. With one worst line, use the absolute difference between its gradient and the best-fit gradient as the estimated gradient uncertainty; with both limits available, explain the chosen convention. Carry that uncertainty into the final physical result. Compare differences with uncertainty intervals before claiming disagreement. Percentage error relative to an accepted value differs from uncertainty; when comparing two experimental values, state the reference or denominator used for a percentage difference.

An anomaly warrants investigation or repetition and a reasoned decision; it is not discarded solely because it spoils a desired line. Explain specific limitations and improvements: longer timing intervals reduce fractional timing uncertainty; a longer wire gives a larger measurable extension; lower current limits unwanted heating. 'Human error' or 'use better apparatus' without a mechanism is inadequate. Rigorous statistics, standard errors, least-squares theory and uncertainty covariance are not required by this qualification. Current mathematics handbook, M1.5.

Practical competence assumed at this level

Plan an investigation from a question: identify an independent variable, a measured dependent variable and controlled variables; choose suitable ranges, resolution and repeats; anticipate hazards; record raw data before processing; justify the graph or comparison; evaluate the evidence. Read analogue scales, calipers and micrometers; use a balance, stopwatch, light gates and multimeter; construct and check circuits; use a signal generator and oscilloscope; use sensors, data logging and software. On an oscilloscope, voltage = vertical divisions × volts/division and period = horizontal divisions per cycle × time/division. Distinguish peak amplitude from peak-to-peak excursion.

Know the operation behind these measurements: correct a micrometer/caliper zero offset before averaging diameters; avoid parallax on a scale; measure the length of a light-gate interrupt card to convert blocking time to mean speed; use a fiducial mark and the same phase/direction when counting cycles; measure the original unloaded wire/spring length and then the change. A multimeter's current and voltage settings require different circuit connections and sensible ranges; an ohmmeter measures an isolated unpowered component. Identify actual risks and controls in the proposed procedure, such as securing falling masses, preventing wire recoil toward eyes, limiting electrical heating, keeping laser beams out of eyes, and minimising source-handling time with distance and appropriate shielding in supervised ionising-radiation work.

The student should be able to explain the following investigations sufficiently to choose apparatus, obtain a derived quantity and evaluate the method:

Investigation: Operational method and analysis
Motion, collisions and free fall: Obtain distances/times or light-gate speeds; interpret motion graphs; for release from rest fit distance against t² to obtain g/2, or velocity against t to obtain g. Account for release/timing arrangements.
Terminal speed in a fluid: Measure travel over a region where successive speeds are constant; vary a justified factor and distinguish the acceleration region from steady motion.
Springs and wires: Measure extension from an initial length under varied loads; plot force against extension; find stiffness in the linear region and recognise permanent deformation.
Young modulus: Use F, original length L, cross-sectional area A and extension x; obtain Y = FL/(Ax) or a fitted equivalent; use repeated diameter measurements and control loading.
Electrical component characteristics: Measure current through and p.d. across a component; vary supply, include polarity where appropriate, and interpret an I–V curve.
Resistivity: Obtain resistance and length of a uniform wire; measure diameter for area; fit R against L; distinguish material resistivity from component resistance and control temperature.
Internal resistance: Vary load and measure terminal voltage and current; V = ℰ − Ir gives intercept ℰ and slope −r.
Sensor potential divider: Construct the specified two-component circuit; measure output as temperature or illumination changes; account for which component has the output across it.
Waves and light: Use stationary-wave spacings, resonance tubes, oscilloscopes, double slits or a grating to infer speed, frequency or wavelength; state the geometric assumptions.
Quantum and optical effects: Explain LED threshold-voltage estimation of h, refraction/critical-angle measurements, polarisation and a photoelectric demonstration.
Gases: Investigate pV at fixed temperature or p/T at fixed volume; a pressure–Celsius temperature extrapolation estimates absolute zero.
Capacitors and decay: Record charging/discharging voltage or current versus time; fit an exponential or a log graph; background-correct radioactive counts before estimating half-life.
Oscillation and electromagnetism: Time multiple cycles using a fiducial marker; observe resonance/damping; infer B from a wire's force measured by balance; investigate changing flux and transformer ratios.
Thermal properties: Compare electrical input VIt with mcΔT or mℓ, identifying energy losses and apparatus heat capacity; use appropriate heating and mass-loss measurements.

Experience with apparatus and demonstrated practical skills are related but distinct. The endorsement requires at least 12 assessed activities, independent evidence of the CPAC skills and experience of the specified apparatus and techniques; the activities are not 12 universally fixed recipes. The practical result is reported separately from the examination grade. The 2026 guidance also requires assessed competence and written evidence to remain authentically the student's own when AI tools are used. Practical handbook, sections 4 and 6, current specification, section 4e.

2. Forces, motion, energy and materials

Motion and forces

Average speed is total distance/time; average velocity is displacement/time. Instantaneous velocity is the displacement–time gradient; acceleration is the velocity–time gradient. Signed area under a velocity–time graph gives displacement; area under a speed–time graph gives distance. A curved graph requires a tangent or area estimate, not a constant-acceleration equation applied blindly.

For constant acceleration along one axis, use v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as and s = ½(u + v)t, with signed u, v, a and s. In near-surface free fall without drag, acceleration is approximately constant g downward. For a projectile without drag, resolve launch velocity into horizontal and vertical components; horizontal velocity stays constant and vertical motion has acceleration −g if upward is positive. Both components share the same elapsed time. Solve for landing time using the actual launch and landing heights; a symmetric-flight shortcut applies only to equal heights.

Draw a free-body diagram for the chosen object and include only forces acting on it. Weight is mg downward; a normal contact force is perpendicular to the contact surface; tension acts along a taut string; friction and drag oppose the relevant relative motion or tendency. Apply ΣF = ma separately along chosen axes. Equilibrium requires zero resultant force; a body can then be at rest or move with constant velocity. Newton's first law describes that condition, the second relates resultant force to momentum change, and the third gives equal and opposite forces on different interacting bodies. A third-law pair is not two balancing forces on one body.

Drag depends on speed and such factors as shape, area and fluid properties. In a falling body it usually increases as speed increases, reducing the downward resultant and acceleration. Terminal velocity occurs when the resultant force is zero: weight balances drag plus any upthrust. Terminal motion therefore has nonzero speed but zero acceleration. A speed–time graph approaches a plateau. A-level reasoning includes changing acceleration; solving a drag differential equation is not presupposed.

Thinking distance = speed × reaction time. Stopping distance is thinking distance plus braking distance. Under an approximately constant braking force, work–energy reasoning makes braking distance proportional to speed squared; the result depends on maintaining that force assumption. Reaction time, surface/tyre conditions and gradients can change different parts of the stopping process.

Equilibrium, pressure and buoyancy

A moment is force × perpendicular distance from the pivot to its line of action, with clockwise/anticlockwise sign. A couple is equal and opposite parallel forces whose torque is Fd, with d their perpendicular separation. Full static equilibrium requires both ΣF = 0 and Σmoments = 0. Three coplanar forces in equilibrium form a closed vector triangle; include the rotational condition as well. Locate a suspended object's centre of gravity by intersecting vertical plumb lines from different suspension points. Centre of mass and centre of gravity coincide for the usual uniform gravitational field.

Density ρ = m/V. Pressure is normal force/area. In a fluid at rest with uniform density, the pressure increase over depth h is ρgh; absolute pressure also includes pressure at the surface. Upthrust equals the weight of displaced fluid, ρfluidgVdisplaced. A floating object balances its weight with upthrust, so displaced fluid mass equals object mass; using the whole object volume is correct only if it is fully submerged.

Energy, work, power and momentum

For a constant force acting through displacement s, work W = Fs cos θ, with θ between force and displacement. Work transfers energy; resistive forces can remove mechanical energy while total energy remains conserved. Kinetic energy is ½mv² for ordinary nonrelativistic motion. The gravitational potential-energy change mgh is valid in a uniform field; it is not the general formula for planetary distances. Obtain these results through constant-force work and motion relations. Solve transfers by explicitly accounting for input, useful stored energy and dissipated energy.

Average power is transferred energy/time; for force along the motion, instantaneous mechanical power is Fv. Efficiency is useful output/input, expressed as a fraction or multiplied by 100% for a percentage. Ratios of powers can replace energy ratios for steady operation.

Momentum p = mv is a vector. Mean resultant force over an interval is Δp/Δt; for constant mass this reduces to ma. Impulse equals momentum change and equals signed area under a force–time graph; FΔt applies to constant or average force. Calculate Δp as final minus initial momentum, including a change of direction. Increasing collision time for the same impulse reduces mean force.

Conserve total momentum for a system with negligible external impulse. This applies separately to x and y in two-dimensional interactions. In a perfectly elastic collision, total kinetic energy is also conserved; an inelastic collision converts some kinetic energy to other forms. Objects sticking together are a particular inelastic case. Momentum conservation does not imply kinetic-energy conservation. Recoil, explosions and oblique collisions can be analysed with these same rules. Angular momentum and rigid-body rotational dynamics are not supplied by this qualification.

Springs and material response

Extension x is the change from original length, not final length. In the linear region, Hooke's law gives force magnitude F = kx; k has units N m⁻¹. A restoring force has the opposite direction to displacement. The gradient of an F–x plot gives stiffness; the area under it gives work. Elastic energy ½kx² = ½Fx follows only for linear loading from zero extension. For nonlinearity use the actual graph area. Springs in series share force and add extensions; springs in parallel share extension and add forces, so an effective stiffness can be derived from those conditions.

Tensile stress σ = F/A, strain ε = x/L, and Young modulus Y = σ/ε = FL/(Ax) in the proportional region. Stress and modulus have units Pa; strain is dimensionless. If using a stress–strain graph, its initial slope is Y. Ultimate tensile strength is the maximum tensile stress supported. Elastic deformation recovers on unloading; plastic deformation leaves a permanent change. The elastic limit and limit of proportionality describe different properties even when close together. Distinguish a brittle material's small plastic region from a ductile material's substantial plastic extension, and recognise the different shape of polymer response. Use the relevant loading/unloading curve to discuss recoverable and dissipated energy.

3. Charge, electrical circuits, waves and quantum phenomena

Current and circuits

Current is charge flow rate: I = ΔQ/Δt, so constant current transfers Q = It. A proton carries +e and an electron −e; for a net ordinary object's charge, Q is an integer multiple of e. Metallic current involves electrons; electrolytes involve ions. Conventional current follows positive-charge motion, opposite electron drift. For a uniform metal conductor, I = nAe vdrift, with n free-electron number density in m⁻³, A cross-sectional area and vdrift mean drift speed. This follows by counting carriers in a length vdriftΔt. Carrier density helps distinguish conductors, insulators and semiconductors; temperature effects need not involve an assumed constant n.

Potential difference is energy transferred per charge between points, V = W/Q. Source e.m.f. ℰ is energy supplied per charge; neither is a mechanical force. A particle accelerated from rest through a suitable potential difference gains kinetic energy |q|ΔV = ½mv² if other energy transfers and relativistic corrections are negligible. One electronvolt is the energy gained by charge magnitude e through 1 V: 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

Resistance R = V/I is a component property at a specified operating state. Ohm's law asserts V ∝ I when conditions, especially temperature, are constant. On an I-against-V graph, an ohmic resistor has slope 1/R; for a nonlinear component V/I at a point is not generally the local tangent's inverse slope. A filament's resistance increases as it heats, an NTC thermistor's resistance falls with temperature, an LDR's falls as illumination increases, and a diode/LED conducts mainly under forward bias. Know circuit symbols and meter connections: an ammeter is in series and a voltmeter across the measured component. Common symbols include a cell's unequal parallel lines (long line positive), repeated cells for a battery, a rectangle for a resistor, a resistor with an adjustment arrow for variable resistance, A or V within a circle for meters, a circle with a cross for a lamp, and parallel equal lines for an unpolarised capacitor. An LED is a diode symbol with outward light arrows; an LDR uses inward light arrows. A junction dot distinguishes connected conductors from crossings without connection. Recognise the switch, thermistor and supply forms and preserve component polarity in an actual diagram.

For a uniform wire, R = ρelectricalL/A; resistivity has units Ω m. Increasing metal temperature normally increases resistivity through more lattice scattering; semiconductor/NTC behaviour can decrease resistance as mobile carrier availability increases. Keep electrical resistivity distinct from mass density despite the shared symbol ρ.

Kirchhoff's junction law follows charge conservation: total current in equals total current out. The loop law follows energy conservation: signed potential rises and falls sum to zero. Series components share current and their voltages add; parallel branches share voltage and their currents add. Hence resistors in series have Rtotal = ΣR and in parallel 1/Rtotal = Σ(1/R). Use these relations or simultaneous loop/junction equations for mixed circuits and circuits with more than one e.m.f. source.

For a source delivering current, terminal voltage V = ℰ − Ir; I = ℰ/(Rexternal + r). Internal power loss is I²r. The measured terminal voltage approaches ℰ as current tends to zero. For a two-resistor unloaded divider with output across R₂, Vout = VinR₂/(R₁ + R₂); a sensor changes this ratio. Swapping the output component reverses the response. If a finite-resistance load is connected, combine it in parallel with the output resistor before using the divider relation. A potentiometer is a continuously adjustable divider.

Electrical power P = VI = I²R = V²/R, where the latter forms use the same component's V = IR. Energy = VIt for constant values. A kilowatt-hour is 3.6 × 10⁶ J; multiply energy in the billing unit by cost per unit. Compare useful energy and input when discussing appliance efficiency.

Waves and optics

A progressive wave transfers energy while its medium undergoes local oscillation. Transverse displacement is perpendicular to travel; longitudinal displacement is parallel, producing compressions and rarefactions. Distinguish a snapshot against position from one point's displacement against time. Amplitude is maximum displacement, wavelength the repeat distance, period the repeat time, f = 1/T and wave speed v = fλ. A spatial separation Δx gives phase difference 2πΔx/λ and a time separation Δt gives 2πΔt/T, for the same periodic wave. Intensity = power/area and is proportional to amplitude squared for a fixed medium and wave type.

Reflection redirects a wave at a boundary. Refraction changes direction when speed changes at an oblique boundary; frequency remains set by the source, so wavelength changes. Diffraction spreads waves at apertures or edges and is conspicuous when wavelength is comparable with the aperture width. Polarisation restricts transverse oscillation directions and provides evidence that electromagnetic waves are transverse; a longitudinal sound wave cannot be plane-polarised in this sense.

Know the electromagnetic spectrum in order of increasing frequency: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma ray. All travel at c in vacuum; order-of-magnitude wavelength knowledge is expected. Useful approximate scales are radio wavelengths from metres upward; microwaves from millimetres to decimetres; infrared around micrometres and longer; visible roughly 400–700 nm; ultraviolet from tens to hundreds of nanometres; X-rays around 10⁻¹¹–10⁻⁸ m; and gamma commonly shorter. These are approximate categories with overlap, rather than exact universal boundaries; gamma and X-rays can be distinguished by production mechanism. Explain polarising filters for light and conducting grilles for microwaves.

For refractive index n = c/v, Snell's law is n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, with both angles from the normal. Total internal reflection requires travel toward a lower refractive index and incidence greater than the critical angle. In general sin C = n₂/n₁; the booklet's sin C = 1/n assumes the second medium is air/vacuum. Use rectangular or semicircular blocks to trace rays and establish these relations.

Superposition means simultaneous displacements add algebraically. Coherent sources maintain a fixed phase relationship and the same frequency. For initially in-phase sources, constructive interference occurs at path difference mλ and destructive interference at (m + ½)λ. Complete cancellation additionally requires equal amplitudes. Young's double slits have fringe spacing x ≈ λD/a, for slit separation a, screen distance D and a geometry with small observation angles and D much greater than a. Measure several spacings to reduce fractional uncertainty.

A grating with spacing d gives maxima d sin θ = mλ, with θ measured from the straight-through direction and m an integer order. Convert lines per unit length to spacing before substitution. A possible order must satisfy |mλ/d| ≤ 1. The grating equation is A-level content, even though it appears in the astrophysics section as well as practical wave work; proving it and knowing spectrometer construction are not required.

Standing waves form through superposition of waves of the same frequency travelling oppositely. Nodes have zero oscillation; antinodes have maximum amplitude. Adjacent nodes or adjacent antinodes are λ/2 apart; a node and its adjacent antinode are λ/4 apart. Points between the same adjacent nodes oscillate in phase; adjacent loops have opposite phase. The ideal standing pattern has no net transfer of energy along it.

For a string fixed at both ends, L = mλ/2 and fm = mv/(2L), m = 1, 2, 3, …. For ideal open-open air tubes, the displacement pattern has antinodes at both ends and the same harmonic sequence; closed-open tubes have a displacement node at the closed end and antinode at the open end, so L = (2m − 1)λ/4 and only odd harmonics of v/(4L). Real tube openings require an end correction; consecutive resonance lengths in a closed-open tube differ by λ/2, which can remove a constant end-correction offset.

Photons and matter waves

A photon carries E = hf = hc/λ. Increased monochromatic intensity means more photons per second, rather than more energy per photon. The photoelectric effect involves one photon transferring energy to one surface electron. With work function φ, hf = φ + Kmax; threshold frequency f₀ = φ/h. Below threshold, the simple one-photon mechanism emits no photoelectrons however large the intensity. Above threshold, increasing frequency increases maximum kinetic energy, while increasing intensity at fixed frequency increases emission rate. Prompt emission and these dependencies support the photon model. If stopping potential is supplied, Kmax = eVs connects it to the emitted electrons' maximum energy.

For the LED experiment, treat eVthreshold ≈ hf as an experimental estimate, not an exact universal semiconductor law: a graph of threshold voltage against frequency estimates h/e. Detailed band theory is not required. For particles, de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p; combine with p = mv and accelerating-potential energy for nonrelativistic electrons. Graphite electron diffraction supports matter-wave behaviour. A greater accelerating voltage increases momentum and decreases wavelength. Wave-particle duality is usable qualitative and quantitative A-level knowledge; wavefunctions, operators, probability amplitudes and the Schrödinger equation are not assumed.

4. Thermal physics, circular motion, oscillations and gravitation

Temperature, internal energy and gases

Thermal equilibrium means no net thermal energy transfer between systems in thermal contact; their temperatures agree. Absolute temperature uses kelvin, approximately T/K = θ/°C + 273. A temperature interval of 1 K equals 1 °C, but gas equations require absolute T. Solids, liquids and gases differ in particle ordering, spacing and motion; Brownian motion is irregular visible-particle movement caused by molecular impacts.

Internal energy is the total microscopic random kinetic and interaction potential energy. Heating without a phase change generally increases temperature and microscopic kinetic energy. During a phase change at constant pressure, supplied energy can change intermolecular potential energy while temperature remains constant. Absolute zero is the lower temperature limit and minimum internal energy, not a licence to assert that every microscopic energy is literally zero.

Heat transferred for a temperature change is Q = mcΔT, with c in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, using a constant or appropriate average specific heat capacity for the stated material, state and process. A gas's heat capacity can differ between constant-volume and constant-pressure heating. Phase-change heat is Q = mℓ, with ℓ in J kg⁻¹ for the stated transition and conditions; distinguish fusion from vaporisation. These heat-input expressions do not by themselves assert Q = ΔU: a gas can also do expansion work on its surroundings. For a specified closed system with no change in bulk kinetic or gravitational potential energy, whose only transfers are heat input Q and work output W, energy conservation gives ΔU = Q − W. This is a conservation deduction when the transfers and work are supplied or established, rather than a grant of further thermodynamic identities or unprovided process-specific heat capacities. A process crossing a phase boundary can require several heat terms that must be added. Electrical input VIt equals the relevant sample energy gain only after considering work, losses and heating of containers or heaters.

Amount n = N/NA, where N is particle count and NA Avogadro's constant. The ideal-gas model uses many rapidly and randomly moving particles with negligible volume, negligible interparticle forces except during collisions, elastic collisions and short collision durations. Pressure results from particle momentum changes at container walls. pV = nRT = NkT, with k = R/NA. For a fixed particle number and composition, pV is constant when temperature is fixed, and p/T is constant when volume is fixed. Identify all held-fixed quantities before applying a gas-law shortcut; fixed volume alone does not make p/T constant if particles are added, removed or changed in number by a reaction.

Kinetic theory gives pV = ⅓Nm⟨v²⟩ for a population of particles with a common mass m. If it is applied to one species in an ideal mixture, N, m and ⟨v²⟩ refer to that species and p is its contribution to pressure; the species' pressure contributions add. Root-mean-square speed is √⟨v²⟩, not the mean speed. Combining the two pressure expressions gives mean translational kinetic energy ½m⟨v²⟩ = 3kT/2 and vrms = √(3kT/m). At a common temperature, ideal species have the same mean translational kinetic energy but different rms speeds when their masses differ; do not use one species' mass as a common particle mass for an arbitrary mixture. The A-level translational particle model gives total kinetic contribution 3NkT/2; applying this as the complete internal energy needs the corresponding model, rather than an unspoken claim about all real polyatomic gases. Increasing T broadens a Maxwell–Boltzmann speed distribution for the stated species and shifts its characteristic speeds upward, while its area remains tied to that particle number. The qualitative shape is required; deriving the distribution or the kinetic pressure equation is not.

Circular motion and simple harmonic motion

A full turn is 2π radians; arc length is rθ. For uniform circular motion, ω = 2π/T = 2πf and speed v = ωr. Velocity changes direction even when speed is constant. Acceleration magnitude v²/r = ω²r points toward the centre, requiring net inward force mv²/r. Identify which actual forces supply this resultant: gravity, tension, friction or field force. 'Centripetal force' describes their resultant role and should not be added as an extra force. Uniform circular motion requires a force of constant magnitude whose direction changes around the circle.

Simple harmonic motion satisfies a = −ω²x, where x is measured from stable equilibrium. Its restoring acceleration is proportional to displacement and opposite in direction. Use x = A cos ωt or A sin ωt with an appropriate time origin/phase. Speed is |v| = ω√(A² − x²); the sign depends on the direction of motion. At equilibrium speed is maximum ωA and acceleration zero; at either extreme speed is zero and acceleration magnitude ω²A. Displacement and acceleration are opposite in phase; velocity is a quarter-cycle out of phase with displacement. Ideal SHM period is amplitude-independent within the model's validity.

For an ideal spring oscillator, combine F = −kx and F = ma to obtain ω² = k/m and T = 2π√(m/k). Potential and kinetic energies trade while total energy ½kA² stays constant; their displacement graphs are ½kx² and ½k(A² − x²). Timing real spring or pendulum motion is required practical experience. A small-angle pendulum can be explained using the supplied/derived restoring-force model; the specification does not separately list memorisation of its period formula as a learning outcome.

Free oscillations occur at a system's natural frequency; a driving force produces forced oscillations. Damping dissipates mechanical energy, reducing free-oscillation amplitude. At resonance a driving frequency near the natural frequency produces a large response; damping reduces and broadens the resonance peak. Relate the driving-frequency/amplitude graph to practical systems. The algebraic SHM results and energy plots are baseline knowledge; solving its differential equation, or general damped/forced oscillator equations, is not a Physics A prerequisite.

Gravity and orbital motion

Gravitational field strength is force per unit test mass, g = F/m, in N kg⁻¹, numerically equivalent to free-fall acceleration. Fields point toward attracting masses. Outside a spherically symmetric mass distribution, use its total mass at its centre: field magnitude GM/r² and force magnitude GMm/r². r is centre-to-centre separation, not height above the surface. With outward radial direction positive, the radial force and field components are negative. Close to Earth's surface, modest height changes permit the uniform-g approximation.

For a circular orbit dominated by central mass M, gravity supplies the inward resultant, so v = √(GM/r) and T² = 4π²r³/(GM). Derive this by equating force expressions, rather than merely recognising it. Kepler's laws describe elliptical orbits with the central body at a focus, equal areas swept in equal times, and period squared proportional to the cube of semimajor axis. The circular formula uses radius; the ellipse generalisation uses semimajor axis. A geostationary satellite must have an equatorial circular orbit, follow Earth's rotation in the same direction and match its rotation period; matching the period alone is insufficient.

Set gravitational potential zero at infinity. For a point/exterior spherical mass, Vg = −GM/r is potential energy per unit mass and U = mVg = −GMm/r. Calculate energy changes from final minus initial values; moving outward raises potential energy toward zero. Graphical area under a force–distance curve gives work with a carefully stated force direction and work convention. Escape to infinity with zero final speed requires ½mvesc² = GMm/r, giving vesc = √(2GM/r) if drag, rotation and other masses are neglected. The formulas for gravitational potential and escape speed are explicitly A-level content.

5. Stars and cosmology

Distinguish planets, their satellites, comets, solar systems, galaxies and the universe. Stellar formation begins with gravity gathering gas and dust; heating leads to fusion, and a main-sequence star balances inward gravitational effects with pressure associated with hot matter and radiation. Hydrogen fusion supplies energy. Low-mass evolution can proceed through a red giant and loss of outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving a white dwarf. A white dwarf is supported by electron degeneracy pressure: quantum restrictions on electron states oppose further compression, even without fusion sustaining ordinary thermal pressure. The Chandrasekhar limit is approximately 1.4 solar masses; above it that form of support cannot maintain a white dwarf. Massive stars can become red supergiants, undergo supernova and leave neutron stars or black holes. Know the characteristics of these remnants qualitatively; a neutron star is an extremely compact, dense, neutron-rich remnant, and a black hole has an event horizon from which light cannot escape.

An HR diagram plots luminosity against surface temperature; temperature conventionally decreases to the right. Locate main-sequence stars, red giants/supergiants and white dwarfs and explain how stars of similar temperature can have very different luminosities through their radii.

An isolated atom has discrete electron energy levels. Taking the free electron as zero makes bound energies negative. An emission transition to a lower level produces photon energy |ΔE| = hf = hc/λ; absorption requires a matching energy gap. Use the gap, not one level's value relative to zero, unless ionisation is the transition. Continuous, emission-line and absorption-line spectra have different origins; line patterns identify stellar elements.

For a black-body approximation, Wien's relation is λmaxT = constant, so hotter surfaces peak at shorter wavelength. The specification/booklet supplies the proportionality λmax ∝ 1/T; use a given Wien constant or comparison data when required. Stellar luminosity L = 4πRstar²σT⁴ is total emitted power; received flux at distance d is L/(4πd²) for isotropic emission without intervening loss. Combine spectral peak, luminosity or observed flux and distance to estimate surface temperature and radius. Do not confuse star radius with distance to the observer.

Use AU, light-year and parsec as distance units. Stellar parallax angle is the half-angle of the apparent annual shift; d in parsecs = 1/p in arcseconds. One arcsecond is 1/3600 degree. Apply the small-angle geometry carefully to a one-AU baseline.

For electromagnetic Doppler shifts at speeds small compared with c, recession gives Δλ/λ ≈ v/c, while the signed frequency change has the opposite sign; the booklet writes the fractional shifts in magnitude form. Hubble's relation v ≈ H₀d connects measured recession and distance in its applicable regime. Convert km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ to s⁻¹ before using 1/H₀ as a time estimate. A Hubble age is an estimate, not an exact age for every expansion history.

The cosmological principle treats the universe as homogeneous and isotropic on large scales, with universal physical laws. Galactic redshifts support expansion; the approximately 2.7 K microwave background supports a hot early universe through its nearly uniform thermal spectrum, interpreted as early radiation cooled/redshifted by expansion. Describe an early hot dense phase, cooling/expansion, formation of nuclei and atoms, and later structure and stars. The Big Bang describes expansion of space-time; it is not an ordinary blast from a privileged centre into pre-existing empty space. Know that ordinary matter is a small part of the current model. Dark matter is inferred through gravitational effects without the ordinary detectable light emission that would explain its mass; dark energy is the model's contribution associated with accelerated cosmic expansion. Detailed cosmological dynamics, tensor relativity and quantitative degeneracy theory are beyond this baseline.

6. Capacitors, electric and magnetic fields

Capacitance and exponential processes

For a linear capacitor, C = Q/V, where ±Q are equal-magnitude opposite plate charges and V the potential difference. The capacitor is net neutral overall in the usual charging circuit; electrons move through the external circuit between plates rather than crossing the insulating gap. Parallel capacitances add because voltages agree and charges add. Series capacitors carry equal charge in the ordinary initially uncharged arrangement, their voltages add, and 1/Ctotal = Σ(1/C). Analyse capacitor/resistor circuits using those charge, current and voltage constraints.

Energy is area under the V-against-Q graph: U = ½QV = Q²/(2C) = ½CV². Changing C has different consequences depending on whether a source fixes V or an isolated capacitor fixes Q. State which condition applies before predicting changes.

For constant R and C, a discharge has Q = Q₀e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ, V = V₀e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ and current magnitude I = I₀e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ. Current direction depends on the adopted convention; a signed charge-removal rate is ΔQ/Δt ≈ −Q/(RC). The time constant τ = RC is the time to reach 1/e of an initial decaying value. Charging from zero through R from constant supply ℰ gives Vcapacitor = ℰ(1 − e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ), Q = Cℰ(1 − e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ), while current decays as (ℰ/R)e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ. The charging expression does not apply to the current. At one time constant the capacitor has reached about 63% of its final charge. Use ln V versus time for discharge to obtain slope −1/(RC); solve for times by logarithms. Model successive small changes in a spreadsheet without presupposing differential-equation solving.

Electric fields and potential

Electric field strength is force per unit positive test charge, E = F/q; its units are N C⁻¹ or V m⁻¹. Field arrows show the force direction on a positive charge, so a negative charge accelerates opposite to the arrows. A point charge Q gives radial field Q/(4πε₀r²), force on q equal to qE, and potential V = Q/(4πε₀r), with zero at infinity. A positive source has outward field and positive potential; a negative source has inward field and negative potential. Potentials add as scalars and fields as vectors; charges can attract or repel, while the gravitational point-mass force is attractive.

For broad parallel plates away from edges, uniform field magnitude is ΔV/d. A charged particle experiences constant acceleration qE/m, so link the field to one-dimensional acceleration or projectile-like deflection. Energy U = qV and work by the electric field is −ΔU. These signs matter for electron motion. Outside a uniformly charged sphere, model charge at its centre; that exterior formula should not be applied indiscriminately inside it.

For a parallel-plate capacitor, C = εA/d, with ε = εrε₀, reducing to ε₀A/d in vacuum/approximately air. A dielectric with greater permittivity increases C at fixed geometry; detailed microscopic dielectric theory is not required. For an isolated conducting sphere of radius R, surface potential Q/(4πε₀R) and C = Q/V give C = 4πε₀R. Force–distance graph areas and potential differences describe the same work when the sign convention is maintained.

Magnetic forces and induction

Moving charges and permanent magnets create magnetic fields. Recognise the field patterns around a straight current, a coil and a solenoid, including their direction relative to current. Magnetic flux density B is measured in tesla. A straight wire length L carrying current I in uniform B has force magnitude BIL sin θ, where θ is the angle between current and field. Fleming's left-hand rule gives the force direction for conventional current.

For a charge moving perpendicular to B, force magnitude is |q|vB, perpendicular to both velocity and field; reverse its direction for negative charge. A magnetic force changes direction rather than speed and does no work. Equating it to mv²/r gives r = mv/(|q|B) for the circular orbit. The explicitly specified charged-particle case is perpendicular motion; general vector products are not presupposed. In crossed fields arranged to oppose, a straight-through velocity selector has |q|E = |q|vB and selected speed v = E/B.

Magnetic flux through a flat area is Φ = BA cos θ for uniform B, with θ measured between field and the area's normal. Flux linkage is NΦ for N identical linked turns; Φ is in Wb. An induced e.m.f. arises when linkage changes: mean ℰ = −Δ(NΦ)/Δt, or use the tangent rate at a specified instant. The minus sign expresses Lenz's opposition to the change in flux, consistent with energy conservation; it does not mean opposition to an existing field in every case. Change B, area, orientation or relative position to induce an e.m.f. A search coil's induced-voltage/time area measures a linkage change.

A simple alternating-current generator uses rotating coil linkage to generate alternating e.m.f.; faster rotation or larger linked flux gives greater magnitude. A transformer uses changing primary current and flux in a core to induce secondary e.m.f. For an ideal transformer, Vs/Vp = Ns/Np = Ip/Is and input power equals output power. A step-up voltage reduces current for the same transferred power; reduced line current reduces I²R transmission loss. Laminations reduce eddy-current heating; real transformers have losses. Constant DC does not sustain transformer action after the initial change.

7. Nuclear and particle physics, and medical imaging

Nuclear structure and particle rules

Alpha scattering supports a small dense positively charged nucleus: most particles pass through, while a small fraction deflect strongly. An atom has a scale around 10⁻¹⁰ m and a nucleus around 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m. In nuclide notation ᴬZX, Z is proton number, A is total nucleon number and neutron number is A − Z. Isotopes share Z but differ in neutron number. Nuclear radius R = r₀A¹ᐟ³ implies nuclear volume proportional to A and approximately constant nuclear density. Nuclear strong-force behaviour is short range, attractive over roughly 0.5–3 fm and repulsive at still shorter separations.

An antiparticle has the same mass as its partner and opposite relevant quantum properties; electron/positron and proton/antiproton have opposite charge. Hadrons, including protons and neutrons, participate in the strong and weak interactions; leptons, including electrons and neutrinos, do not participate in the strong interaction but can participate in the weak. The simple quark model uses u, d and s and their antiquarks: u has +2e/3; d and s have −e/3; antiquark charges reverse. Proton = uud and neutron = udd. Add constituent charges and check charge conservation in proposed transformations.

Beta-minus changes d → u + e⁻ + electron antineutrino, corresponding to n → p + e⁻ + antineutrino. Beta-plus changes u → d + e⁺ + electron neutrino, corresponding to p → n + e⁺ + neutrino in an energetically allowed decay. In beta-minus, nuclide A remains fixed and Z rises by one; beta-plus lowers Z by one. Alpha emission decreases A by four and Z by two. Gamma emission changes nuclear energy without changing A or Z. Balance nucleon and charge numbers, include the relevant beta neutrino, and recognise that beta particles are not pre-existing orbital electrons expelled from the atom.

Radioactivity and nuclear energy

Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random: a single nucleus's decay time cannot be predicted, but a large population has a predictable average behaviour. Ionisation removes electrons from atoms or molecules. Alpha particles are helium nuclei, highly ionising, travelling only a few centimetres in air and stopped by paper or the outer layer of skin; beta particles are electrons/positrons with intermediate penetration, typically travelling farther in air and attenuated by millimetres of suitable material such as aluminium; gamma photons are more penetrating and less densely ionising, requiring substantial lead/concrete to reduce intensity rather than having a sharp universal stopping range. Actual ranges depend on energy and material. Identify appropriate absorbers and distinguish external from internal exposure in the stated context. Correct a detector's count rate for background and recognise that count rate is not automatically the source's total activity because efficiency and geometry intervene.

Activity A = λN is the expected decay rate in Bq, where N is the number of undecayed nuclei at that time and λ is the isotope's constant conditional decay rate per surviving nucleus, in s⁻¹. N = N₀e⁻λt, A = A₀e⁻λt and t½ = ln 2/λ. Given that a nucleus has survived to the start of an interval Δt, the exponential survival law gives probability 1 − e⁻λΔt of decay during that interval; this is approximately λΔt only when λΔt is small compared with one. Thus the usual phrase 'decay probability per unit time' describes a rate, rather than an exact finite-interval probability. Use either repeated halvings or logarithms; the same fraction disappears in equal time intervals. ln(activity) against time has slope −λ only after background and other relevant contributions are dealt with. A small-time spreadsheet model uses ΔN ≈ −λNΔt under the same small-λΔt condition. Dating assumes an initial isotopic ratio/activity and a closed-system history adequate for the model; the measured fraction gives t = ln(N₀/N)/λ. Simple probability reasoning and dice models are part of this level; the interval-probability expression follows directly from the stated survival law and does not assume advanced probability theory.

Mass defect is the separate constituents' mass minus the bound nucleus's mass; binding energy is Δmc². Use compatible masses, especially whether quoted masses are nuclear or atomic and whether electron masses cancel. Convert kg, unified atomic mass units and eV consistently. Binding energy per nucleon measures how strongly bound a nucleus is on average, not the energy of one chosen nucleon. A reaction moving products toward higher total binding energy can release energy: fission of heavy nuclei and fusion of light ones. Calculate reaction energy from the total initial mass minus final mass times c², or from consistent total binding energies; distinguish energy per nucleon from total energy.

Induced fission can emit neutrons that continue a chain reaction. Fuel supplies fissile nuclei, a moderator slows neutrons where the reactor design requires it, and control rods absorb neutrons to control the chain. Explain heat transfer and waste implications in context. Fusion combines light nuclei and requires sufficiently energetic encounters to overcome electrostatic repulsion; high temperature and confinement matter. Balance the nuclear equations.

Particle–antiparticle annihilation converts rest energy and any kinetic energy into other products while conserving energy and momentum. Pair creation requires sufficient energy for both rest masses and a way to conserve momentum. For an electron–positron pair at rest, two opposed annihilation photons each have approximately 511 keV. This is an application of E = mc², not an assumed relativistic-mechanics course.

Imaging models and calculations

In an X-ray tube, a heated cathode supplies electrons; a high voltage accelerates them toward a metal anode target. Electron deceleration produces a continuous spectrum and atomic transitions can give characteristic lines. Maximum photon energy cannot exceed eVaccelerating, so λminimum = hc/(eV) under that model. Most input energy heats the target.

X-ray attenuation includes scattering, photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering and, at sufficiently high photon energy, electron–positron pair creation. Know the physical distinctions: photoelectric absorption removes the incident photon; Compton scattering transfers some energy to an electron and leaves a lower-energy scattered photon; simple scattering changes direction; pair creation makes particles. The quantitative Compton wavelength-shift formula is not required.

For constant attenuation coefficient μ and the specified beam/material model, I = I₀e⁻μx; μ has inverse-length units. A plot of ln(I/I₀) against thickness has slope −μ, and half-value thickness is ln 2/μ. Keep thickness units consistent. Different attenuation across tissues produces an image; barium or iodine contrast increases the relevant visibility. A plain radiograph combines structures along a line of sight; a CAT/CT scanner acquires many angular X-ray measurements with a rotating tube, thin fan beam and detectors, then reconstructs slices, reducing superposition ambiguity.

A tracer's emissions reveal its distribution/function in the body. Know technetium-99m and fluorine-18 as the specified examples; a useful choice considers detectable radiation, biological targeting and a half-life long enough for the examination but short enough to limit dose. A gamma camera uses a collimator to select directions, a scintillator to convert gamma detection to light, photomultiplier tubes to produce/amplify electrical signals, and a computer to form an image. In PET, positron-emitting tracer decays are followed by annihilation; nearly opposing photon detections in coincidence identify a line on which the event occurred, and many events form a reconstructed activity image. Imaging geometry does not locate one event at an exact point without additional information.

Ultrasound is a longitudinal wave above 20 kHz. A piezoelectric transducer converts electrical signals into ultrasound and returning sound into electrical signals. Echo timing gives depth = cΔt/2 because travel is outward and back. An A-scan displays echo amplitude against delay/depth; a B-scan combines lines to map brightness in a section.

Acoustic impedance Z = ρc. For the stated normal-incidence boundary model, the reflected intensity fraction is (Z₂ − Z₁)²/(Z₂ + Z₁)². Large mismatch produces a large reflection; coupling gel removes an intervening air gap and improves transmission. In the assessed low-speed Doppler ultrasound model, |Δf|/f = 2v|cos θ|/c, where f is transmitted frequency, v is blood-flow speed, c is sound speed, v is small compared with c, and θ is the angle between the directed flow and beam. Approaching the transducer increases reflected frequency; moving away decreases it. Solve for v, account for the round-trip factor two, and recognise that perpendicular flow gives no Doppler shift in this model. If θ is explicitly the acute angle between the flow line and beam, cos θ is already nonnegative. Ultrasound avoids ionising radiation, while X-ray, gamma camera and PET techniques involve it; compare choices using the anatomical/function information and context given, not a universal ranking of scanners. MRI is not part of the H556 imaging content listed here.

8. What may be assumed when first-year teaching begins

The student can provisionally be expected to select equations, justify their assumptions, connect multiple topic areas, use a diagram and graph, carry units and signs through a calculation, and explain physical mechanisms in a coherent argument. A question can combine acceleration through a p.d., a magnetic orbit and a photon transition; it need not be presented as a familiar single-topic exercise. Practical methods and evidence evaluation apply throughout. The 2025 Unified physics examiner report supports testing multi-step reasoning, unfamiliar contexts, data selection, unit prefixes and contextual explanations.

Physics A itself does not establish fluency in formal differentiation/integration, vector dot/cross notation, differential-equation solving, linear algebra, Maxwell's equations, thermodynamic identities or entropy, relativistic spacetime, quantum operators or advanced statistical data analysis. It does establish rate/area reasoning graphically, exponential and logarithmic calculations, component vectors, algebraic SHM, inverse-square fields and potentials, and the substantive nuclear/medical/astrophysical models above. A student taking A-level Mathematics or Further Mathematics may already possess additional formal methods; those belong to that separate subject baseline.

No fixed threshold score or claimed personal mastery follows from curriculum completion. First-year diagnostic testing should distinguish lack of retained knowledge, incorrect assumptions/signs, weak algebra and unfamiliar formalism rather than treating every university-labelled topic as new.

Course completion is a declared starting assumption. Later notes must supply new chemical premises beyond these boundaries before they can be used. New information given in a question may be combined with these rules. The external source links are provenance, not extra lookup inputs, and the complete periodic table and spectral data sheet are not assumed memorised. For each assessment, the visible notes must include any needed relative atomic masses A_r, atomic numbers or other periodic data not stated here, and any finer IR/NMR reference ranges needed beyond the approximate recognitions below. Required tabulated enthalpies, entropies, electrode potentials, calibration data or other numerical reference values must likewise be present in the baseline or assessment notes. An instruction to consult an absent data sheet does not supply its contents.

In formulae, square brackets such as [A] mean molar concentration, normally in mol dm^-3; n means amount in mol; nu means a stoichiometric coefficient; and a delta means final minus initial, or products minus reactants for a stated reaction. A superscript standard symbol means a value under stated standard conditions. Changes of equation direction reverse the sign of an enthalpy or entropy change; multiplying the whole equation multiplies that change.

[CH-01] 1. Quantities, calculation and representation

[CH-01.01] Units and arithmetic

Use a scientific calculator for powers, square roots, exponential functions, base-10 logarithms and natural logarithms. Rearrange equations before or during substitution, retain extra digits in working, and round the final answer to precision justified by the supplied values. A result needs units unless it is a dimensionless relative mass, ratio or pH.

- 1 kg = 1000 g; 1 g = 1000 mg; 1 tonne = 10^6 g.
- 1 dm^3 = 1000 cm^3 = 10^-3 m^3; 1 cm^3 = 10^-6 m^3. Convert cm^3 to dm^3 before using concentration in mol dm^-3.
- 1 kPa = 1000 Pa; 1 kJ = 1000 J. Use kelvin for gas, Arrhenius and Gibbs equations: T/K = temperature/degree C + 273, with 273.15 if the precision calls for it. A temperature interval has the same numerical value in K and degree C.
- Compound units obey ordinary exponent rules: (mol dm^-3)^2 = mol^2 dm^-6. Determine unfamiliar constant units by substituting the units of each measured quantity into the rearranged equation.
- A number written a x 10^b is in standard form when 1 <= a < 10. Trailing zeroes after a decimal point can carry precision: 0.0050 has two significant figures.

Use ratios rather than treating every chemical equation as 1:1. Keep a label for each intermediate amount or concentration. Draw a line, smooth curve or tangent as appropriate to a graph; use labelled axes and units, a sensible scale, a large gradient triangle, and gradient = change in vertical quantity / change in horizontal quantity. A tangent gives an instantaneous rate of change; a chord gives an average.

[CH-01.02] Atoms, isotopes and relative masses

An atom contains protons (+1 charge, relative mass approximately 1), neutrons (zero charge, relative mass approximately 1) and electrons (-1 charge, much smaller mass). Atomic number Z is proton number. Mass number A is proton plus neutron number. For a species with signed ionic charge z, electron number = Z - z: a 2+ ion has two fewer electrons than the neutral atom and a 1- ion one more. Neutrons = A - Z.

Isotopes share Z but have different neutron numbers and mass numbers. They generally have the same electron structure and chemical behaviour; their masses differ. Relative isotopic mass compares the mass of one isotope with one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Relative atomic mass A_r is an abundance-weighted mean on that scale:

$$A_r=\frac{\sum_i f_i m_i}{\sum_i f_i}.$$

Here m_i is relative isotopic mass and f_i is relative abundance, a percentage, fraction or consistent intensity measure. A_r and relative molecular/formula mass M_r have no units. Obtain M_r by adding each element's A_r multiplied by its count in the formula. A molecular mass refers to a molecule; formula mass is appropriate for an ionic or other extended structure.

A mass spectrum places mass-to-charge ratio m/z on the horizontal axis and relative abundance on the vertical axis. For the singly charged ions considered here, m/z is numerically the ion's relative mass. Isotope masses and relative peak heights permit the weighted mean calculation. An ion's electron count must reflect its charge even when the spectrum concerns an isotope. The mechanism of the mass spectrometer itself is not part of this starting toolkit.

[CH-01.03] Formulae, balanced equations and the mole

An ionic compound has zero net charge. Choose the lowest whole-number ion ratio that cancels charges, and bracket a polyatomic ion when more than one is needed: Ca(NO3)2 and Al2(SO4)3. Common ions include nitrate NO3-, carbonate CO3^2-, sulfate SO4^2-, hydroxide OH-, ammonium NH4+, silver Ag+ and zinc Zn^2+. Main-group metals commonly form +1 or +2 ions; aluminium forms +3, halogens -1, oxygen -2. Use the formula or supplied charge for species outside the usual rules.

Balance equations by changing coefficients, never the internal formula of a substance. Conserve every element and, for ionic equations, total charge. State symbols are (s), (l), (g) and (aq). To obtain a net ionic equation, split dissolved strong electrolytes into ions and cancel unchanged spectator ions. Do not split solids, gases or molecular water into invented ions. Use supplied observations or products to construct an unfamiliar equation.

One mole contains the Avogadro number of specified entities. Use N_A = 6.02 x 10^23 mol^-1 at OCR data-sheet precision. Always identify whether the entities are molecules, atoms, ions or formula units. The molar mass M has units g mol^-1 and is numerically equal to M_r when measured in those units.

$$N=nN_A,\qquad n=\frac mM,\qquad n=cV,\qquad c_{\rm mass}=cM.$$

Here N is particle count; m is mass in g; c is molar concentration in mol dm^-3; V is solution volume in dm^3; and c_mass is mass concentration in g dm^-3. At room temperature and pressure (RTP), the molar gas volume is approximately 24.0 dm^3 mol^-1, so n = V_gas/(24.0 dm^3 mol^-1). This RTP approximation is not a value for every temperature and pressure.

For a gas at specified temperature and pressure use

$$pV=nRT,$$

with p in Pa, V in m^3, n in mol, T in K and R = 8.314 J mol^-1 K^-1. Convert both pressure and volume consistently. This is an ideal-gas model; it is not a grant of a real-gas equation of state.

For a balanced reaction, divide the available moles of each possible limiting reactant by its coefficient. The smallest quotient fixes the maximum reaction extent. Product moles equal that extent times the product coefficient. Excess reactant remains after subtracting its reacted amount. In a dilution, solute moles stay constant, so c1 V1 = c2 V2 if there is no reaction or loss.

To obtain an empirical formula, divide each element's mass by its A_r, divide all mole amounts by the smallest, and multiply the ratios if necessary to obtain small whole numbers. Do not round a ratio such as 1.50 directly to 2. The empirical formula gives the simplest atom ratio. A molecular formula is the empirical formula multiplied by M_r/(empirical formula mass), a whole number. For a hydrated salt, find water lost and remaining anhydrous salt separately, convert each to moles, and calculate their ratio. Heating to constant mass tests whether mass loss has finished; decomposition or loss of material can invalidate a hydration calculation.

$$\%\,\text{yield}=100\frac{\text{actual amount of product}}{\text{theoretical amount of product}},\qquad
\%\,\text{atom economy}=100\frac{\nu_{\rm useful}M_{\rm useful}}{\sum_{\rm products}\nu_i M_i}.$$

Use consistent amounts or masses of the same product in the yield ratio. Theoretical yield comes from the limiting reactant. Atom economy uses the balanced reaction, not the actual isolated yield: it measures the fraction of reactant mass incorporated in desired product. A high atom economy can reduce material waste but does not by itself establish low energy use, low toxicity or a high practical yield.

[CH-02] 2. Electrons, bonds, structure and periodic patterns

[CH-02.01] Electron structure

Shell n can hold up to 2n^2 electrons: capacities of the first four shells are 2, 8, 18 and 32. A shell contains sub-shells; an s sub-shell has one orbital and capacity 2, p has three and capacity 6, and d has five and capacity 10. An orbital can contain two electrons with opposite spins. s orbitals are spherical; p orbitals have two lobes with different orientations. In equal-energy orbitals, occupy them singly before pairing, with parallel spins for the single electrons.

For the ground-state atoms covered by the course, fill 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, then 4p, subject to the Cr and Cu exceptions. Use notation such as 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6. Neutral chromium is [Ar]3d^5 4s^1; neutral copper is [Ar]3d^10 4s^1. For transition-metal cations remove 4s electrons before 3d electrons: Fe is [Ar]3d^6 4s^2, Fe^2+ is [Ar]3d^6, Fe^3+ is [Ar]3d^5. Atom and main-group ion configurations are covered to Z = 36; period-4 d-block atom and ion configurations cover Sc to Zn.

An element belongs to the s, p or d block according to the sub-shell associated with its characteristic electron filling. Across a period the proton number increases while electrons enter the same principal outer shell. Down a main-group column another occupied shell is added. Use nuclear charge, distance and shielding together when explaining attraction between nucleus and outer electrons; naming only one factor can produce a wrong trend.

The first ionisation energy is the energy to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous neutral atoms:

$$X(g)\rightarrow X^+(g)+e^-.$$

Successive ionisation energies remove electrons from successively more positive gaseous ions. A large jump after a particular number of electrons usually shows that the next electron comes from an inner shell, identifying the number of outer-shell electrons in simple main-group cases. First ionisation energy generally rises across periods 2 and 3 as effective attraction strengthens, and falls down a group as extra distance and shielding outweigh increased nuclear charge. The Be-to-B and Mg-to-Al dips arise when the removed electron enters a higher-energy p sub-shell; the N-to-O and P-to-S dips arise from repulsion between paired p electrons. State whether the comparison concerns an atom or an ion.

[CH-02.02] Ionic, covalent and metallic bonding

An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction of opposite ions. An ionic solid is an extended lattice, with attractions in all directions. In a dot-and-cross diagram show electrons transferred, complete outer shells as appropriate, brackets and ion charges. The large energy needed to separate ions gives generally high melting points. A solid does not conduct because its ions are fixed; a melt or aqueous solution can conduct because ions move. Solubility in water depends on competition between lattice disruption and hydration, so it is not correct to call every ionic solid soluble.

A covalent bond involves a shared electron pair attracted by both nuclei. In a coordinate or dative bond both electrons in the original shared pair came from one donor. After formation it is a covalent bond. Dot-and-cross diagrams show bond pairs and all relevant lone pairs; multiple bonds share two or three pairs. A larger average bond enthalpy indicates a stronger bond.

Metallic bonding involves a lattice of positive metal centres attracted to mobile delocalised electrons. Electrons explain electrical conductivity; non-directional bonding allows layers to move while bonding persists, explaining malleability. Metal melting points vary with attraction and structure; they cannot be assigned solely from a label such as 'metal'.

A simple molecular solid contains strong bonds within molecules and weaker attractions between molecules. Melting or boiling usually overcomes intermolecular attractions without breaking each molecule's covalent bonds. Such substances usually lack mobile charged particles and do not conduct. Giant covalent structures instead contain a network of strong covalent bonds, so melting demands extensive bond breaking. Diamond has four bonds per carbon and no mobile electrons. Graphite has three bonds per carbon in layers, delocalised electrons, strong within-layer bonding and weak interlayer attractions; it conducts and layers can slide. Graphene is one such sheet with high strength and conductivity. Silicon is also a giant covalent solid in this scope.

Across period 3, sodium, magnesium and aluminium have metallic lattices; silicon has a giant covalent structure; phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine form simple molecules (commonly P4, S8 and Cl2); argon is monatomic. Compare the actual particles and attractions to explain melting points. Sulfur's larger molecule has stronger dispersion attractions than phosphorus or chlorine; neither should be explained by breaking all its internal covalent bonds during ordinary melting.

[CH-02.03] Molecular shape, polarity and attractions

Electron regions around a central atom repel and adopt arrangements that minimise repulsion. A multiple bond counts as one directional bonding region when predicting shape. Lone-pair repulsion is stronger than bond-pair repulsion, so lone pairs can compress adjacent bond angles. Use a wedge for a bond towards the viewer, a dashed wedge away, and plain lines in the plane.

| Bonding regions and lone pairs | Molecular shape | Typical angle |
|---|---|---|
| 2 bonds, no lone pairs | linear | 180 degrees |
| 3 bonds, no lone pairs | trigonal planar | 120 degrees |
| 4 bonds, no lone pairs | tetrahedral | 109.5 degrees |
| 3 bonds, 1 lone pair | pyramidal, as in NH3 | about 107 degrees |
| 2 bonds, 2 lone pairs | bent/non-linear, as in H2O | about 104.5 degrees |
| 6 bonds, no lone pairs | octahedral | 90 and 180 degrees |

Electronegativity measures attraction for a shared bonding electron pair. It generally increases towards fluorine in the periodic table. In an unequal bond, label the more electronegative end delta-minus and the other delta-plus. Molecular polarity depends on the vector directions of all bond dipoles. The two equal opposite C=O dipoles in linear CO2 cancel; the O-H dipoles in bent H2O do not.

All atoms and molecules have London/dispersion attractions: fluctuating electron density produces temporary and induced dipoles. More polarisable electron clouds and greater molecular contact usually strengthen them. Larger unbranched alkanes generally boil at higher temperatures; branching of isomers reduces contact and usually lowers boiling point. Polar molecules also have permanent dipole-dipole attractions. A hydrogen bond occurs between H bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F in another suitable molecule. Show it with a dotted line and the lone-pair acceptor. It is distinct from the covalent N-H or O-H bond itself.

Hydrogen bonding raises water's melting and boiling temperatures relative to similar small molecules. Ice forms an open hydrogen-bonded network and is less dense than liquid water. Small alcohols and carboxylic acids hydrogen-bond with water; their water solubility generally decreases as a larger nonpolar hydrocarbon portion becomes dominant. When comparing boiling points, first identify which intermolecular forces are possible, then compare molecular size/contact and strength.

[CH-03] 3. Acids, redox and inorganic chemistry

[CH-03.01] Acid-base chemistry and oxidation numbers

Common acids are HCl, HNO3, H2SO4 and CH3COOH; common alkalis include NaOH, KOH and NH3(aq). A strong acid dissociates essentially completely in the relevant dilute aqueous model; a weak acid only partly dissociates. Strength is different from concentration. An acid can be weak and concentrated, or strong and dilute. A base accepts H+; an alkali is a soluble base giving OH- in aqueous solution. In the Brønsted-Lowry model an acid is a proton donor and a base a proton acceptor. Conjugate pairs differ by one H+ and corresponding unit of charge: HA/A- or BH+/B. A monobasic, dibasic or tribasic acid can supply one, two or three protons per formula unit in full neutralisation, respectively; that label does not prove all dissociation stages are equally strong.

Neutralisation of H+ and OH- forms water. An acid plus a basic oxide or hydroxide forms a salt and water. An acid plus carbonate produces a salt, water and CO2. Many sufficiently reactive metals plus an appropriate dilute non-oxidising acid form salt and H2. Construct the balanced equation from the actual metal charge and acid anion; do not extend the simple hydrogen-product rule to every concentrated or oxidising acid.

An oxidation number is a formal electron-assignment number. An element in its elemental form has oxidation number 0. A monatomic ion's number equals its charge. The sum is zero in a neutral compound and equals overall charge in an ion. Oxygen is normally -2, but -1 in peroxides; hydrogen is normally +1, but -1 in metal hydrides. Fluorine is -1. Use these and known simple ion charges to calculate the unknown number. A Roman numeral in a name specifies the oxidation number of the stated element, not the number of atoms: iron(III) has iron at +3.

Oxidation means electron loss and a rise in oxidation number; reduction means electron gain and a fall. An oxidising agent accepts electrons and is itself reduced. A reducing agent donates electrons and is itself oxidised. In disproportionation the same element is both oxidised and reduced in the same reaction.

To balance a redox half-equation in acidic solution: conserve the changing element, balance O with H2O, balance H with H+, and balance charge with electrons. Multiply half-equations to make their electron numbers equal, add and cancel. In an alkaline system use supplied OH-/water conditions or convert an acid-balanced equation by adding OH- to cancel H+, forming and cancelling water. Check charge and atoms at the end. Multiplying a half-equation changes amounts but not its electrode potential.

[CH-03.02] Group 2

Group-2 metals Mg to Ba have an outer ns^2 arrangement and form M^2+ by losing two electrons. Down the group the first and second ionisation energies generally decrease and reaction with water or dilute acid becomes easier. Typical equations are

$$2M+O_2\rightarrow2MO,\quad M+2H_2O\rightarrow M(OH)_2+H_2,\quad M+2H^+\rightarrow M^{2+}+H_2.$$

Magnesium reacts very slowly with cold water, but with steam forms MgO and H2; heavier members react more readily with cold water to hydroxides. Group-2 oxides react with water to hydroxides. Mg(OH)2 is sparingly soluble; the increasingly soluble hydroxides down the group can produce more alkaline solutions, ranging from moderately alkaline magnesium hydroxide mixtures (typically around pH 9-10) to calcium hydroxide solutions around pH 12 and strongly alkaline heavier hydroxide solutions around pH 13. These are approximate common laboratory observations, not universal values at every concentration. Explain the amount of OH- present. Ca(OH)2 neutralises acidic soils; Mg(OH)2 and CaCO3 can neutralise stomach acid. For example CaCO3 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + CO2 + H2O.

[CH-03.03] Halogens and ion tests

Chlorine, bromine and iodine are diatomic, with outer ns^2 np^5 arrangements. They can gain an electron to form halide ions. Going down Cl2, Br2, I2, molecular size/polarisability and boiling point increase. At room conditions chlorine is a pale green gas, bromine a red-brown liquid and iodine a dark grey solid with violet vapour. Aqueous iodine is brown; an organic solvent gives iodine a violet/purple colour, bromine orange and chlorine pale green or very pale.

Halogen oxidising strength decreases down the group because electron gain becomes harder with greater distance and shielding. A more reactive halogen oxidises a less reactive halide:

$$Cl_2+2Br^-\rightarrow2Cl^-+Br_2,\qquad Br_2+2I^-\rightarrow2Br^-+I_2.$$

Chlorine oxidises both bromide and iodide; bromine oxidises iodide; iodine does not displace either chloride or bromide in this model. Interpret observed colours using the halogen actually produced, with awareness of the phase or solvent.

Chlorine disproportionates in water and cold dilute alkali:

$$Cl_2+H_2O\rightleftharpoons HCl+HClO,\qquad Cl_2+2OH^-\rightarrow Cl^-+ClO^-+H_2O.$$

The chlorine atoms change from 0 to -1 and +1. Chlorine-based disinfection kills microorganisms, while handling toxic chlorine and possible chlorinated by-products requires weighing evidence and benefits. Supplied analogous reactions can be analysed by the same oxidation-number rules.

For inorganic identification, work with small fresh aliquots and distinguish reagent from observation and inference:

- Carbonate: add dilute acid; effervescence comes from CO2. Confirm by passing gas into limewater, which turns cloudy from CaCO3. Net equation CO3^2- + 2H+ -> CO2 + H2O.
- Sulfate: acidify appropriately to remove carbonate interference, then add Ba^2+ solution; a white BaSO4 precipitate remains. Net equation Ba^2+ + SO4^2- -> BaSO4(s). Do not use sulfuric acid as the acidifying reagent when testing sulfate.
- Halide: acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add AgNO3(aq). AgCl is white, AgBr cream and AgI yellow. Net equation Ag+ + X- -> AgX(s). AgCl dissolves in dilute NH3; AgBr needs concentrated NH3; AgI remains insoluble. Hydrochloric acid would introduce chloride and contaminate this test.
- Ammonium: warm with NaOH(aq); NH4+ + OH- -> NH3 + H2O. Ammonia turns damp red litmus blue; use the chemical test rather than relying on smell.

For a sequential anion identification scheme, establish/remove carbonate first, sulfate next, then halide. Otherwise BaCO3 or Ag2SO4 can mimic the intended precipitates. Separate aliquots with appropriate acidification are usually clearer. The solubility-product formula is not an assumed memory rule for H432.

[CH-03.04] Transition metals, complexes and reactions

In the course definition, a transition element has an ion with an incomplete d sub-shell. Period-4 Ti to Cu meet that definition; Sc^3+ is d^0 and Zn^2+ d^10. Transition elements commonly show several oxidation states, coloured ions, and catalytic behaviour. Familiar examples include MnO2 catalysing H2O2 decomposition and Cu^2+ assisting zinc reactions with acid. The detailed orbital account of colour or catalytic mechanism is not assumed.

A ligand donates a lone pair to a metal centre to form a coordinate bond. H2O, NH3 and Cl- are monodentate (one donor attachment per ligand). Ethane-1,2-diamine, en = NH2CH2CH2NH2, is bidentate and can attach through both N atoms. A complex contains a central metal and coordinated ligands; its overall charge is metal charge plus ligand charges. Coordination number counts donor bonds, not ligand molecules: three en ligands give six-coordinate bonding.

Six-coordinate examples are octahedral, with 90-degree adjacent bond angles, such as [Fe(H2O)6]^3+. Four-coordinate complexes may be tetrahedral, as in [CuCl4]^2-, or square planar, as in Pt(NH3)2Cl2; use the identified structure or supplied information. In square-planar cis-platin the two Cl ligands are adjacent; the trans isomer has them opposite. Octahedral complexes with appropriate ligand arrangements can also show cis/trans or optical isomerism. A tris-bidentate complex can have non-superimposable mirror forms. Draw the three-dimensional geometry; a flat formula alone cannot establish stereoisomerism. Cis-platin can bind DNA and impede cell division. In haemoglobin, oxygen binds at iron; CO competes strongly for binding and impairs oxygen transport.

Ligand substitution changes ligand identity without necessarily changing metal oxidation number. Examples are

$$[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+}+4NH_3\rightleftharpoons[Cu(NH_3)_4(H_2O)_2]^{2+}+4H_2O,$$
$$[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+}+4Cl^-\rightleftharpoons[CuCl_4]^{2-}+6H_2O,$$
$$[Cr(H_2O)_6]^{3+}+6NH_3\rightleftharpoons[Cr(NH_3)_6]^{3+}+6H_2O.$$

Copper aqua ions are pale blue, the copper ammine complex dark/deep blue and the tetrachlorocuprate complex yellow; mixed solutions may look green. Chromium(III) aqua ions can be pale violet, though common laboratory solutions are green because of other coordinated species; the chromium ammine complex is purple. Use exact complex formulae in substitution equations and distinguish change of coordination geometry from a redox change.

Both aqueous OH- and small additions of NH3 can precipitate metal hydroxides. NH3 acts as a base by NH3 + H2O <=> NH4+ + OH-. Typical precipitation is M^2+ + 2OH- -> M(OH)2(s), or M^3+ + 3OH- -> M(OH)3(s). Expected observations:

| Ion in solution | Hydroxide precipitate from small OH-/NH3 addition | Behaviour with excess reagent in this scope |
|---|---|---|
| Cu^2+, pale blue | Cu(OH)2, pale blue | dissolves in excess NH3 to deep-blue ammine; does not dissolve in excess NaOH |
| Fe^2+, pale green | Fe(OH)2, green | no further dissolution; can brown on oxidation in air |
| Fe^3+, yellow/orange-brown | Fe(OH)3, orange-brown | no further dissolution |
| Mn^2+, very pale pink | Mn(OH)2, pale brown | no further dissolution |
| Cr^3+, violet/green | Cr(OH)3, green | excess NaOH gives green [Cr(OH)6]^3-; excess NH3 gives purple ammine under appropriate conditions |

Complex ions can instead be used in balanced precipitation formulae. For chromium in excess base, Cr(OH)3(s) + 3OH- -> [Cr(OH)6]^3-. Precipitation and subsequent ligand substitution are different stages, so a little NH3 and excess NH3 can give different observations.

For redox interconversion, Fe^2+ can be oxidised to Fe^3+ by acidified MnO4-; Fe^3+ can oxidise I- to I2 and be reduced to Fe^2+. Chromium(III) can be oxidised by H2O2 in alkaline solution to yellow CrO4^2-; acidification connects chromate with orange Cr2O7^2-. Dichromate can be reduced by Zn/H+ to chromium(III). Cu^2+ with I- forms white CuI and iodine:

$$2Cu^{2+}+4I^-\rightarrow2CuI(s)+I_2.$$

In aqueous solution, free Cu+ readily disproportionates: 2Cu+ -> Cu^2+ + Cu(s). Use half-equations or oxidation numbers to construct unfamiliar redox equations; memorising all transition-redox equations is not required. Classify an unfamiliar change by checking oxidation state (redox), insoluble solid production (precipitation), and ligand exchange (substitution).

[CH-04] 4. Rates and equilibrium

[CH-04.01] Collision theory and qualitative energy distributions

Reacting particles must meet with adequate energy and an effective arrangement. Increasing concentration or gas pressure normally increases collision frequency. Increasing temperature makes collisions more frequent and, more substantially, increases the fraction energetic enough to cross the activation-energy barrier. A catalyst provides a different reaction route with a lower activation energy and is regenerated overall. A homogeneous catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants; a heterogeneous catalyst is in a different phase. Lower-temperature operation can reduce energy demand, although production decisions also involve selectivity, waste and catalyst properties.

A qualitative Boltzmann energy-distribution curve begins at zero at zero energy, rises to a maximum and has a tail approaching the horizontal axis. Total area corresponds to the particle population. At higher temperature the peak is lower and shifts towards higher energy, with a greater area above E_a; the distribution does not become a rectangular threshold. A catalyst lowers E_a without changing the energy distribution at the same temperature. This baseline grants the graph and qualitative interpretation, not a probability-density formula, microstate counting or statistical-mechanics derivation.

[CH-04.02] Rate data and rate laws

Reaction rate can be measured from decrease in reactant concentration, increase in product concentration, gas-volume change or mass loss per unit time. Make the sign appropriate to whether the measured quantity rises or falls. Concentration rates commonly have units mol dm^-3 s^-1. For aA -> bB, the species loss rate of A is -d[A]/dt and the species formation rate of B is d[B]/dt. Their coefficient-normalised common reaction rate is r = -(1/a)d[A]/dt = (1/b)d[B]/dt. Specify which rate is being used; the tangent magnitude on an A concentration-time graph is A's loss rate, not automatically r.

$$\text{rate}=k[A]^m[B]^n.$$

Unless another convention is stated, this displayed rate and k refer to the positive concentration loss rate of a chosen reactant A. m and n are empirical orders with respect to A and B, and m+n is the overall order. For this course the individual orders normally considered are 0, 1 or 2. Do not take them from the overall balanced equation. Compare experiments where all but one concentration and the temperature remain constant: rate ratio = (concentration ratio)^order. Doubling concentration gives unchanged rate for zero order, twice the rate for first order and four times the rate for second order. Use an appropriate ratio or logarithm to determine an unknown order.

k = rate/([A]^m[B]^n). With concentration rate units as above, an overall order r gives units (mol dm^-3)^(1-r) s^-1: zero order mol dm^-3 s^-1; first order s^-1; second order dm^3 mol^-1 s^-1; third order dm^6 mol^-2 s^-1. k depends on temperature and the reaction route, not on the concentration changes already represented in a fixed-temperature rate law.

For a reactant A concentration-time graph, zero-order loss gives a straight descending line; first-order loss gives a curve whose successive half-lives are equal. A's loss rate is the magnitude of its tangent gradient. For this species loss rate versus [A], zero order is horizontal, first order a line through the origin, and second order an upward curve, with any other concentrations fixed or incorporated into the stated constant. For first-order disappearance described by -d[A]/dt = k[A], the rate-concentration gradient is k. Its half-life t_1/2 is constant for successive halvings and

$$k=\frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}}.$$

Use the time units consistently. If instead a coefficient-normalised rate law is defined as r = -(1/a)d[A]/dt = k_r[A], then A's loss rate is a k_r[A], the decay constant and species-rate graph slope are a k_r, and t_1/2 = ln 2/(a k_r). These constants coincide only when a = 1 or the law has already been expressed as A's loss rate. Integrated rate equations such as concentration as an exponential function of time, and their differential-equation derivation, are not assumed knowledge here.

A mechanism consists of steps whose sum gives the overall equation. The rate-determining step is the slow controlling step. A proposed simple slow step must be consistent with the observed rate law, not merely with the overall stoichiometry. Species used in a slow step can be formed in earlier steps. A rate law constrains possible mechanisms; it does not uniquely prove one mechanism. Cancel intermediates when adding steps and ensure any catalyst is regenerated. More elaborate steady-state or pre-equilibrium derivations require new notes.

For temperature dependence use

$$k=A\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right),\qquad \ln k=-\frac{E_a}{R}\frac1T+\ln A.$$

E_a is activation energy in J mol^-1 when R = 8.314 J mol^-1 K^-1, T is absolute temperature, and A is the pre-exponential factor with the same units as k. A plot of ln k versus 1/T is linear in the assumed Arrhenius model: gradient = -E_a/R and intercept = ln A, so E_a = -R(gradient) and A = exp(intercept). If 1000/T is plotted, the scale changes the gradient relation. A molecular explanation of A is not assumed. The two-point relation follows by subtraction: ln(k2/k1) = -(E_a/R)(1/T2 - 1/T1). Increasing T normally increases k for a positive E_a.

[CH-04.03] Dynamic equilibrium and constants

In a closed reversible system at equilibrium, forward and reverse rates are equal and macroscopic composition is constant; molecular reactions continue. Equal rates do not imply equal reactant and product concentrations. Le Chatelier reasoning predicts that a disturbed equilibrium moves in a direction tending to oppose the disturbance. Increasing a reactant concentration tends to favour its consumption. Increasing pressure by compressing an ideal gas mixture tends to favour the side with fewer gas molecules if the gas coefficients differ. Heating favours the endothermic direction. A catalyst accelerates attainment of equilibrium in both directions without changing the equilibrium composition.

Industrial conditions compromise between rate, equilibrium yield, energy use, plant cost and other constraints. For example the exothermic Haber equilibrium N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3 favours higher pressure and lower temperature for equilibrium yield, but too low a temperature gives an impractically slow rate; a catalyst assists rate. Be able to reason from any given equation rather than treating one process's operating conditions as a universal rule.

For aA + bB <=> cC + dD, a concentration expression is

$$K_c=\frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b},$$

using equilibrium, not initial, concentrations. For gas equilibria replace concentrations by gas partial pressures to obtain K_p. A gas's partial pressure is its mole fraction times total pressure: p_i = (n_i/n_total)p_total. Count all gases in n_total; do not include nongaseous substances. In a heterogeneous equilibrium omit pure solid and pure liquid phases from the expression because their effective concentration is constant. Dissolved species remain in K_c expressions; the omission of pure liquids does not mean all substances in a liquid solution are omitted.

Determine equilibrium amounts by a reaction-change balance: initial amount minus coefficient times the reacted extent for a reactant, initial amount plus coefficient times extent for a product. Divide equilibrium moles by the common solution volume for K_c, or use mole fractions for K_p. Set up this balance before solving a non-structured problem. H432 does not require solving quadratic equations in equilibrium assessments. Simple rearrangements, powers and roots are pre-taught here; solving a quadratic that follows from these stated premises is nevertheless legitimate if the student's separate mathematics baseline supplies that skill.

The formal OCR units follow from the expression. K_c units are (mol dm^-3)^(sum of included product coefficients - sum of included reactant coefficients); K_p units are the chosen pressure unit raised to the analogous gas-coefficient difference. A zero difference gives no units. Keep all pressures in the same unit, and do not mix gas pressure units with concentration units. For a fixed written reaction, temperature and concentration/pressure-unit convention, changing concentration, pressure or catalyst does not change K. Composition adjusts to satisfy the same K. Temperature can change K: heating an endothermic forward reaction increases K; heating an exothermic forward reaction decreases it. Within a fixed reaction-equation and unit convention, larger K means a larger equilibrium product-side/reactant-side ratio in that expression, and smaller K a smaller ratio. Numerical labels such as 'large' and 'small' must be interpreted within that convention. Dimensionful K values cannot be universally compared across different reactions or unit choices; reversing or rescaling an equation also changes its K expression and value. The actual equilibrium amounts still require the initial-composition and stoichiometric balances.

K_c/K_p reasoning can be applied to another supplied equilibrium expression. This does not grant a memorised K_sp solubility-product model, activities, chemical potentials, a K_p-K_c conversion formula, van't Hoff temperature equation or a general reaction-quotient free-energy equation.

[CH-05] 5. Quantitative acids, bases and buffers

The course's concentration approximation treats aqueous H+ concentration numerically in mol dm^-3:

$$\mathrm{pH}=-\log_{10}[H^+],\qquad [H^+]=10^{-\mathrm{pH}}.$$

A decrease of one pH unit is a tenfold rise in [H+]. For a dilute strong monobasic acid at the concentrations considered, [H+] is approximately its analytical concentration. For a strong alkali determine [OH-] from concentration and the number of OH- ions supplied by each dissolved formula unit, then use

$$K_w=[H^+][OH^-].$$

At 298 K use K_w = 1.00 x 10^-14 mol^2 dm^-6, so [H+] = K_w/[OH-]. Equivalently pH+pOH = 14.00 at that temperature, where pOH = -log10[OH-]. K_w changes with temperature. Neutrality means [H+] = [OH-], so neutral pH = -log10(sqrt(K_w)); it need not be 7 at every temperature. Extremely dilute strong acid/base solutions for which water's contribution matters need further treatment.

For HA <=> H+ + A-,

$$K_a=\frac{[H^+][A^-]}{[HA]},\qquad \mathrm pK_a=-\log_{10}K_a,\qquad K_a=10^{-\mathrm pK_a}.$$

A larger K_a or lower pK_a indicates greater acid dissociation in comparable conditions. In the course units K_a has mol dm^-3. For a weak monobasic acid alone at starting concentration c, let its dissociation produce x mol dm^-3 of each H+ and A-. If water contributes negligibly and x << c, use [H+] approximately [A-] approximately x and [HA] approximately c:

$$[H^+]\simeq\sqrt{K_a c}.$$

This is a conditional approximation. Check that the calculated x is small compared with c; a stronger weak acid or sufficient dilution can make appreciable acid depletion, and at very low concentrations water can invalidate the other assumption. Recognising failure is included. Retaining acid depletion gives [HA] = c-x and K_a = x^2/(c-x) when water is still negligible, from the same dissociation stoichiometry and equilibrium expression. H432 does not require solving this quadratic, so the exact weak-acid algorithm is not assumed as a memorised chemistry method; a student with quadratic-solving mathematics may derive and solve it. A full coupled water/speciation/charge-balance treatment needs its additional chemical premises stated.

An acid buffer contains substantial amounts of weak acid HA and its conjugate base A-, commonly produced by adding its salt or partially neutralising excess weak acid with strong alkali. The base component consumes added H+ by A- + H+ -> HA. The acid component consumes added OH- by HA + OH- -> A- + H2O. The solution therefore resists small pH changes until one component is substantially exhausted. It does not keep pH exactly fixed or withstand unlimited addition.

From the K_a expression,

$$[H^+]\simeq K_a\frac{[HA]}{[A^-]},\qquad
\mathrm{pH}\simeq\mathrm pK_a+\log_{10}\!\frac{[A^-]}{[HA]}.$$

The second form is simply the logarithmic rearrangement of the first. Use actual buffer amounts after any strong-acid/base neutralisation. If both share the same total volume their concentration ratio equals their mole ratio. To form a buffer with strong base, first subtract added OH- moles from HA moles and add that amount to A-; HA must remain. If strong acid is added, subtract it from A- and add it to HA. If a component is exhausted, switch to the chemistry of the excess rather than reusing the buffer formula. Diluting both components equally approximately preserves their ratio and pH, while reducing capacity. At equal acid/base component concentrations pH approximately pK_a. The H2CO3/HCO3- system is an important blood-plasma buffer; added acid converts hydrogencarbonate towards carbonic acid and added base consumes acid. The broader biological regulation system requires its own notes.

For acid/base mixing or titration, start from stoichiometric neutralisation moles before deciding which pH formula applies. Divide excess H+ or OH- by the final combined volume if a strong species remains. The equivalence point occurs at the exact chemical ratio; the observed indicator endpoint approximates it. Strong acid/strong base curves have a steep region around neutral pH at 298 K; weak acid/strong base curves have a buffer region and equivalence above pH 7; strong acid/weak base curves have equivalence below 7. Weak acid/weak base titration has no sufficiently sharp pH jump for a suitable ordinary indicator in this scope. Curve direction depends on which solution is added to which.

Choose an indicator whose transition range lies in the steep section around equivalence. Indicators are weak-acid equilibria HIn <=> H+ + In- with differently coloured acid/base forms. Adding acid shifts towards HIn and adding base removes H+ and favours In-. At half-neutralisation of a weak acid its HA and A- amounts are equal, giving pH approximately pK_a. Detailed weak-base K_b, salt hydrolysis and exact equivalence-point calculations are not granted unless supplied.

[CH-06] 6. Enthalpy, lattice cycles, entropy and Gibbs energy

[CH-06.01] Heat and enthalpy

An exothermic reaction has negative reaction enthalpy because the chemical system loses enthalpy to its surroundings; an endothermic one has positive enthalpy. An enthalpy-profile diagram places reactant and product enthalpy on a vertical axis and reaction progress on a horizontal axis. Products are lower for an exothermic change. The activation barrier is measured from reactant level to the transition-state peak; forward and reverse barriers differ. A catalyst lowers the barrier without changing the reactant/product enthalpy difference.

Standard conditions in this course use 100 kPa and a stated temperature, commonly 298 K, with substances in their standard physical states. Standard formation enthalpy is the change to form one mole of compound from its elements in their standard states. Standard combustion enthalpy is for complete burning of one mole of a specified substance in oxygen. Neutralisation enthalpy is for formation of one mole of water by acid/base neutralisation. Always inspect the balanced equation and states: the reaction enthalpy belongs to the equation as written, so it need not be 'per mole of any arbitrarily chosen reactant'. An element in its standard state has standard formation enthalpy zero; its standard entropy is not therefore zero.

For heating a measured substance,

$$q=mc\Delta T.$$

q is heat gained in J, m is the mass that changes temperature, c is its specific heat capacity, and delta T is its temperature rise. For dilute aqueous calorimetry, use c = 4.18 J g^-1 K^-1 and density about 1 g cm^-3 if those water-like assumptions are appropriate or stated. m is the heated solution's mass, not automatically the mass of a small solid reagent. Delta T is a temperature difference, so do not add 273 to it. If the solution warms, its q is positive and the reaction's approximate heat is -q. Divide by the amount corresponding to the equation and convert J to kJ to obtain kJ mol^-1. Heat lost to the environment, absorbed by apparatus, incomplete reaction and evaporation can make the result less accurate; explain the direction of a bias when possible.

Breaking bonds requires energy; forming bonds releases energy. A mean bond enthalpy is a positive average energy to break one mole of a given bond type in gaseous molecules, and actual values depend on chemical environment. An estimate is

$$\Delta H\simeq\sum\text{bond energies broken}-\sum\text{bond energies formed}.$$

Count each bond and coefficient accurately. Using gaseous mean bond energies for a process with liquids/solids omits phase-change effects and makes an approximation.

Hess reasoning uses enthalpy as a state-dependent quantity, so alternative routes between the same initial and final states have the same net change. Reverse an equation to reverse its delta H, and multiply coefficients to multiply delta H. With formation values,

$$\Delta_rH^\circ=\sum_{\rm products}\nu_i\Delta_fH_i^\circ-\sum_{\rm reactants}\nu_i\Delta_fH_i^\circ.$$

With combustion values for routes to identical combustion products, reaction enthalpy = sum of reactant combustion enthalpies minus sum of product combustion enthalpies, with coefficients and consistent product states. For an unfamiliar cycle, follow directed arrows, choose a path, and add signed values; do not guess a remembered triangle direction.

[CH-06.02] Lattice, atomisation, ionisation, electron affinity and hydration

OCR uses lattice formation enthalpy: formation of one mole of ionic solid from separated gaseous ions. This is normally negative. Lattice dissociation is the reverse and normally positive; a question using that convention requires a sign change. Atomisation creates gaseous atoms from an element in its stated form. Ionisation removes electrons from gaseous atoms/ions and costs energy. Electron affinity adds electrons to gaseous atoms/ions; the first affinity is commonly exothermic for nonmetals, while adding an electron to an already negative ion can be endothermic.

A Born-Haber cycle combines these terms. For MX with monovalent ions, write the exact steps: elements in standard states -> gaseous atoms -> M+(g) and X-(g) -> MX(s). The overall formation enthalpy equals atomisation terms plus ionisation plus electron affinity plus lattice formation enthalpy. From X2(g), formation of one mole of X(g) requires half the gaseous X-X bond-dissociation energy. If the standard elemental state is X2(l) or X2(s), first account for vaporisation or sublimation to X2(g), with the appropriate coefficient, then dissociate the gas; alternatively use a supplied atomisation enthalpy that already includes both steps. Thus Br2(l) and I2(s) cannot be treated as gaseous molecules without a phase-change contribution. For MgCl2 there are two chloride electron-affinity/atomisation contributions and both first and second Mg ionisation contributions. Draw and count the actual states and coefficients before rearranging for an unknown term.

Solution enthalpy concerns dissolving one mole of solute; hydration enthalpy concerns gaseous ions becoming aqueous ions. For a salt,

$$\Delta_{\rm sol}H=-\Delta_{\rm lattice,formation}H+\sum_i\nu_i\Delta_{\rm hyd}H_i.$$

The first contribution separates the solid into gaseous ions; hydration then attracts water around those ions. More highly charged and smaller ions generally have more negative lattice-formation and hydration enthalpies because attraction is stronger. Compare both terms; a more negative lattice enthalpy alone does not prove greater solubility. Solution enthalpy can be either sign, and entropy also matters for dissolution.

[CH-06.03] Quantitative entropy and Gibbs feasibility

Entropy describes energy dispersal and the number of ways the system can distribute energy, with greater disorder being a useful qualitative course model. For comparable substances gas entropy is generally larger than liquid entropy, which is generally larger than solid entropy. Melting and vaporisation generally increase entropy. An increase in gas molecule number often suggests positive reaction entropy, and a decrease often negative, but actual tabulated values determine a quantitative result.

$$\Delta_rS^\circ=\sum_{\rm products}\nu_i S_i^\circ-\sum_{\rm reactants}\nu_i S_i^\circ.$$

S_i values are molar entropies, commonly J K^-1 mol^-1. Apply coefficients. Distinguish a substance's absolute standard entropy S from the entropy change of a reaction. Elements can have positive standard entropy while having zero standard formation enthalpy. A reaction with more gas products can have positive delta S even if all reactants/products individually have positive entropies.

For a reaction compared at one common temperature, Gibbs, enthalpy and entropy changes are related by delta G = delta H - T delta S. The forward-feasibility criterion applies at constant temperature and pressure. When the calculation uses tabulated standard reaction enthalpies and entropies, it gives the standard reaction Gibbs change:

$$\Delta_rG^\circ=\Delta_rH^\circ-T\Delta_rS^\circ.$$

T must be kelvin. Use compatible energy units: if delta H is kJ mol^-1, divide delta S in J K^-1 mol^-1 by 1000 before multiplying by T; or convert delta H to J. Negative standard delta G predicts a thermodynamically feasible forward process with reactants and products in their standard states at that temperature; positive standard delta G favours the reverse under those conditions. A zero standard delta G is the standard-condition feasibility boundary. It does not assert that every mixture or composition is at equilibrium. For a specified actual composition, a negative actual reaction delta G indicates forward feasibility and zero actual reaction delta G marks equilibrium at constant T and p; its value need not equal the standard value. Calculation of that composition dependence requires additional premises in the notes. None of these signs establishes a useful reaction speed: a large activation barrier can prevent a feasible reaction occurring observably.

Treating the standard delta H and delta S as approximately temperature-independent over a relevant interval gives these standard-condition sign rules:

| standard delta H | standard delta S | Standard-condition forward feasibility from standard delta G |
|---|---|---|
| negative | positive | negative delta G at all positive T in this approximation |
| positive | negative | positive delta G at all positive T in this approximation |
| positive | positive | feasible above the crossing temperature |
| negative | negative | feasible below the crossing temperature |

The standard-condition crossing is T = standard delta H/standard delta S when the ratio is positive and units match. Substitute a representative temperature or use sign reasoning to decide which side is feasible under those conditions. A negative algebraic crossing temperature is not a physical positive-temperature threshold. If phase changes or substantial temperature dependence intervene, the constant-values approximation must be reconsidered. These quantitative entropy, Gibbs and threshold-temperature calculations are fully part of the A-level baseline. They do not grant Boltzmann's S = k_B ln W, partition functions, chemical potentials, heat-capacity integration, or delta G standard = -RT ln K.

[CH-07] 7. Redox titration and electrochemistry

In acidified permanganate/iron(II) titration,

$$MnO_4^-+8H^++5e^-\rightarrow Mn^{2+}+4H_2O,\qquad Fe^{2+}\rightarrow Fe^{3+}+e^-.$$

One mole of permanganate consumes five moles of Fe^2+. Permanganate is purple and acts as its own indicator; in a sufficiently acidic solution its reduced Mn^2+ is very pale, and the endpoint is the first persistent very pale pink from slight excess permanganate. Use an appropriate acid such as dilute sulfuric acid; alternative acids can produce interfering redox chemistry. For iodine/thiosulfate,

$$I_2+2S_2O_3^{2-}\rightarrow2I^-+S_4O_6^{2-}.$$

Iodine is reduced and thiosulfate oxidised. Starch indicates remaining iodine by a blue-black colour; add it when iodine is pale rather than at high iodine concentration, and the endpoint is its disappearance. A liberation step may produce iodine indirectly from the analyte, so use both that equation's ratio and the thiosulfate ratio. Dilution/aliquot factors must be included. Non-familiar titration chemistry can be handled by balanced equations and mole conservation; no universal 1:1 rule applies.

A half-cell contains a redox pair with an electrode allowing electron exchange. A metal electrode can contact its aqueous metal ions. For two aqueous oxidation states or a nonmetal system use an inert conducting electrode such as platinum where appropriate. A cell connects two half-cells by a wire/voltmeter and an ion-conducting salt bridge; the bridge maintains ionic charge balance while the wire carries electrons. Choose bridge ions that do not react significantly with the half-cell contents. A high-resistance voltmeter minimises current and measures cell potential close to equilibrium.

The standard hydrogen electrode has H2 at standard pressure, H+ at the course standard concentration 1 mol dm^-3, and a platinum electrode at a specified standard temperature. Its potential is assigned 0.00 V. The half-reaction is 2H+ + 2e- <=> H2. A standard electrode potential E standard is measured relative to this reference under standard conditions, normally 298 K, 100 kPa for gases and 1 mol dm^-3 for relevant ions. Use supplied tables as reduction potentials.

For a selected cell direction,

$$E^\circ_{\rm cell}=E^\circ_{\rm reduction\,at\,cathode}-E^\circ_{\rm reduction\,pair\,at\,anode}.$$

The more positive reduction potential usually corresponds to the half-reaction that runs as reduction; the other is reversed to oxidation. Electrons flow from the oxidation electrode (anode) to the reduction electrode (cathode) in a discharging galvanic cell. In such a cell the anode is negative and cathode positive. Balance transferred electrons to obtain the chemical equation, but never multiply E values by those electron coefficients. For a forward reaction E standard cell > 0 predicts thermodynamic feasibility under standard conditions; E standard cell < 0 predicts that the opposite direction is favoured. Concentration departures and kinetic barriers limit that prediction. Exact concentration dependence via the Nernst equation, delta G = -nFE and a memorised Faraday-law electrolysis formula are not assumed H432 rules. Stoichiometric electrolysis charge can nevertheless be reconstructed from a supplied half-equation's electron count, N_A, and the separate Physics baseline's elementary charge and Q = It: count transferred electrons, convert between particle counts and moles, and match that amount to the half-equation coefficients. Such a demonstrated route uses available premises and does not require importing a memorised university formula.

A fuel cell continually receives fuel and oxidant and converts chemical energy to electrical energy; fuel is oxidised and oxygen reduced. A storage cell has reagents within it and may be rechargeable by driving its redox chemistry in reverse. For unfamiliar storage/fuel cells, use supplied half-equations, potentials and conditions to establish electrode changes, overall reaction and voltage. Recall of a catalogue of cell designs or every fuel-cell equation is not granted.

[CH-08] 8. Organic language, isomerism and mechanisms

[CH-08.01] Structures and names

An organic structural formula shows connectivity with grouped atoms; a displayed formula shows all atoms/bonds; a skeletal formula uses line ends and corners for carbon atoms and omits attached carbon hydrogens. Add enough H to give an ordinary neutral carbon four bonds, but show heteroatoms and their attached H explicitly. Every substituent must connect to the chemically intended atom: for example a nitrile joins through carbon, R-C triple-bond N, rather than through nitrogen. A general formula describes a homologous series; it is not the formula of every cyclic or multifunctional molecule.

For naming, identify the relevant principal functional group, choose the appropriate longest parent chain/ring, number to locate it and unsaturation/substituents appropriately, state positions, and name substituents. First ten alkane parent names are methane, ethane, propane, butane, pentane, hexane, heptane, octane, nonane and decane; methyl, ethyl etc. are corresponding alkyl groups. Basic suffixes are -ane, -ene, -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid, -amine, -amide and -nitrile, with halogen prefixes fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-. Esters are named from the alcohol-side alkyl group followed by acid-side -oate. Use ring locants for substituted benzenes. Where several functional groups occur, the displayed connectivity and specified naming information control the intended structure.

| Family | Structural feature | Simple general formula where applicable |
|---|---|---|
| acyclic alkane | C-C single bonds only, hydrocarbon | C_n H_(2n+2) |
| acyclic alkene with one double bond | C=C | C_n H_(2n) |
| monohaloalkane | C-X | C_n H_(2n+1)X for a saturated acyclic member |
| alcohol | carbon-bound -OH, excluding phenol/carboxyl OH | R-OH |
| phenol | -OH directly on aromatic ring | Ar-OH |
| aldehyde | terminal -CHO | R-CHO |
| ketone | internal carbonyl | R-CO-R' |
| carboxylic acid | -COOH | R-COOH |
| ester | -COO- between organic portions | R-COO-R' |
| acyl chloride | -COCl | R-COCl |
| acid anhydride | -CO-O-CO- | R-CO-O-CO-R' |
| amine | N with organic substituent(s), not an amide N | R-NH2, R2NH or R3N |
| amide | carbonyl bonded to N | R-CONH2 or R-CONHR' |
| nitrile | -C triple-bond N | R-CN |

A homologous series shares a characteristic functional group and similar reactions, with successive members differing by CH2. An aliphatic compound has straight, branched or nonaromatic cyclic carbon arrangements; alicyclic means nonaromatic cyclic; aromatic in this course involves a benzene ring. 'Saturated' and 'unsaturated' refer here to the absence or presence of multiple carbon-carbon bonds, including aromatic bonding; a carbonyl alone is not a carbon-carbon double bond.

Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different connectivity. Check changes of chain, functional-group position and functional-group type, without counting equivalent redrawings twice. Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but different spatial arrangement. A C=C permits E/Z isomerism only if each double-bond carbon has two different attached groups. Assign priority on each carbon by atomic number of the directly bonded atom, then compare subsequent attached-atom lists at the first difference if necessary. The higher-priority groups on the same side give Z, opposite sides E. Ordinary cis/trans is a special case involving comparable identical groups; it is not a universal synonym for Z/E. Treatment of multiple bonds inside priority side groups is outside this starting toolkit.

A carbon bonded to four distinct groups is a potential chiral centre. Its two mirror arrangements can be non-superimposable enantiomers; draw wedge/dash stereochemistry to show them. Ring paths count as distinct only if their actual connectivity differs. Do not infer chirality merely from four drawn bond lines, and do not label a trigonal carbonyl carbon a tetrahedral chiral centre. R/S naming, quantitative optical rotation and stereoselective synthesis are not assumed.

[CH-08.02] Electron flow and common mechanisms

Homolytic bond breaking gives one electron to each atom and produces radicals, species with unpaired electrons; show radical dots. Heterolytic breaking gives both bond electrons to one atom. A full curly arrow tracks an electron pair: its tail starts at a bond, lone pair or negative charge and its head points where that pair goes. Include relevant bond dipoles and charges. A nucleophile donates an electron pair; an electrophile accepts one. Do not start an arrow at a positive charge or make it represent the physical travel of a whole atom. Routine radical chains are written as equations with radical dots rather than requiring single-electron arrow diagrams.

A sigma bond has orbital overlap along the internuclear line and permits rotation if no other constraint prevents it. A pi bond comes from sideways p-orbital overlap above/below the line and prevents free rotation of a C=C without breaking that overlap. An alkene carbon is approximately trigonal planar, 120 degrees; a saturated alkane carbon tetrahedral, 109.5 degrees. Orbital hybridisation terminology is not needed for these rules.

For an alkane/chlorine or bromine UV substitution chain:

- Initiation: X2 -> 2X radical, a homolytic photochemical cleavage.
- Propagation: X radical + RH -> HX + R radical; R radical + X2 -> RX + X radical. Each step consumes and regenerates a radical so the chain continues.
- Termination: two radicals combine, for example X radical + X radical -> X2, R radical + X radical -> RX, or two R radicals -> R-R.

An unsymmetrical alkane can react at several positions and undergo further substitutions, producing a mixture. Alkanes are relatively unreactive with many other reagents because their C-C/C-H sigma bonds are strong and weakly polar. Complete combustion yields CO2 and H2O; insufficient oxygen can produce CO and/or carbon as well, with CO toxicity.

For electrophilic addition of HX to an alkene, the pi-bond electron pair attacks H delta-plus while the H-X bond pair moves to X. The resulting carbocation is attacked by X-'s lone pair. With an unsymmetrical alkene the route through a more stable tertiary rather than secondary rather than primary carbocation generally dominates (Markovnikov prediction). Thus ordinary HBr addition to propene mainly gives 2-bromopropane. The detailed physical origin of carbocation stability is not assumed. Halogen addition starts with polarisation of X2 by the alkene and attack on its delta-plus end; a carbocation or bridged halonium intermediate can be used at this level. Add the halogens across the original double-bond carbons, not both to one by accident.

For nucleophilic substitution of a primary haloalkane, mark C delta-plus and X delta-minus. Draw an arrow from the nucleophile's donor pair to C and from the C-X bond to X. This replaces X with the nucleophile and releases X-. OH- gives an alcohol; CN- attacks through its carbon and adds a carbon to the chain. A university classification or kinetic comparison of SN1 and SN2 is not granted by knowing this diagram.

For nucleophilic addition to an aldehyde or ketone, mark carbonyl C delta-plus and O delta-minus. Attack C with H- (the NaBH4 reducing model) or CN-; simultaneously move the C=O pi pair to O, giving an alkoxide. Its O- then acquires H+ from a suitable source (water/proton donor) to give an alcohol or hydroxynitrile. The carbonyl double bond becomes a single C-O bond; there is no leaving-group substitution at that stage. For HCN formation, CN- attacks via carbon. A planar carbonyl can lead to both mirror products if a new chiral centre is created, but stereochemical control beyond this inference needs notes.

For aromatic electrophilic substitution, an electron pair from the ring attacks E+, producing an intermediate in which the delocalised ring is interrupted at the attacked carbon; then electron movement from the C-H bond restores the ring and H+ is removed. Show the positive intermediate and correct electron arrows. Nitration generates NO2+ with concentrated acids, for example HNO3 + H2SO4 -> NO2+ + HSO4- + H2O; use NO2+ as the electrophile, not neutral NO2. For aromatic halogenation a halogen carrier makes an effective X+ electrophile in the course mechanism. Friedel-Crafts preparation is included as a transformation, but a separately memorised detailed mechanism for every acyl derivative is not granted.

[CH-09] 9. Organic transformations and synthesis

The following is a usable reaction toolkit. R and R' represent specified organic fragments, not a permission to invent new reagents. Use [O] and [H] in organic redox equations while naming the actual reagent and conditions separately. Before applying a route, track its carbon count, functional-group placement and any other group that may react under the chosen conditions.

| Starting feature | Reagent/conditions | Change and essential interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| alkene C=C | H2 with a suitable metal catalyst, e.g. Ni | add H across C=C to give alkane |
| alkene C=C | Cl2 or Br2 | add one halogen to each double-bond carbon; bromine solution decolourises |
| alkene C=C | HX | form haloalkane; consider alternative carbocations for unsymmetrical alkene |
| alkene C=C | steam, acid catalyst such as H3PO4 | hydration to alcohol |
| alcohol | hot acid catalyst such as H2SO4 or H3PO4 | eliminate H2O to give an alkene; consider possible double-bond positions |
| alcohol | appropriate halide salt plus acid, e.g. NaBr/H2SO4 | replace OH by halogen; supply both halide and proton source |
| primary alcohol | acidified dichromate, controlled oxidation with distillation | RCH2OH + [O] -> RCHO + H2O; remove aldehyde to limit further oxidation |
| primary alcohol | excess acidified dichromate, reflux | RCH2OH + 2[O] -> RCOOH + H2O |
| secondary alcohol | acidified dichromate, heat | RCH(OH)R' + [O] -> RCOR' + H2O |
| tertiary alcohol | ordinary acidified dichromate conditions | resists oxidation because no H is attached to the OH-bearing carbon |
| primary haloalkane | aqueous OH-, heat | substitution to alcohol; RX + OH- -> ROH + X- |
| haloalkane | CN- in ethanol, heat | substitution to RCN; chain extended by one carbon |
| haloalkane | excess ethanolic NH3, heat | primary amine; RX + 2NH3 -> RNH2 + NH4X |
| haloalkane plus primary/secondary amine | ethanolic conditions as appropriate | further N-substitution gives secondary/tertiary amines, so excess NH3 helps favour primary product |
| aldehyde | acidified dichromate | RCHO + [O] -> RCOOH; ketones resist this ordinary oxidation |
| aldehyde/ketone | NaBH4 | carbonyl reduction to primary/secondary alcohol; add two [H] in a balanced shorthand equation |
| aldehyde/ketone | HCN generated from CN-/H+ | add H and CN across C=O to a hydroxynitrile, adding one carbon |
| nitrile | H2/Ni or specified suitable reduction | RCN + 4[H] -> RCH2NH2; retain the nitrile carbon |
| nitrile | aqueous acid, heat/reflux | RCN + 2H2O + H+ -> RCOOH + NH4+ |
| carboxylic acid plus alcohol | acid catalyst, heat/reflux | reversible esterification RCOOH + R'OH <=> RCOOR' + H2O |
| acid anhydride plus alcohol | suitable esterification conditions | ester plus carboxylic acid, not ester plus water |
| ester | hot aqueous acid | reverse esterification, giving carboxylic acid and alcohol |
| ester | hot aqueous alkali | RCOOR' + OH- -> RCOO- + R'OH; acidify to obtain free acid if required |
| carboxylic acid | SOCl2 | RCOOH -> RCOCl, with SO2 and HCl by-products |
| acyl chloride | water | carboxylic acid plus HCl |
| acyl chloride | alcohol or phenol | ester plus HCl; phenol is not readily esterified directly by an ordinary carboxylic acid |
| acyl chloride | ammonia | primary amide; two NH3 are required if one neutralises HCl to NH4Cl |
| acyl chloride | primary amine | secondary amide; allow an extra amine equivalent/base to neutralise HCl |
| amine | dilute inorganic acid | ammonium salt, e.g. RNH2 + H+ -> RNH3+ |

Amines act as bases through the N lone pair. Comparison of different amines' basicity is outside the required baseline. An amide's -CONH- is a different functional group from a free amine. An alpha-amino acid RCH(NH2)COOH has both acid and amine functionality: base gives the carboxylate, acid protonates the amine, and its COOH can esterify. Do not apply an alcohol reaction simply because an OH appears inside a carboxyl group. Full zwitterion/isoelectric-point chemistry is not a required memorised rule here.

Benzene has six p orbitals overlapping into a delocalised pi system above and below a planar ring. Its C-C bonds have equal lengths intermediate between simple single and double bonds. Its hydrogenation is less exothermic than three independent alkene hydrogenations would predict, indicating extra stability. It resists ordinary bromine addition more than an alkene because the ring's electron density is delocalised and losing aromatic delocalisation is costly. These observations support the delocalised model over a literal fixed alternating-bond model.

Aromatic substitution transformations include nitration with concentrated HNO3/H2SO4, halogenation with X2 and a halogen carrier such as Fe/FeX3/AlX3, and Friedel-Crafts alkylation/acylation with a haloalkane/acyl chloride and an appropriate carrier. Alkylation adds an R group; acylation adds -COR, both making a C-C bond to the ring. Nitrophenyl groups can be reduced using Sn/concentrated HCl to aromatic amines; subsequent alkali treatment liberates the free amine where the acidic mixture initially contains its salt.

Phenol has OH directly bonded to the aromatic ring. It is weakly acidic: ArOH + OH- -> ArO- + H2O, but does not release CO2 from carbonate in the usual test. An oxygen lone pair can contribute electron density to the ring, making electrophilic attack easier than in benzene. Bromine water is decolourised with a white 2,4,6-tribromophenol precipitate without a halogen carrier; dilute HNO3 gives 2- and 4-nitrophenol without the concentrated sulfuric-acid catalyst required for benzene. OH and NH2 direct further substitution mainly to positions 2 and 4; NO2 directs mainly to position 3. Other directing effects need supplied information. Use existing substituent positions when numbering predicted products; conflicting directives can produce more than one possibility.

Addition polymerisation opens alkene double bonds and joins monomers without a small-molecule by-product. To draw a repeat unit, replace C=C with the C-C backbone, preserve substituents on their original carbons, and show bonds extending through brackets with n. Reverse that operation to obtain the monomer. Condensation polymerisation joins functional groups while releasing a small molecule, commonly H2O (acid/alcohol or acid/amine) or HCl (acyl chloride routes). A diol plus dicarboxylic acid gives ester links -COO-; a diamine plus dicarboxylic acid gives amide links -CONH-. A single monomer with complementary end groups can also polymerise. Infer monomers by cutting the relevant linking bond and restoring functional groups.

Acid hydrolysis of a polyester yields carboxylic-acid and alcohol ends; alkali gives carboxylate and alcohol ends. Hydrolysing a polyamide restores acid/carboxylate and amine/ammonium groups appropriate to the acidic/basic conditions. Retain the whole carbon skeleton on the correct side of the broken link. Ordinary addition-polymer backbones do not contain the ester/amide hydrolysis link. Discuss recycling, use as chemical feedstock, energy recovery, removal of acidic/toxic combustion products (e.g. HCl from chlorinated plastics), and degradable materials using the chemistry and supplied evidence. A polymer's label alone does not establish real-world biodegradation conditions.

For a multistep synthesis, work backwards from the target group and carbon skeleton, then forwards to check each transformation. Nitrile routes and Friedel-Crafts routes can increase carbon number; alcohol/haloalkane substitution or carbonyl redox normally retain it. State reagents and conditions at each arrow, draw actual intermediate structures, avoid silently changing branch positions, and account for multifunctional substrates. New reaction information supplied by a question may extend this toolkit; unstated Grignard, organolithium, coupling, protecting-group or enolate chemistry is not automatically available.

[CH-10] 10. Analysis and spectroscopy

[CH-10.01] Test observations

Use positive results as evidence for a feature and negative results in conjunction with other data; several functional groups can share a test. Keep separate aliquots to avoid one reagent altering a later test.

- Bromine decolourisation supports alkene unsaturation, but activated aromatic phenol also reacts, often with a white precipitate. A general 'bromine positive' does not uniquely prove an alkene.
- Hydrolysis of a haloalkane in aqueous AgNO3 with ethanol produces AgX; ethanol helps mixing of organic substrate and aqueous reagent. For comparable primary haloalkanes the C-I bond is weaker and hydrolysis faster than C-Br, then C-Cl, with C-F slowest. Explain by bond strength rather than claiming C-I is most polar. This test concerns a covalently bonded halogen, while a direct aqueous halide-ion test already contains free X-.
- Acidified dichromate changes orange to green when reduced by a suitable primary/secondary alcohol or aldehyde. Tertiary alcohol/ordinary ketone is negative under the usual mild course conditions. A positive test alone does not distinguish every responding group.
- 2,4-DNPH gives a yellow/orange precipitate with aldehydes or ketones. Isolate/purify the derivative and compare its melting point with reference values for identification. Its detailed reaction equation and derivative structure are not assumed.
- Tollens reagent gives silver metal, often a silver mirror, with an aldehyde, which is oxidised; ordinary ketones do not give that result. The reagent is ammoniacal silver nitrate.
- Carboxylic acid gives CO2 effervescence with carbonate; phenol's weaker acidity does not. Both can react with an appropriate strong alkali.

[CH-10.02] Chromatography and quantitative instrumental use

Chromatography separates components through differing distributions between a stationary and moving phase. In TLC, apply small spots to a pencil baseline above the solvent level, develop in the chosen solvent, mark the solvent front promptly, and detect spots appropriately. Define

$$R_f=\frac{\text{distance from baseline to centre of solute spot}}{\text{distance from baseline to solvent front}}.$$

R_f has no units and normally lies from 0 to 1. Compare unknown and standards under the same stationary phase, solvent and conditions. Matching R_f supports an identity but does not prove uniqueness; one spot can contain coeluting species. Stronger attraction to the stationary phase means shorter travel relative to solvent in a comparable system.

A gas chromatogram has detector response versus time. Retention time can identify a component by comparison with a reference under the same conditions. Integrated peak area, not just peak height, measures detector response to amount. If response factors are the same or known, area fractions can give component proportions; otherwise use calibration. Prepare known concentration standards, record integrated areas, plot the calibration relation and interpolate the unknown area within a suitable range; account for any prior dilution. Different retention times alone do not tell amounts, and a shared time does not rule out coelution. Beer-Lambert coefficients or a full chromatographic theory are not assumed.

[CH-10.03] Infrared and mass spectra

IR absorption increases molecular bond vibration. A spectrum shows features at characteristic wavenumbers in cm^-1; a transmittance spectrum has downward absorption features, while an absorbance spectrum has upward ones. Use the supplied/reference ranges rather than expecting every related group at one exact number. Useful recognitions include alcohol/phenol O-H in the approximate 3200-3600 region, the very broad carboxylic-acid O-H extending about 2500-3300, a strong carbonyl feature in roughly 1630-1820, and a nitrile C triple-bond N around 2220-2260. C-H near 3000 is common in organic compounds and rarely sufficient for identification. An acid usually needs both C=O and broad acid O-H evidence; an O-H feature alone may instead be an alcohol/phenol. Ester, amide, aldehyde and ketone carbonyls need supporting information to distinguish them.

IR can detect particular gases, monitor combustion emissions and determine breath ethanol using suitable calibration. Atmospheric absorption by greenhouse gases is a molecular-vibration application, not a grant of a climate model. Predict plausible features from functional groups, and recognise disappearance/appearance when a reaction changes a group; not every fingerprint-region band must be assigned.

In an organic mass spectrum, the molecular-ion peak corresponds to the ionised molecule and can give M_r for a single charge. It is not necessarily the strongest peak and a small M+1 peak from carbon-13 can occur. Fragment peaks indicate positively charged fragments with matching masses. Propose a chemically plausible fragment formula, include its positive charge, and check its elemental count against the parent. Neutral lost fragments are not the ions detected. Combine fragment evidence with molecular mass and other data; one mass can have more than one fragment formula. Interpretation of halogen-containing organic mass spectra is outside the declared H432 requirement.

[CH-10.04] Carbon-13 and proton NMR

NMR chemical shift delta is measured in ppm relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS), defined at zero. Different chemical environments have different shifts. TMS has four equivalent carbons and twelve equivalent H atoms, giving one simple signal for each ordinary reference spectrum. It is relatively unreactive, dissolves in common organic NMR solvents and is volatile enough to remove readily. Deuterated solvents largely avoid the overwhelming ordinary proton solvent signal; deuterium is not seen as a proton in the usual proton spectrum. Chemical shifts vary with solvent, concentration and substituents, so reference intervals guide rather than rigidly determine identification.

The carbon-13 spectra assumed here are proton-decoupled. Count signals to count distinct carbon environments, taking molecular symmetry into account; chemically equivalent carbons give one environment. Do not use carbon peak heights as a simple atom-count integration rule. Match carbon shifts to plausible functional environments, then check a full structure. Typical broad distinctions are saturated carbons at lower shifts, carbons bonded to O/other electron-withdrawing groups higher, alkene/aromatic carbons higher again, and carbonyl carbons furthest downfield. Finer carbon-13 ranges needed for a task must be included in its visible notes; the external data-sheet link does not make those unstated ranges available. Signal count alone does not identify a functional group.

In a high-resolution proton spectrum:

1. Count distinct signal groups to establish proton environments, including symmetry and any exchangeable H.
2. Use chemical shift to suggest environment. For orientation, ordinary alkyl H is near 0.5-2 ppm; H near C=O/aryl often about 2-3; H on C bonded to electronegative atoms often about 3-4.5; alkene H about 4.5-6; aromatic H about 6-8; aldehyde H about 9-10; acid OH about 10-12. These are approximate recognitions, not exact fixed lines.
3. Use integrated total group area/ratio to count relative H numbers. A triplet's three lines do not mean three protons in that environment; its area determines that count.
4. For the simple applicable splitting model, n equivalent neighbouring protons split a signal into n+1 lines: singlet, doublet, triplet, quartet etc. Chemically equivalent protons do not split each other. Adjacent groups separated by a carbonyl or oxygen should not be automatically treated as directly coupling neighbours in this basic model.
5. Join compatible fragments and require every signal, area, splitting pattern and molecular formula to fit. An ethyl fragment often gives mutually linked CH3 triplet/CH2 quartet with a 3:2 area ratio. Two equivalent methyl groups adjoining one H can give a methyl doublet integrating to six and a partner multiplet for the methine.

OH and NH signals are often broad and variable, normally treated as unsplit in this scope because of exchange. Add D2O: exchangeable OH/NH protons are replaced by D and their proton signals disappear. Do not assign an OH signal solely because a peak happens to lie near an O-bonded carbon-H shift; disappearance and splitting provide additional evidence. Identify aromatic protons from shifts, but analysis of their detailed splitting is not assumed. Spin quantum mechanics, coupling constants, multiplet tree analysis for several non-equivalent neighbour sets and two-dimensional NMR require new notes.

For combined structure determination, first obtain empirical/molecular formula from composition and molecular mass; identify functional groups from IR/chemical tests; use carbon environments, proton shifts/areas/splitting and fragment masses to constrain connectivity; then check the complete candidate against all evidence. A proposed structure that fits one feature but violates the formula or leaves a signal unexplained is not a completed solution. Explicitly distinguish supported deductions from ambiguous alternatives.

[CH-11] 11. Practical reasoning and measurement

[CH-11.01] Planning, data and limitations

Design an experiment around a defined chemical question, a quantity that can answer it, apparatus capable of measuring that quantity, and controllable variables. Identify independent, dependent and controlled variables. Select a useful measurement range and enough data points/repeats, specify quantities and temperatures, and connect a risk-control measure to the actual hazard. A written plan needs enough detail to reproduce it and to recognise its endpoint. Label observations directly: 'blue precipitate' is data, while 'Cu(OH)2' is an interpretation.

For scientific research, identify the question, consult relevant scientific sources, compare their evidence and record enough reference information for another reader to find each source (author/organisation, title, date, publication or URL, and access date for changing web material). Distinguish a proposed model from a measurement and correlation from a demonstrated cause; agreement of independent results, reproducibility and peer review strengthen confidence without guaranteeing a conclusion. Models can change when new evidence explains observations better, as for atomic structure or benzene bonding. Evaluate a chemical application by relating claimed benefits, costs, environmental effects and risks to evidence and uncertainty, and separate empirical findings from value judgements. Communicate the result using appropriate chemical terminology, units, figures and stated limits.

Accuracy concerns closeness to the true/reference value; precision concerns spread of repeated values. Resolution is the smallest displayed/measurable increment. Repeatability concerns agreement under the same method/operator/apparatus, reproducibility under changed conditions. A systematic error biases results consistently; repeated readings do not remove it. Random variation contributes scatter; repeats help estimate/average it. An anomaly is investigated against procedure and other data, not removed solely because it does not fit a preferred answer. Use a mean of valid comparable measurements and explain exclusions.

Record every displayed digit for a digital reading (stopwatch precision may be limited by operator reaction). For an analogue scale, record reliably read digits and a justified estimated digit. With a usual 0.1 cm^3 burette scale, record readings/titres to two decimal places with the final digit 0 or 5. Titre = final minus initial reading. A mean titre can have any final digit justified by its arithmetic; it need not end in 0 or 5. Use concordant accurate titres normally agreeing within 0.10 cm^3, excluding the rough run; if no pair agrees, improve the practical rather than claiming reliable concordance.

Use a stated apparatus tolerance first. Without one, OCR handbook guidance estimates each analogue reading uncertainty as half the smallest division, each digital reading as one resolution step; operator reaction time may dominate a hand-timed event. Absolute uncertainty carries the quantity's units. Relative/percentage uncertainty is

$$\%u=100\frac{u}{|x|}.$$

For a quantity obtained by difference of two independently read values, add their absolute uncertainty bounds in the simple course worst-case treatment: a titre from two readings each +/-0.05 cm^3 has +/-0.10 cm^3, and a temperature rise from two readings each +/-0.5 degrees has +/-1.0 degree. Greater measured change or a higher-resolution/tighter-tolerance instrument can reduce percentage uncertainty. Precision from repeats is different from the instrument tolerance. The Chemistry mathematical handbook explicitly does not require more complex propagation for multiplication/division; those rules must be supplied by notes or another declared baseline before being assumed.

The current chemistry handbook also states that consistent half-division/full-division approaches, and half-resolution/full-resolution digital approaches, are accepted for assessment because reference conventions have differed. The declared default above follows the current recommended guidance. Do not conceal a question's explicit uncertainty or treat an uncertainty interval as a proof of exact error. Compare a measured result with an accepted value using percentage error = 100 x |measured-reference|/|reference|, distinct from estimated percentage uncertainty.

[CH-11.02] Volumetric analysis

For a standard solution, calculate required solute mass, weigh accurately, dissolve in a beaker with deionised water, transfer quantitatively to the volumetric flask with washings, and add water to the mark only after the solution is at the appropriate temperature. Approach the mark dropwise, read the meniscus at eye level, stopper and invert to mix. Lost solute lowers concentration; filling past the mark dilutes it; using a measuring cylinder in place of a volumetric flask generally reduces volumetric accuracy.

Rinse a pipette with the solution it will transfer and a burette with its titrant so residual water does not dilute them. Use a pipette filler; allow the calibrated pipette to drain according to its design rather than blowing out an uncalibrated last drop. Remove burette-tip air, take out the filling funnel before titration and read the meniscus at eye level. The conical flask can be rinsed with deionised water: extra water changes concentration but not the analyte moles already pipetted. Swirl, wash down splashes, use a few drops of suitable indicator and a white tile, and add titrant dropwise near the endpoint. Do a rough run then accurate repeats.

In calculations find titrant moles, convert using the balanced equation to analyte moles in the aliquot, then divide by aliquot volume or scale to the original flask/sample. For back titration, determine the initially added excess reagent, find the unreacted remainder from its titration, subtract to get amount consumed, then use the original reaction ratio. For mixtures, use separate chemical/quantitative constraints rather than assuming all solutes respond identically.

[CH-11.03] Heating, calorimetry and gas/rate methods

Choose a water bath or electric/sand heating source suitable for flammable materials and required temperature. Under reflux a vertical condenser returns vapour to the reaction flask, allowing heating without rapidly losing volatile reactants. Condenser water enters low and exits high to keep the jacket full. Keep the apparatus vented appropriately; a sealed heated system can build pressure. Add anti-bumping granules before heating and use appropriate support/clamping. Distillation instead directs condensed vapour into a receiver, allowing separation or removal of a volatile product; position the thermometer to measure vapour near the take-off, and collect an appropriate boiling fraction. Reflux and distillation serve different purposes.

For aqueous calorimetry, use an insulated container, lid, mixing and a temperature-time record to reduce/estimate heat-exchange effects. When appropriate, extrapolate pre/post-reaction trends back to mixing time rather than taking a cooled late reading as the true peak. Burning-fuel experiments can lose heat to air, produce incomplete combustion and lose volatile fuel, so interpret resulting bias and suggest a relevant improvement. A more accurate thermometer alone will not cure large heat loss.

For a gas-producing reaction use a gas syringe or appropriate collection method, test leaks, account for lost gas before sealing and choose a sensible capacity. Mass loss tracks escaping gas but can also include evaporation/spillage. Continuous monitoring follows one reaction over time (volume, mass, concentration or calibrated colorimeter reading); an initial-rate scheme compares fresh mixtures at varied starting concentration. In a clock method the time to a fixed small chemical change gives an initial-rate proxy proportional to 1/t, only while the endpoint corresponds to the same small amount and reaction is not too far advanced. Keep final mixed volumes and temperature controlled when varying one concentration. Set a colorimeter to a suitable wavelength, blank it, use clean consistently oriented cuvettes and a calibration curve before converting response to concentration. No unstated Beer-Lambert constant is needed for a supplied calibration.

Calibrate/rinse a pH probe appropriately, avoid cross-contaminating samples, immerse its sensing region adequately, allow the reading to settle, and keep temperature controlled. A pH titration record needs many small volume increments near the steep change and enough elsewhere to establish the whole curve. For electrochemical voltage, establish actual electrode/ion identity, bridge contact, concentrations and temperature; nonstandard composition can alter readings.

[CH-11.04] Separation, purification and identity

Use a separating funnel for immiscible liquid phases. Mix appropriately, vent away from people when pressure can build, allow layers to separate, and identify the organic layer from density or a simple test rather than assuming it always floats. Remove the stopper when draining the lower layer as appropriate. Wash with suitable aqueous reagents to remove impurities. An anhydrous drying agent such as MgSO4 or compatible CaCl2 removes residual water from the organic phase; remove the drying solid before redistilling the liquid. Drying a liquid is not the same as evaporating it.

For recrystallisation, dissolve an impure solid in the minimum suitable hot solvent, remove insoluble impurities if necessary while hot, cool to crystallise, filter under reduced pressure, wash crystals with a little cold solvent and dry. A solvent should dissolve much more product hot than cold and not react with it. Too much solvent leaves product dissolved and reduces recovery; a large warm wash redissolves it. A vacuum/Buchner assembly collects crystals rapidly; a fluted paper gravity filter is useful for removing insoluble material from a hot solution. Match the filtration method to the fraction you need to keep.

Dry material before measuring yield or melting point. Impurities commonly depress and broaden a melting range; a pure reference-consistent narrow range supports identity/purity. Heat slowly near melting to observe the range accurately. One melting point does not prove a unique chemical identity. Chromatography, chemical tests or spectra can provide independent evidence. Practical preparation needs both amount/recovery and evidence of the product actually made.

[CH-12] 12. Declared scope and transfer limits

The baseline includes theoretical, mathematical and written practical reasoning across the whole H432 course, not just AS modules. It includes reading new chemical data, constructing equations and planning synthesis using the supplied chemistry. It is fair to expect transfer of stated principles to an unfamiliar but accessible context; it is not fair to assume independent university facts because the same topic label occurs there.

In particular, do not silently grant prior chemistry teaching of: quantum-mechanical orbital derivations or hybridisation theory; molecular-orbital diagrams; crystal-field/ligand-field colour calculations; K_sp/common-ion solubility models; activities, chemical potentials or Nernst equations; general coupled acid/base speciation algorithms; integrated kinetics and steady-state mechanisms; statistical thermodynamics, partition functions or microstate entropy; delta G standard = -RT ln K; advanced organic reactions/mechanism catalogues; R/S stereochemical naming; detailed aromatic NMR splitting, coupling constants or 2D NMR; organic halogen isotope-pattern mass-spectrum interpretation; or quantitative Beer-Lambert coefficients. Formal statistics are not granted by Chemistry A alone, but remain available if actually supplied by another declared subject baseline. None of these scope statements prohibits a legitimate derivation from supplied premises and available mathematics. In particular, the exact weak-monobasic-acid quadratic can follow from the explicit K_a and stoichiometric rules above. General laboratory competency is not established merely by an assumed written curriculum completion.

Conversely, do not withhold actual A-level knowledge such as quantitative entropy, Gibbs feasibility and temperature thresholds; Born-Haber and hydration/solution cycles; K_p and heterogeneous equilibrium expressions; Arrhenius plots; acid-buffer mole balances; CN- carbon-chain extension; chromatography calibration; or integrated IR/MS/NMR structure deduction. Any first-year notes or questions using additional material must teach or supply it explicitly.

The practical endorsement is separately assessed from the written grade. The course requires at least 12 assessed practical activities spanning its skills and apparatus, with independent evidence. An assumed completed curriculum does not prove any particular student's observed laboratory performance, endorsement result, speed, confidence or retention. Those are separate empirical questions. This baseline introduces no additional A-level test requirement; its role is to make the declared starting knowledge concrete for first-year bachelor's assessments.
