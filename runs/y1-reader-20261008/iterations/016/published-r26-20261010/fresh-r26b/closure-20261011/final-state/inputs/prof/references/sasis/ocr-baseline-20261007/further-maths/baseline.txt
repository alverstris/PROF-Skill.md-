Further Mathematics A starting knowledge

Qualification H245. Compulsory Pure Core Y540 and Y541, with the user's selected Statistics Y542 and Mechanics Y543. Current official specification checked on 7 October 2026: Version 2, October 2025. Both stages of the A-level content apply. Mathematics A knowledge is supplied separately in the Mathematics packet.

This is an operational starting-knowledge packet for a student assumed to have completed these components. Each entry supplies usable mathematical premises, not just a topic name. It is not a claim that a particular real graduate recalls every result perfectly. Knowledge of Additional Pure Y545 or Discrete Y544 is not assumed. A result outside this packet can nevertheless be obtained by a valid deduction from supplied premises; an assessment exclusion is not a prohibition on reasoning.

Pure Core

FM01 Mathematical induction. To prove P(n) for integers n at or above n0, establish P(n0), assume P(k) for an arbitrary eligible k, and prove P(k+1) from that assumption and established rules. The implication and base case are both necessary. It is legitimate to form a conjecture by computing cases, but cases alone do not prove an unrestricted statement. Apply this to divisibility, inequalities, finite sums, powers of matrices, derivatives, exponentials and factorials.

FM02 Complex quantities. i^2=-1. A complex number z=x+iy has real part x and imaginary part y, with x and y real. Equality requires equality of both parts; z=0 requires x=y=0. The conjugate is z*=x-iy and the modulus |z|=sqrt(x^2+y^2). Therefore zz*=|z|^2. i is not an unspecified variable or a real square root.

FM03 Complex arithmetic. Add and subtract real and imaginary components; multiply by distribution and i^2=-1. Divide by nonzero w using z/w=zw*/|w|^2. For example (1+2i)/(3-i)=(1+7i)/10. Products conjugate componentwise: (zw)*=z*w*. A conjugate is not a reciprocal in general.

FM04 Polar and exponential form. For nonzero z, z=r(cos(theta)+i sin(theta))=r exp(i theta), r=|z|>0. Select theta using both component signs; a single arctangent without quadrant checking is insufficient. Angles differ by multiples of 2pi. State any required principal-argument interval; otherwise either (-pi,pi] or [0,2pi) is available in OCR A. The zero number has no unique argument. Euler's relation is exp(i theta)=cos(theta)+i sin(theta).

FM05 Polar arithmetic and geometry. Multiplication multiplies moduli and adds arguments; division divides moduli and subtracts arguments. On an Argand diagram, real and imaginary components are Cartesian coordinates. Conjugation reflects in the real axis; multiplication by exp(i theta) rotates anticlockwise through theta, while multiplication by a positive real scale changes modulus. Addition is vector addition. These statements describe complex arithmetic, not a quantum-state interpretation.

FM06 Polynomial roots. A polynomial with real coefficients has non-real roots in conjugate pairs. Use the quadratic formula with complex square roots when the real discriminant is negative. To find square roots of a+ib, write u+iv, impose u^2-v^2=a and 2uv=b, and retain both opposite solutions. Factor theorem and supplied roots or factors can reduce cubics and quartics; an unrestricted memorised cubic or quartic solution formula is not assumed.

FM07 Complex loci. |z-a|=r is the circle of radius r centred at a, for r>0. |z-a|<r selects its interior; > selects exterior. |z-a|=|z-b| is the perpendicular bisector for distinct a,b. arg(z-a)=theta is a ray from a with its vertex excluded because the argument there is undefined. Re(z)=c and Im(z)=c are vertical and horizontal lines. Translate combinations into intersections of sets and distinguish included from excluded boundaries; clearly identify the chosen region instead of assuming a shading convention.

FM08 De Moivre and roots. (cos(theta)+i sin(theta))^n=cos(n theta)+i sin(n theta) for integer n; negative powers require a nonzero base. For r>0 the n roots of r exp(i theta), n a positive integer, are r^(1/n) exp(i(theta+2kpi)/n), k=0,...,n-1. These are equally spaced on an Argand circle. Use roots of unity, finite geometric sums and Euler's relation to obtain trigonometric multiple-angle identities or sums. No complex analysis theorem is supplied by these rules.

FM09 Matrix objects and operations. An m by n matrix has m rows and n columns. Equal matrices have equal shape and entries. Add or subtract equal shapes entrywise; multiply every entry by a scalar. If A has shape m by n and B has shape n by p, AB has shape m by p with (AB)_(ij)=sum_(k=1)^n A_(ik) B_(kj). Matrix entries may be real or complex. A transpose A^T swaps row and column indices and shape; it does not conjugate complex entries. A conjugate transpose has not been granted as a separate taught operation here.

FM10 Order and identities. Matrix multiplication is associative and distributive but generally AB is different from BA. For column vectors, ABv applies B first, then A. The n by n identity I leaves conformable vectors/matrices unchanged. Adding a zero matrix leaves entries unchanged; multiplying by a conformable zero matrix produces zero. A^k is repeated multiplication for positive integer k. For example A=[[1,2],[0,1]], B=[[1,0],[3,1]] gives AB=[[7,2],[3,1]] and BA=[[1,2],[3,7]].

FM11 Transformation matrices. A linear transformation represented on coordinate column vectors has columns equal to the images of coordinate unit vectors. In two dimensions, anticlockwise rotation through theta is [[cos(theta),-sin(theta)],[sin(theta),cos(theta)]]. Reflection in the x axis is diag(1,-1), in the y axis diag(-1,1), in y=x is [[0,1],[1,0]], and in y=-x is [[0,-1],[-1,0]]. Uniform enlargement is kI; axis stretches and shears follow by mapping the coordinate unit vectors. For successive transformations, preserve their application order. A translation by a nonzero vector is not represented by a two-by-two linear matrix alone.

FM12 Three-dimensional transformations. Reflections in x=0, y=0 or z=0 reverse the corresponding coordinate. Coordinate-axis rotations are obtained by holding that coordinate fixed and rotating the other two with the specified orientation. Positive rotation is anticlockwise when viewed towards the origin from the positive side of the rotation axis. State the viewing direction before interpreting signs. Only these named three-dimensional transformations are baseline assessment knowledge.

FM13 Invariance. An invariant point satisfies Av=v. An invariant line is mapped onto itself as a set and need not consist entirely of fixed points. Substitute a general point of a candidate two-dimensional line into the transformation to test the distinction. This does not grant a general eigenvalue/eigenvector theory; the selected Pure Core uses invariance directly.

FM14 Determinants. det([[a,b],[c,d]])=ad-bc. For a three-by-three matrix, expand along a row or column using alternating signs and the corresponding two-by-two minors. det(AB)=det(A)det(B). A nonzero determinant means non-singular; zero means singular. In two dimensions |det(A)| is the area scale factor and its sign records preservation or reversal of orientation. In three dimensions |det(A)| is the volume scale factor and its sign distinguishes orientation. These are geometric properties of the selected finite-dimensional transformations.

FM15 Inverses. An inverse of a square A satisfies A^(-1)A=AA^(-1)=I. For a two-by-two matrix with ad-bc nonzero, A^(-1)=[[d,-b],[-c,a]]/(ad-bc). For a non-singular three-by-three matrix use cofactor matrix, transpose to form the adjugate, then divide by the determinant; verify by multiplication. A singular matrix has no two-sided inverse. (AB)^(-1)=B^(-1)A^(-1) when the relevant inverses exist; an inverse undoes its transformation.

FM16 Simultaneous equations. Encode two or three linear equations as Ax=b. If det(A) is nonzero, x=A^(-1)b is the unique solution. If singular, use the equations to distinguish inconsistency from dependence; zero determinant alone does not distinguish no solution from infinitely many. Interpret three equations as planes that can meet at a point, along a line, coincide appropriately or have no common point. Finding a full parameterisation of every infinite solution set or a line of intersection of planes is not automatically prior assessment knowledge.

FM17 Lines and planes. A line is r=a+t u, where a is a point and u is a nonzero direction. In three dimensions its Cartesian symmetric form follows componentwise, with zero direction components treated separately rather than dividing by zero. A plane is r=a+s u+t v with independent directions u,v, or n dot (r-a)=0 with nonzero normal n. Its Cartesian form is ax+by+cz=d, with normal (a,b,c). Convert between forms using their geometric roles.

FM18 Scalar products and angles. a dot b=sum a_i b_i=|a||b|cos(theta) for real Cartesian vectors. Nonzero perpendicular vectors have scalar product zero. Use this to find angles between lines or between plane normals. A line-plane angle is the complement of the acute angle between its direction and the plane normal; choose the requested acute or oriented angle deliberately. This real-vector rule is not a rule for complex quantum inner products.

FM19 Vector products. For a=(a1,a2,a3), b=(b1,b2,b3), a cross b=(a2b3-a3b2, a3b1-a1b3, a1b2-a2b1). The result is perpendicular to both; swapping inputs negates it. Use it to construct a plane normal or a mutual perpendicular. The component formula is expressly supplied when needed in OCR A; it is supplied here so outside formula lookup is unnecessary. The separate magnitude-angle identity |a cross b|=|a||b|sin(theta), surface-area interpretation and angular-momentum/torque interpretation are not automatically recalled from this content. A magnitude can still be calculated from resulting components using the ordinary vector norm.

FM20 Intersections and distances. Equate line components and solve parameters to test a common point; parallel directions do not prove coincident lines, and nonparallel three-dimensional lines may be skew. Substitute a line into a plane equation for its intersection. The distance from point b to plane n dot r=d is |n dot b-d|/|n|. For skew lines through a and b with mutual perpendicular n, shortest distance is |(b-a) dot n|/|n|. In two dimensions point (x0,y0) to ax+by=c has distance |ax0+by0-c|/sqrt(a^2+b^2). These distance formulae are supplied resources, not a claim of unaided exam recall. Projection or perpendicular conditions determine a point-line distance.

FM21 Roots and coefficient relations. For a monic polynomial x^n+c1 x^(n-1)+...+cn with roots r1,...,rn, sum roots=-c1, sum pairwise products=c2, and successive elementary symmetric sums alternate signs, with product roots=(-1)^n cn. The selected assessment covers through quartics. Use these relations, expansions and substitution to construct a new polynomial whose roots are scaled, shifted or reciprocated, retaining exclusions where roots are zero.

FM22 Extended partial fractions. For a rational function, first divide polynomials if the numerator degree is at least the denominator degree. Use constants over linear factors, successive powers for repeated factors, and a linear numerator over a quadratic factor when required. Determine coefficients by multiplication and comparison or suitable substitutions. Integrate the resulting terms using established logarithmic, power and inverse-function rules. Do not cancel a denominator without retaining its original domain restrictions.

FM23 Sums and telescoping. sum_(r=1)^n r=n(n+1)/2; sum r^2=n(n+1)(2n+1)/6; sum r^3=[n(n+1)/2]^2. Rearrange a term into a difference whose neighbouring sums cancel and retain endpoint terms. For an infinite telescoping sum, compute the limit of the finite partial sum and establish that the remaining boundary term converges. A finite cancellation pattern alone does not establish convergence of an arbitrary infinite series.

FM24 Hyperbolic definitions. sinh(x)=(e^x-e^(-x))/2, cosh(x)=(e^x+e^(-x))/2, tanh(x)=sinh(x)/cosh(x). sinh is odd with range all real numbers; cosh is even with range [1,infinity); tanh is odd with range (-1,1). cosh^2(x)-sinh^2(x)=1. Other hyperbolic identities can be derived from these definitions, rather than imported as memorised facts.

FM25 Hyperbolic calculus. d(sinh x)/dx=cosh x, d(cosh x)/dx=sinh x, d(tanh x)/dx=1/cosh^2 x. Antiderivatives of sinh and cosh exchange them; integral tanh x dx=ln(cosh x)+C. Use the supplied ordinary product, chain and substitution rules to extend these expressions.

FM26 Inverse hyperbolic functions. arsinh(x)=ln(x+sqrt(x^2+1)) for real x; arcosh(x)=ln(x+sqrt(x^2-1)) for x>=1, selecting the nonnegative inverse of cosh; artanh(x)=0.5 ln((1+x)/(1-x)) for |x|<1. Derivatives are 1/sqrt(1+x^2), 1/sqrt(x^2-1) for x>1, and 1/(1-x^2), respectively. Their inverses and logarithmic forms can be derived by solving the exponential definitions; an inverse notation is different from a reciprocal.

FM27 Maclaurin expansions. Build the expansion around zero as f(0)+f'(0)x+f''(0)x^2/2!+...+f^(n)(0)x^n/n!+... when the expansion is valid. Supplied standard expansions are e^x=sum x^n/n!; sin x=x-x^3/3!+x^5/5!-...; cos x=1-x^2/2!+x^4/4!-...; ln(1+x)=x-x^2/2+x^3/3-...; and (1+x)^a=1+ax+a(a-1)x^2/2!+.... Exponential, sine and cosine expansions apply for real x. The supplied logarithmic series applies for -1<x<=1, including ln(2)=1-1/2+1/3-.... Use |x|<1 for a nonterminating general binomial expansion unless its boundary is separately established. Nonnegative integer binomial expansions terminate. Substitution changes the validity interval. Expansions in a non-real argument, convergence proofs and a general remainder theorem are not automatically granted.

FM28 Improper integrals. Replace an infinite endpoint by a finite bound and take its limit. If an integrand is singular inside an interval, split into two integrals and require each relevant one-sided limit to exist separately. For example integral_1^infinity x^(-p) dx converges for p>1, as follows by evaluating its finite primitive and limit. Cancellation across a divergent singularity is not a legitimate improper-integral value.

FM29 Inverse trigonometric derivatives and substitution. Principal real inverse domains give d(arcsin x)/dx=1/sqrt(1-x^2) and d(arccos x)/dx=-1/sqrt(1-x^2) for |x|<1; d(arctan x)/dx=1/(1+x^2). Differentiate implicit inverse relationships to derive them. Integrals involving a^2-x^2, a^2+x^2 or x^2-a^2 can be treated by suitable trigonometric or hyperbolic substitutions, with signs and real domains checked. For a>0: integral dx/sqrt(a^2-x^2)=arcsin(x/a)+C for |x|<a; integral dx/(a^2+x^2)=arctan(x/a)/a+C; integral dx/sqrt(a^2+x^2)=arsinh(x/a)+C; and integral dx/sqrt(x^2-a^2)=arcosh(x/a)+C for x>a. The latter follows using x=a cosh(u) on the positive branch; ln|x+sqrt(x^2-a^2)|+C works on either interval x>a or x<-a, with a separate constant on each disconnected interval. Verify variants by differentiation.

FM30 Volumes and means. Rotating a region around a coordinate axis produces disks or washers. About the x axis V=pi integral [R(x)^2-r(x)^2] dx, with analogous y-axis form. Derive it from cross-sectional areas. For parametric curves use dx=x'(t)dt or dy=y'(t)dt with correctly ordered bounds. The average value of f on a finite interval [a,b], a<b, is integral_a^b f(x) dx/(b-a); this is distinct from a probability-weighted mean unless the distribution is uniform.

FM31 Polar curves. With r>=0, x=r cos(theta), y=r sin(theta). Converting back requires modulus and quadrant checks. Analyse symmetry, intercepts, extrema of r and angular ranges to sketch r=f(theta). Enclosed area on a correctly selected angular interval is 0.5 integral r(theta)^2 dtheta; for a region between radial boundaries use the difference of squares. A curve traced twice must not be counted twice accidentally.

FM32 First-order differential equations. A general solution contains arbitrary constant(s); initial or boundary conditions choose a particular solution. For y'+P(x)y=Q(x), use integrating factor mu(x)=exp(integral P(x)dx), giving (mu y)'=mu Q and y=[integral mu Q dx+C]/mu. Rearrange to this linear form before applying the rule. Separability from Mathematics remains available. Deriving an equation from a model and solving it are separate abilities.

FM33 Constant-coefficient homogeneous second-order equations. For a y''+b y'+c y=0 with a nonzero constant, solve a r^2+b r+c=0. Distinct real roots give C1 e^(r1 x)+C2 e^(r2 x); a repeated root r gives (C1+C2 x)e^(r x); roots alpha +/- i beta give e^(alpha x)(C1 cos(beta x)+C2 sin(beta x)). Use two independent conditions where available and verify by substitution. The corresponding OCR normalised form has leading coefficient one.

FM34 Forced second-order equations. For a y''+b y'+c y=f(x), add a particular solution to the homogeneous solution. For polynomial forcing try a polynomial of sufficient degree; for exponential forcing try an exponential multiple; for sine/cosine forcing try both sine and cosine terms. If the trial duplicates a homogeneous mode, multiply by sufficient powers of x to remove that duplication. Substitute to determine coefficients; a guessed trial is not an answer until checked. Supplied trial forms may extend an individual problem. Laplace transforms and arbitrary variable-coefficient solution methods are not granted.

FM35 SHM and damping. x''=-omega^2 x, with omega>0, has x=A cos(omega t)+B sin(omega t)=R sin(omega t+phi), period 2pi/omega, with displacement measured from equilibrium. A positive damping term in m x''+c x'+k x=0 changes the roots: c^2<4mk is oscillatory decay, equality is critical damping, and c^2>4mk is overdamping. These classifications assume m,k>0 and c>0. Interpret initial conditions and physical decay; do not confuse the undamped natural frequency with every observed damped frequency.

FM36 Coupled linear equations. For x'=a x+b y+f(t), y'=c x+d y+g(t), with constant coefficients, differentiate one equation and use the equations to eliminate the other variable, producing a second-order equation where the coupling permits. Recover the eliminated variable from an original equation and check both. Apply initial data to the full system. This permits elimination-based solution, not an assumed general eigenmode or matrix-exponential method.

Statistics Y542

FM37 Counting. n!=n(n-1)...1, 0!=1; ordered choices of r distinct objects from n have count n!/(n-r)!, unordered choices n!/[r!(n-r)!]. Identical repetitions divide arrangements by the factorials of repeated counts. Handle restrictions by grouping, complementary counting or sequential choices, with attention to whether choices are equally likely. Use these counts within the conditional-probability rules in Mathematics; counting alone does not establish equiprobability.

FM38 Discrete distributions and expectation. A probability mass function p(x) is nonnegative and sums to one on its support. E(X)=sum x p(x), E(g(X))=sum g(x)p(x), and Var(X)=E(X^2)-E(X)^2=sum (x-mu)^2 p(x). Standard deviation is sqrt(Var(X)). These rules permit weighted means directly. A distribution table or normalising factor must specify the weights; these rules do not select canonical thermal weights or quantum probabilities. A nonstandard distribution in the assessed baseline is finite.

FM39 Linear coding and binomial moments. E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b and Var(aX+b)=a^2 Var(X). For X binomial(n,p), P(X=k)=C(n,k)p^k(1-p)^(n-k), mean np and variance np(1-p), under fixed-number, independent trials with common success probability. Interpret the random quantity before selecting a distribution.

FM40 Discrete uniform and geometric models. Uniform on {1,...,n}, for positive integer n, gives P(X=k)=1/n, mean (n+1)/2 and variance (n^2-1)/12. A geometric(p) variable, with 0<p<=1, counts trials up to and including first success: P(X=k)=p(1-p)^(k-1), k>=1, P(X>k)=(1-p)^k, mean 1/p, variance (1-p)/p^2. This assumes independent trials with unchanged p. For p=1 the first trial succeeds with certainty; p=0 gives no finite first-success distribution. A failures-before-success convention has different support and mean and must be explicitly translated.

FM41 Poisson model. For independent events at a constant average rate without clustering in disjoint small intervals, counts over an interval are modelled by Poisson(lambda), lambda>=0: P(X=k)=e^(-lambda)lambda^k/k!, k=0,1,...; E(X)=Var(X)=lambda. For lambda=0 the count is zero with certainty. Rescale lambda for interval size. Independent Poisson counts sum to a Poisson count with parameter sum lambda_i. A Poisson approximation to a binomial distribution is not separately granted by the selected OCR content.

FM42 Continuous density and distribution. A density f(x)>=0 integrates to one on its support; P(a<X<b)=integral_a^b f(x) dx and an individual point has probability zero for an absolutely continuous distribution. F(x)=P(X<=x)=integral_(-infinity)^x f(t)dt, and f=F' at differentiable points. For piecewise densities enforce the support, normalisation and boundary continuity of F. Medians and quartiles solve F(x)=0.5,0.25,0.75 respectively. A density value is not itself an interval probability.

FM43 Continuous moments. E(X)=integral x f(x) dx; E(g(X))=integral g(x)f(x)dx; Var(X)=integral x^2 f(x)dx-mu^2, over the support where the required integrals converge. For Y=g(X), construct F_Y(y)=P(g(X)<=y) using the inverse inequality and all relevant branches, then differentiate if a density exists. An arbitrary remembered change-of-variables Jacobian formula is not supplied.

FM44 Standard continuous distributions. Uniform on [a,b], b>a, has density 1/(b-a), mean (a+b)/2, variance (b-a)^2/12. Exponential with rate lambda>0 has density lambda e^(-lambda x) for x>=0, F(x)=1-e^(-lambda x), mean 1/lambda, variance 1/lambda^2; waiting time to the next event in a Poisson model connects these quantities. Normal(mu,sigma^2), sigma>0, is symmetric with density exp[-(x-mu)^2/(2sigma^2)]/(sigma sqrt(2pi)); standardise with Z=(X-mu)/sigma. Probability values may be evaluated from numerical information supplied in the notes; no external calculator access is implied by this packet.

FM45 Sums of variables. E(aX+bY+c)=aE(X)+bE(Y)+c without an independence requirement. If X,Y are independent, Var(aX+bY+c)=a^2 Var(X)+b^2 Var(Y). Independent normal variables have normally distributed linear combinations; their means and variances follow these formulae. Without independence, the simple variance-sum formula is not justified. The general covariance correction is not automatically supplied here.

FM46 Sampling distributions. For independent identically distributed samples with finite population mean mu and variance sigma^2, the sample mean has mean mu and variance sigma^2/n. A normal population gives a normal sample mean exactly; a sufficiently large random independent sample often supports a central-limit normal approximation. OCR uses roughly n>25 as a practical indication, not a universal guarantee for every population shape. Unbiased sample variance is sum (x_i-xbar)^2/(n-1), n>1; the sample mean estimates population mean.

FM47 Tests for a population mean. State H0 and H1 in terms of the population mean. With known variance, use z=(xbar-mu0)/(sigma/sqrt(n)); use a normal population or a justified large-sample approximation. For large samples with unknown variance, replace sigma by the supplied unbiased sample estimate s under the approximation. Compare the relevant one- or two-tailed probability or critical value to the significance level. Rejecting H0 is evidence against it, not certainty; failure to reject does not prove H0. Small-sample Student t tests are not selected baseline knowledge.

FM48 Confidence intervals. Under the conditions for the preceding normal calculation, a two-sided confidence interval for mu is xbar +/- z_(1-alpha/2) sigma/sqrt(n), using s for the appropriate large-sample unknown-variance approximation. z_(0.975) approximately 1.960 gives 95%; z_(0.995) approximately 2.576 gives 99%. The confidence level describes the repeated-sampling coverage of the procedure, not a new probability distribution over the fixed parameter after observing this sample. Additional quantiles must be supplied or derived from supplied numerical data.

FM49 Chi-squared independence. Under independent observations classified in an r by c contingency table, expected count E_ij=(row total_i)(column total_j)/(grand total) under independence. Compute sum (O-E)^2/E and compare with a supplied chi-squared critical value at degrees of freedom (r-1)(c-1). Combine appropriate rows/columns when expected frequencies are below five; this changes degrees of freedom. OCR calls for Yates' continuity correction in a two-by-two table: reduce each absolute O-E discrepancy by 0.5, without making a discrepancy negative, before squaring and dividing by E. Interpret evidence for association, not a proved causal effect.

FM50 Chi-squared goodness of fit. For mutually exclusive classes, a hypothesised distribution supplies expected counts N p_i. Combine adjacent classes as needed to keep expected counts at least five. Sum (O-E)^2/E and use degrees of freedom equal to number of resulting classes minus one minus the number of parameters estimated from these data. Compare with the supplied critical value and state the conclusion in context. Numerical critical-value tables are resources, not all memorised values.

FM51 Sign tests. For an independent random sample with no observation equal to the hypothesised median, signs relative to that median have probability one-half under H0. Count one sign using a binomial(n,0.5) model and use the appropriate tail(s). For paired observations apply signs to within-pair differences; do not treat paired and unpaired samples as interchangeable. This provides a non-parametric alternative when a mean/normal model is inappropriate.

FM52 Wilcoxon signed ranks. For a single random sample or independent sampled pairs, form differences around the hypothesised centre, rank absolute nonzero differences, attach original signs and sum positive/negative ranks W+ and W-. The null model requires a continuous symmetric distribution of centred differences and independent observations or pairs. Ties and zero differences are outside the selected assessment cases. OCR's table statistic is T=min(W+,W-): reject for T at or below the supplied critical value, using the table's one- or two-tailed level and the correct sign of difference for a directional alternative. For sufficiently large n, either raw sum W+ or W- has null mean n(n+1)/4 and variance n(n+1)(2n+1)/24. Apply a normal approximation to that raw sum, not to folded T. For an integer-valued raw statistic R with matched normal variable N: P(R<=k) approximately P(N<=k+0.5), P(R>=k) approximately P(N>=k-0.5), and P(a<=R<=b) approximately P(a-0.5<=N<=b+0.5).

FM53 Wilcoxon rank sum. For two independent random unpaired samples, choose the smaller size m<=n, rank all m+n observations and sum the smaller sample's ranks as R_m. Under the null hypothesis that the populations have the same continuous distribution and with no ties, E(R_m)=m(m+n+1)/2 and Var(R_m)=mn(m+n+1)/12. OCR's table uses the reflected statistic W=min(R_m, m(m+n+1)-R_m), with rejection for W at or below the supplied critical value. Use its stated one- or two-tailed level and check that the direction of the observed difference supports a directional alternative. A large-sample normal approximation applies to raw R_m with the displayed mean and variance and FM52's explicit continuity corrections, not to folded W. For a median-shift interpretation, the two population distributions must have the same shape and spread. This is different from the paired signed-rank calculation.

FM54 Correlation and coding. For paired data define Sxx=sum(x-xbar)^2, Syy=sum(y-ybar)^2 and Sxy=sum(x-xbar)(y-ybar). Pearson r=Sxy/sqrt(Sxx Syy) where both sums of squares are nonzero. It measures linear association and lies between -1 and 1. Adding constants leaves r unchanged; multiplying x,y by nonzero a,b changes its sign by sign(ab) and preserves magnitude. Positive linear coding preserves its value. A source shorthand that linear coding leaves r unchanged must not license ignoring a negative scale factor.

FM55 Rank correlation and tests. With n paired observations and no ties, Spearman r_s=1-6 sum d_i^2/[n(n^2-1)], n>1, where d_i is the difference of the two ranks. It assesses monotonic rank association. Select Pearson for an appropriate linear relationship and Spearman for suitable ranked/monotonic relationships. For Pearson hypothesis testing, use the supplied critical values or p-values under the specified bivariate-normal assumption; rank tests use their supplied critical values and relevant sampling assumptions. Correlation does not establish causation.

FM56 Regression. A least-squares line of y on x minimises vertical squared residuals. Its slope b=Sxy/Sxx and intercept a=ybar-b xbar, provided Sxx>0. Use it to predict response y from controlled/explanatory x within a reasonable data range. Do not reverse it to treat y as the controlled predictor or claim reliability of extrapolation without justification. Linear coding changes slope and intercept through substitution. A regression estimate has uncertainty even when arithmetic is exact.

Mechanics Y543

FM57 Dimensions. Write dimensions in mass M, length L and time T; position L, velocity LT^(-1), acceleration LT^(-2), force MLT^(-2), energy ML^2T^(-2), momentum MLT^(-1), power ML^2T^(-3). Terms added must share dimensions. Solve for exponents in a proposed relationship and check dimensional consistency. Matching dimensions cannot determine a dimensionless coefficient or establish that a physical law is true.

FM58 Work and energy. Constant force work is F dot displacement. A one-dimensional variable force does work integral F(x)dx. Kinetic energy is m(v dot v)/2, local gravitational potential energy is mgh. Net work equals change of kinetic energy for the stated fixed-mass particle model. Conservative changes can be tracked through potential energy; include dissipative loss explicitly when mechanical energy is not conserved. The multidimensional line-integral form for a variable field is university teaching, not a prior rule supplied by this mechanics option.

FM59 Elastic strings and springs. For natural length l, extension x and modulus lambda, tension T=lambda x/l in the Hookean range, and stored elastic energy=lambda x^2/(2l). A spring can resist compression within its model; an elastic string carries tension when extended and becomes slack when not taut. Spring constant k=lambda/l gives E=kx^2/2. Establish which length is extension and whether the string is taut before using energy or force equations.

FM60 Power. Average power is work/time and instantaneous power is F dot v. For positive force and speed magnitudes this is +Fv in the same direction and -Fv in opposite directions; signed one-dimensional components already account for the direction. Resolve force components on slopes and balance tractive force with resistance/weight components. Constant power does not imply constant driving force or speed. If a model predicts a terminal or maximum speed, check its assumptions and force balance.

FM61 Momentum and impulse. Momentum is m v for a fixed-mass particle. Total vector momentum is conserved when resultant external impulse is zero over the event. Impulse equals momentum change; for constant force J=F Delta t, and in one dimension a variable force has J=integral F(t)dt. Track signs and resolve components for two-dimensional events. Internal interaction impulses cancel for the system; momentum of either object alone need not be conserved.

FM62 Restitution. For a direct impact, relative separation speed=e times relative approach speed along the impact line, with 0<=e<=1 in the assessed models. With consistent one-dimensional coordinates this is v2-v1=e(u1-u2). For a smooth fixed plane, the normal velocity component reverses and is scaled by e; the tangential component is unchanged in the smooth model. For two smooth spheres use their line of centres at contact as the normal, apply restitution and conservation of normal momentum, and leave each sphere's tangential component unchanged because the smooth impact impulse is normal. e=1 has no impact kinetic-energy loss; e=0 is the maximum-loss normal impact case. General scientific terminology calls 0<e<1 inelastic too; OCR's stated coalescing 'inelastic' limiting case is e=0. Coalescence of oblique bodies is not established merely by equal normal components.

FM63 Centres of mass. For point masses, r_cm=sum m_i r_i/sum m_i, with positive total mass. Uniformity and symmetry can locate a body's centre. Combine uniform bodies by signed masses when material is removed, checking the resulting physical body and mass. For a uniform lamina between y=f(x) and y=g(x), on bounds with f>=g and positive area A=integral(f-g)dx, xbar=integral x(f-g)dx/A and ybar=integral(f^2-g^2)dx/(2A). For a uniform solid with cross-sectional area A(x)>0 on its occupied interval, xbar=integral x A(x)dx/integral A(x)dx. A solid made by revolving the region from the x axis to y=f(x) has A(x)=pi f(x)^2; a hollow washer with outer radius R(x) and inner radius r(x) has A(x)=pi[R(x)^2-r(x)^2], R>=r>=0. These use one-variable integration and do not grant arbitrary multivariable density integrals.

FM64 Standard uniform-body centres. A rod's centre is its midpoint; a triangle's centroid lies one-third of its altitude from the base. A semicircular lamina's centre is 4r/(3pi) from its diameter; a hemispherical solid's centre is 3r/8 from its flat face; a thin hemispherical shell's centre is r/2 from the sphere centre towards its pole. A solid cone's centre has perpendicular height h/4 above its base; a hollow thin conical surface's centre has perpendicular height h/3. A solid pyramid's centre is r_base+(r_vertex-r_base)/4, where r_base is the base centroid; its perpendicular height is h/4, while its distance along an oblique centroid-to-vertex line is one-quarter of that line's length. A circular-sector lamina of half-angle alpha has centre 2r sin(alpha)/(3alpha) from its circle centre on its symmetry axis; a uniform circular arc with angle 2alpha has centre r sin(alpha)/alpha on that axis. Angles are in radians, alpha>0, and these formulae assume the stated uniform solid, surface, lamina or wire model. The OCR booklet supplies the shape formulas apart from the rod midpoint obtained by symmetry and the semicircle obtained by setting alpha=pi/2 in its sector formula. Notes must supply any further shape constant actually required.

FM65 Equilibrium and stability. In a uniform gravitational field a body's weight can be represented at its centre of mass. For coplanar equilibrium use zero resultant force and zero resultant moment about a chosen point. Resolve support/friction forces and check limiting friction if relevant. A body may topple when the vertical line through its centre of mass reaches the edge of its support; sliding depends on friction and normal reaction. The current H245 6.04e text both mentions hinged bodies and excludes them in the same stage-two cell. This ambiguity is retained in the research record; a test requiring a specific hinge-assessment convention must supply the hinge model explicitly in its notes.

FM66 Circular kinematics. For a point on a circle of fixed radius r>0, signed tangential velocity is r theta', speed v=r|theta'|, inward radial acceleration v^2/r=r(theta')^2, and signed tangential acceleration r theta''. For a horizontal circle at constant speed, resolve forces and equate the inward resultant to mv^2/r. 'Centripetal force' names this resultant rather than an extra force added to tension, gravity or contact force. These circular components do not grant a general spherical-coordinate acceleration formula.

FM67 Vertical circles and loss of constraint. For variable-speed circular motion, use energy to obtain speed and radial Newton's law to obtain tension or normal reaction, with radial gravity sign dependent on position. A string can sustain nonnegative tension but cannot push; a contact reaction cannot pull. A calculated zero force is the boundary: check whether subsequent motion can retain the constraint or whether maintaining it would require a negative force. Zero at an isolated point does not necessarily imply departure; a limiting complete vertical circle can have zero tension at its top. When the constraint is lost, use the specified free-fall/projectile model and check actual separation. A result requiring negative tension indicates breakdown of the assumed taut-string path.

FM68 Variable-force motion. In one dimension use m dv/dt=F and, where a velocity-as-position description is valid, a=v dv/dx from the chain rule. Solve the resulting separable or linear first-order equation with appropriate data; a force depending on velocity need not produce constant acceleration. Recover position and interpret stopping, limiting speed and the physical domain. Variable mass and arbitrary nonlinear/PDE solution methods are not granted. The Pure Core exclusion of a=v dv/dx does not remove it from this selected Mechanics option.

FM69 What is not a prior university formalism. This selected course does not by itself grant partial differentiation, a gradient/divergence/curl calculus, multiple/line/surface integrals of fields, general abstract vector spaces, eigenvalue diagonalisation, Hilbert-space inner products, quantum measurement postulates, Lagrangians or stationary action, a canonical partition function, Laplace/Fourier transforms, stochastic processes or general PDE solving. Earlier supplied operations can support an explicit deduction, but their names cannot silently import these additional theories.

FM70 Resources and calculation conditions. All formulas actually used from this packet are supplied in its text. An assessment may require a numerical normal/chi-squared/rank critical value normally obtained from a calculator or statistical table; the corresponding notes must supply needed values if outside tools are unavailable. A missing lookup datum is different from an untaught probability concept. Use consistent symbols and define quantities afresh when a university course uses a different convention.

Physical interpretations in those packets may support deductions using the mathematics here. Knowledge of a mathematical exponential weight does not select a physical distribution; knowledge of complex matrices does not define quantum states; knowledge of reaction Gibbs energy does not supply statistical free-energy identities.
