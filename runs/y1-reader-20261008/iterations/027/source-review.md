## Complete coverage and mathematical witnesses

- PDF 2 / printed 1: Product-rule derivation of integration by parts, complete Example 1, and start of definite version. Rearranging (uv)'=u'v+uv' and integrating correctly gives integral uv' dx=uv-integral u'v dx. For u=arctan x,v=x, the primitive x arctan x-(1/2)ln(1+x^2) has derivative arctan x+x/(1+x^2)-x/(1+x^2)=arctan x on all R. Absolute values around 1+x^2 are harmless. The definite identity being derived is integral_a^b uv'=[uv]_a^b-integral_a^b u'v.

For I_n=integral(ln x)^n dx, integration by parts yields I_n=x(ln x)^n-n I_(n-1), with I_0=x up to constants. The crossed-out x factors on the page cancel correctly. For J_n=integral x^n e^x dx, choose u=x^n,v=e^x, giving u'=n x^(n-1). The v'=dx notation in the logarithm example is a typo listed below.
- PDF 4 / printed 3: Example 4 recurrence, both 'impossible' integrals, definite-integral interpretation, numerical claim and parameter family. J_n=x^n e^x-n J_(n-1), J_0=e^x, is correct. For nonnegative integer n, both recurrences terminate: I_n=x sum_(k=0)^n(-1)^k n!/(n-k)! (ln x)^(n-k)+C and J_n=e^x sum_(k=0)^n(-1)^k n!/(n-k)! x^(n-k)+C; differentiating telescopes to the original integrands. Integral_1^2 e^x/x dx exists as an ordinary positive finite integral because the integrand is continuous on [1,2]. In contrast the displayed F(a)=integral_1^infinity e^x/x^a dx diverges for every fixed real a, as proved below.

## Confirmed source errors

1. PDF 3, Example 3: v'=dx is a mismatch between a derivative and a differential. It should be v'=1 or dv=dx; v=x and the subsequent integration-by-parts computation are correct.
2. PDF 4, the parameter-dependent improper integral F(a)=integral_1^infinity e^x/x^a dx is presented as a family whose finite answer can be studied as a changes. As printed it is +infinity for every real a. For a<=0, the integrand is at least e^x on x>=1. For a>0 choose an integer N>a; the exponential series gives e^x>=x^N/N!, hence e^x/x^a>=x^(N-a)/N!, whose integral diverges. Thus no finite-valued dependence exists here. A negative exponent on e might have produced a different useful family, but that correction is not asserted or inferred as authorial intent.
3. PDF 6, Figure 4 caption says 'Example 1' although the circle is Example 5. The drawn circle and its equation are correct.

## Conditions, overstatements and proof limits

- The definite integration-by-parts identity needs hypotheses supporting the product rule and FTC, for example u,v in C^1[a,b]. Indefinite identities are understood up to additive constants on a common interval. For improper integrals the boundary terms and remaining integrals require justified limits; subtracting divergent quantities is not permitted by the finite-interval identity.
- Repeated lowering of n terminates for nonnegative integer n, not for arbitrary real or negative n. The logarithm-power recurrence is used on x>0; for noninteger powers further real-domain restrictions on ln x can arise. Its n=0 base case should be handled directly rather than evaluating a formal zero times a potentially singular (ln x)^(-1) at x=1.
- The word 'impossible' for integral(arctan x)^2 dx and integral e^x/x dx cannot mean that no antiderivative or no numerical value exists. The first integrand is continuous on R, and the second on each component excluding zero, so the FTC constructs primitives there. In elementary-integration context the intended claim is absence of an elementary closed form; the lecture supplies no proof of that stronger classification. This audit verifies the operational limitation rather than claiming a differential-algebra proof: integration by parts for the first gives x(arctan x)^2+2t ln(cos t)-2 integral ln(cos t)dt, t=arctan x in (-pi/2,pi/2), leaving a logarithmic trigonometric integral outside the methods demonstrated in this packet. For e^x/x on x>0, the exact convergent expression ln x+sum_(k>=1)x^k/(k k!) differentiates to e^x/x. Thus exact non-elementary representations are available; literal impossibility is an overstatement.
- The assertion that one can 'always' evaluate an integral numerically needs existence, a specified finite target/accuracy and suitable computational treatment. The finite integral on [1,2] qualifies; the displayed divergent F(a) does not have a finite value for a numerical routine to recover. Finite quadrature samples alone would not prove convergence of an improper tail.
- Arc length is nonnegative. In an orientation-independent notation ds=sqrt(1+f'^2)|dx| and ds=sqrt((dx/dt)^2+(dy/dt)^2)|dt|. The source uses increasing x/t integration, which permits positive dx/dt-parameter increments in the relevant formulas. Reversing limits in an ordinary signed integral would not produce a length without correcting orientation.
- A sufficient regularity setting for the graph formula is a C^1 function on the interval, or a piecewise-smooth graph with the lengths summed. The finite-polygon limit justifying the differential formula is not proved in the notes. Vertical tangencies require another parametrization or a convergent improper limit, as at the unit-circle endpoint a=1. For a<0, the displayed integral from 0 to a is negative and is not the positive arc length; the figure restricts the intended range to [0,1].
- In Example 6, a must be positive to be the labelled radius and to write speed a; a=0 is a constant point, and a<0 has radius |a| and shifted traced quadrant. Increasing t still has counterclockwise orientation for a nonzero signed scaling, but the displayed first-quadrant picture presumes a>0. Length over a parameter range counts repeated traversals; the given quarter-circle interval has no repetitions.
- Radian measure is essential for the angle-length and trigonometric derivative statements. The same letter a changes roles between the unit-circle arc endpoint and the later radius; those domains must not be carried across examples accidentally.
