D140-C01 | faraday_law_moving_boundary_conditions | lecture15.pdf, PDF 1,2,3,4, slides 2,4,5,6,8 | Integral/differential induction laws
The Gaussian differential law curl E=−(1/c)∂B/∂t is correct. For changing loop area/orientation, the flux rule must include moving-boundary effects, not merely differentiate B at a fixed surface.
For a material loop C(t) with local wire velocity u, ε=∮[E+u×B/c]·dl=−(1/c)d/dt ∫S(t) B·da. Use consistent right-hand contour/surface orientations. For a stationary loop this reduces to ∮E·dl and a partial time derivative at fixed S.
A fixed steady B has curl E=0, yet a moving rod has nonzero motional EMF from u×B/c. Thus applying the displayed partial derivative only to B would incorrectly predict zero for slide 5. The area/angle/field list is illustrative for a uniform field and planar loop; nonuniform translation/deformation can also change flux.

D140-C02 | eddy_current_demonstration_scope | lecture15.pdf, PDF 2, slides 3 | Slotted/solid copper pendulum comparisons
Slots interrupt large eddy-current loops and can substantially reduce damping. The source no-effect/slow/abrupt wording reports qualitative demonstration behavior without field map, dimensions or timings.
Do not treat no effect as an exact theorem that any slot eliminates all currents. Local loops may remain, and changing the field-overlap region changes available paths. No quantitative damping rate or demonstration outcome was independently observed.
For an Ohmic moving conductor J=σ(E+u×B/c); charge redistribution enforces boundary conditions. Joule power ∫J²/σ dV≥0 is supplied by mechanical work in the passive quasistatic setup, giving damping. Cuts constrain current paths but do not make the local induction drive identically zero everywhere.

D140-S01 | sliding_rod_direction_and_energy_verified | lecture15.pdf, PDF 3, slides 5 | Rod circuit
The rod moves right while the right-hand resistor remains fixed, so enclosed area shrinks. The displayed counterclockwise current and lower-rod positive charge are correct for B out of the page.
For rod speed v, length L, uniform B and negligible rail/rod resistance, |ε|=vBL/c and I=vBL/(cR) in the packet’s Gaussian circuit convention.
u×B points downward, producing positive lower rod end. Counterclockwise current supplies outward induced field to oppose decreasing outward flux. Magnetic force on downward rod current is leftward with magnitude ILB/c. Mechanical input Fv=I(vBL/c)=I²R agrees with Joule power; external motion supplies the energy.

D140-E01 | magnitude_derivative_and_angle_errors | lecture15.pdf, PDF 3,4,5,6,7,8,9,10, slides 6,8,9,10,11,13,16,17,19 | Absolute-value EMF equations and omitted cosθ
Many slides label the left side |EMF| but leave dI/dt, dB/dt or sinωt signed on the right. Slide 6 additionally writes a positive derivative of BS cosωt and then a positive sine, inconsistent with that derivative. Slide 8 retains cosθ in flux but drops it from its induced-EMF formula.
With fixed oriented loop, ε=−(1/c)dΦ/dt. For rotation, ε=(BSω/c)sinωt and |ε|=(BS|ω|/c)|sinωt|. For fixed area/angle and changing B, ε=−(S cosθ/c)Bdot, magnitude |S cosθ Bdot|/c. Dropping cosθ requires the unannounced perpendicular-field case θ=0 (or π with sign handled).
Differentiate Φ=BS cosωt explicitly to get Φdot=−BSω sinωt. For an ideal fixed solenoid, signed induced back EMF is −L Idot and magnitude L|Idot|. A decreasing current gives negative Idot but cannot give negative |EMF|. The separate Lenz-law explanation shows the intended physical opposition, but does not make the displayed absolute-value identities valid for both increasing and decreasing current. Mutual/transformer signs also require chosen winding terminal orientations.

D140-S02 | solenoid_flux_coefficients_and_unit_convention | lecture15.pdf, PDF 5,7,9, slides 9,10,13,17 | Pickup and self-inductance formulas
The numerical geometric coefficients are correct for a long air/vacuum solenoid of length l, N turns, radius R, and an internal perpendicular pickup loop of radius r≤R, away from end effects.
With n=N/l, B=4πnI/c. Pickup M=4π²nr²/c²=4π²Nr²/(c²l). Self-inductance L=4π²R²N²/(c²l). These are the packet’s coefficients in ε=−L Idot, with CGS units s²/cm, not a convention in which c² is factored outside L.
One-turn flux is BπR²; total linkage is N BπR². Dividing its current coefficient by c gives L. For the pickup use πr² instead. In SI the same vacuum geometries give L=μ0N²πR²/l and M=μ0Nπr²/l. Dimensional check: L_CGS has length/c²=s²/cm and L_SI=V·s/A=henry. Fixed geometry and linear magnetic response are needed for constant L/M; permeability and material response also matter, so not geometry alone in a core-filled coil.

D140-C03 | back_emf_and_interruption_limits | lecture15.pdf, PDF 6,7, slides 11,12,13 | Opposing current change and H17
For an isolated passive positive L, self-induced EMF opposes changes of that circuit’s current. It is not a separate freely specifiable countercurrent in the same one-loop circuit. The iron-core interruption illustration cannot be described fully by a constant-L, zero-parasitic model.
Use terminal voltage v=L Idot under passive convention and internal induced EMF −L Idot. A finite voltage cannot change ideal inductor current discontinuously. Geometry variation adds I Ldot; nonlinear cores need differential flux linkage and possible hysteresis/loss terms.
Integrating v=L Idot over a vanishing interval gives LΔI=∫v dt. Abruptly opening an ideal current-carrying inductor without any path is a singular idealization; real capacitance, switching arc and losses provide dynamics. The source 125 V, 30 A and spark are demonstration claims, not enough data to calculate a unique peak voltage. No experiment was performed.

D140-S03 | inductor_energy_and_field_energy_conditions | lecture15.pdf, PDF 7,8, slides 14,15,16 | Work and magnetic-energy derivation
For constant linear L, the source derivation U=LI²/2 and the long-solenoid result uB=B²/(8π) are correct. The separate bullet B=4πN/(lc) on slide 15 omits I, although its subsequent algebra correctly includes I.
Correct that bullet to B=4πNI/(lc). Integrate field energy over all space for a general finite geometry; the long-solenoid example neglects fringe/external fields. B²/(8π) is the vacuum Gaussian magnetic energy density. A single uniform-field example motivates but does not prove its general local applicability.
Under passive convention P=Iv=LI Idot=d(LI²/2)/dt. Insert L=4π²R²N²/(c²l) and B=4πNI/(cl): U=(πR²l)B²/(8π). Negative P during current decay means energy returned, not negative stored energy. For reversible linear magnetic media, u=B·H/(8π); more generally u=(1/4π)∫H·dB with suitable state assumptions, and hysteresis can prevent a single-valued recoverable-energy function. The iron-core demo does not certify the vacuum formula for that material.

D140-S04 | reciprocity_derivation_and_scope | lecture15.pdf, PDF 9, slides 17,18 | Vector-potential double integral
The reciprocal mutual-inductance conclusion is correct for fixed, disjoint closed wire loops in vacuum under the magnetoquasistatic approximation. Equality of the two fluxes themselves assumes equal source currents, as the source’s concluding sentence notes.
The invariant statement is Φ21/I1=Φ12/I2, with consistent loop orientations. From the packet’s EMF convention M=(1/c)(Φ/I), so M12=M21=(1/c²)∮C1∮C2 dl1·dl2/|r1−r2|.
Use A1(r2)=(I1/c)∮C1 dl1/|r2−r1| and Stokes to compute Φ21=∮C2 A1·dl2. The kernel and dot product are symmetric under 1↔2, proving reciprocity. Signed M can change when one reference orientation reverses; equal sign conventions are essential. For reciprocal linear media the theorem can extend, but this vacuum proof does not establish it for arbitrary nonlinear/nonreciprocal media. Reviewer energy check: U=(L1I1²+2MI1I2+L2I2²)/2≥0 implies M²≤L1L2 for passive coupled coils.

D140-C04 | transformer_ratio_power_and_loading_conditions | lecture15.pdf, PDF 10, slides 19,20 | Recovered transformer page and demonstrations
The turns-ratio result is correct when every turn links the same changing core flux. Voltage and current transformation and power conservation are ideal-transformer approximations; no secondary load or loss data are specified.
Use |V2/V1|=N2/N1 for common-flux, negligible winding/leakage drops. With negligible magnetizing current and losses, load-current magnitudes satisfy |I2/I1|=N1/N2. Terminal phase/polarity depends on winding references. For AC average real power use Vrms Irms cosφ, rather than multiplying unrelated peak values.
From εk=−Nk Φdot/c, voltage ratios follow. Ideal transferred power then gives reciprocal current ratio for the same operating point; it does not mean adding turns always reduces current at a fixed resistive secondary load, where I2=V2/Rload may increase. Source numbers predict 208/220≈0.94545 V for one turn and 208(10000/220)≈9454.55 V for 10000 turns, using the same amplitude convention as the input. Nail heating/spark length/current cannot be computed without load, coupling and loss data. A real transformer input supplies output plus losses and changing stored energy.

D140-C05 | dc_ac_generator_and_transmission_scope | lecture15.pdf, PDF 4, slides 7 | Carrier-flow description and AC advantage
The DC/AC description concerns macroscopic drift/current, not identical microscopic electron velocities. The illustrated generator needs mechanical rotation as well as a magnetic excitation field.
Constant DC is a special steady unidirectional case; an AC waveform need not be sinusoidal. The historical AC transmission advantage here is transformer-based voltage conversion, not a theorem that every efficient transmission system must use AC.
For a rotating loop mechanical work sustains electrical output against electromagnetic torque. At fixed delivered real power and comparable load power factor, increasing transmission voltage lowers current and I²R line loss. This supports the source’s transformer motivation without asserting present-day technology rankings or supplying unverified efficiency figures.
