Science textbook readability — research carried into this run

Research was requested before this run. These are bounded findings, not validation of SASIS or a claim that an AI reader predicts human outcomes. The current dedicated-reader procedure is mandated by Jonathan's instruction; the studies inform what relationships to inspect.

1. Missing relations between facts
Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994), Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding, Cognitive Science18,439–477.
https://education.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz656/files/lcl/chideleeuwchiulavancher_3.pdf
Full paper read in prior research turn. Small study of24eighth graders, self-explanation versus rereading, with unequal study time; textbook analysis identifies omitted functional/causal connections. Editorial inference: inspect which links the text leaves for the reader and whether their construction is actually supported. This does not require spelling out every possible connection.

2. Coherence depends on starting knowledge
McNamara, E.Kintsch, Songer & W.Kintsch (1996), Are good texts always better?, Cognition and Instruction14,1–43.
https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/are-good-texts-always-better-interactions-of-text-coherence-backg/
Official author abstract read; full text was unavailable. Benefits depended on prior knowledge and the type of understanding measured. Editorial inference: distinguish a missing necessary bridge from productive use of already available knowledge. Do not prescribe maximal explicitness for everyone.

3. Representations need explicit correspondence
Klein, Viiri & Kuhn (2019), Visual cues improve students' understanding of divergence and curl, Physical Review Physics Education Research15,010126.
https://journals.aps.org/prper/pdf/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.15.010126
Full paper read. Small short intervention with32physics majors supports specific equation/vector-field cues, not indiscriminate highlighting. Editorial inference: inspect the relationship between mathematical symbols and the features a figure encodes.

4. A correct calculation may conceal missing meaning
Kuo, Hull, Gupta & Elby (2013), How students blend conceptual and formal mathematical reasoning in solving physics problems, Science Education97,32–57.
https://arxiv.org/pdf/1102.3453
Full author manuscript read. Qualitative interviews with13students, with two cases discussed in depth; not a prevalence estimate or causal experiment. Editorial inference: inspect the interpretation that licenses each use of mathematics, not only the final number.

5. Language can suggest an unintended physical model
Brookes & Etkina (2015), The importance of language in students' reasoning about heat in thermodynamic processes.
https://www.researchwithrutgers.org/en/publications/the-importance-of-language-in-students-reasoning-about-heat-in-th/
Official author abstract read. Exploratory interviews with10students; association does not establish that a phrase causes a misconception. Editorial inference: inspect referents, metaphors and competing readings that would change the physical conclusion.

6. Misconceptions sometimes need explicit reconciliation
van Loon, Dunlosky, van Gog, van Merrienboer & de Bruin (2015), Refutations in science texts lead to hypercorrection of misconceptions held with high confidence.
https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/ws/files/73013517/merrienboer_2015_refutations_in_science_texts_lead.pdf
Full manuscript read. Randomised eighth-grade study with114participants and a one-week check; benefit depended on response format. Editorial inference: when evidence identifies a plausible conflicting interpretation, show why the correct relationship follows and the competing one does not. Do not assume a learner holds a misconception from one simulated reading.

7. Interesting additions can obstruct the explanation
Kienitz, Krebs & Eitel (2023), Seductive details hamper learning even when they do not disrupt.
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-023-09632-w
Full methods/results read. Short lightning-text experiments; recall effects do not establish universal transfer harm or a confirmed cognitive-load mediator. Editorial inference: retain detail for a particular explanatory function, and inspect whether it interrupts necessary relations.

8. Appearance is weak evidence of understanding
Jaeger & Wiley (2014), Do illustrations help or harm metacomprehension accuracy?, Learning and Instruction34,58–73.
https://www.projectreadi.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Jaeger-Wiley-2014.pdf
Full paper read. Two undergraduate experiments (102and145participants); decorative illustrations impaired relative monitoring accuracy without a significant overall image-condition comprehension effect. Editorial inference: a polished page or a reader's confidence is not a substitute for an inspectable connection.

Application to this run

Keep scientific truth, logical availability, and accessibility at the declared baseline distinct. Inspect meaning, actual premises, consequential inference, conclusion, alternative reading and downstream dependency. Preserve correct compact reasoning. No study above validates SASIS, proves human learning from the generated notes, or supports grading the simulated reader.
